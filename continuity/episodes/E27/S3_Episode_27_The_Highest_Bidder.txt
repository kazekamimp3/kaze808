Season 3, Episode 27 — The Highest Bidder

“A tailor,” Samira, the one-eyed gunfighter, called from the wagon. “You need a tailor.”

Kaze looked down at the strip of cloth hanging from his knee.

“Still pants.”

“For now.”

Briar stepped over a rut to join him on the road, white hair brushing the red pillory around her neck. Behind them, Orren's horses plodded past the field where Kaze had landed. The carrier kept both hands on his reins and his opinion of the travelers firmly behind his teeth.

Kaze had healed from his fight with Kratos. His clothes had enjoyed no such benefit. Dust stuck to dried blood across his bare chest, and the black living structure along his right arm shifted as he picked a loose thread from his trousers.

He pulled. The tear widened.

Samira made a sympathetic noise that lacked all sympathy.

“Fixed it,” he said, dropping the thread.

“You removed the part holding it together.”

“Wasn't doing a good job.”

They walked beside the wagon until the road climbed out of the fields. From the ridge, Lask appeared around a squat watchtower and a market square spread wider than its walls. Red awnings striped the streets. Beyond the eastern houses stood sheds, timber racks and a long enclosed yard with a lifting crane above it.

Hammering reached them before the smell of hot iron.

Briar stopped to look.

“That all sells things?”

“Most of it,” Orren said.

“What's the rest?”

“People who bought things regretting it.”

I liked the man. He had the proper respect for commerce.

At the western gate, Samira climbed down using her sound hand. Her other wrist remained braced against her chest. Orren pointed to a narrow carrier's office beside the gatehouse.

“Your letter goes from there.”

She followed him inside. Kaze and Briar waited in the doorway while Orren took the sealed letter from the leather packet beneath his coat and handed it to a gray-haired woman sorting dispatches. He gave her the names Lani and Miel, paid her from his own purse, and watched her put the letter into an outgoing satchel.

“South road,” the woman said. “Leaving after the market bell.”

Samira tapped the satchel with one finger.

“They make weapons. Don't let that make you curious.”

“People who make weapons send very dull letters.”

“I don't.”

Orren had provided the ride and forwarded the message. Its journey to Samira's weaponsmiths was still ahead of it. Her beautiful guns and blade were still somewhere with the men who had robbed her, and one ordinary pistol on her belt was a poor replacement.

She came out looking toward the hammering.

“I need to see what this place calls a weapon.”

“I need to see what it calls lunch,” Briar said.

Orren climbed back onto his wagon.

“Neither of those is included.”

He drove toward the ironmongers without waiting to hear what else might be.

Kaze spent one of his six silver coins at a stall with hot bread and skewers of meat. Briar took her portion, bit into it, and followed a boy's shouted advertisement across the square.

“Siege sale! Officers, household buyers, licensed companies! East yard!”

The boy waved a wooden sword at the passing crowd.

“No children inside!”

Briar looked back at Kaze. “He's outside.”

“Smart business.”

“Do you think he knows?”

Kaze watched the boy work himself into a sweat selling other people the privilege of entering.

“Don't ruin it for him.”

They followed the crowd toward the yard. Samira finished her bread on the way and inspected two sword stalls without stopping. At the second, she lifted a blade with her right hand, sighted along its edge, and put it down with an expression that injured the seller more than a bargain would have.

The siege yard had stone walls, a narrow front entrance and a timber gallery along its left side. Wide service doors faced the entrance across the yard. A crane stood beside those doors above rows of equipment. There were ordinary crossbows, shields stacked like scales, and heavy carts whose wheels reached Kaze's waist.

Under the gallery, a painted board showed a fortress gate split by a marvelous bolt.

“Is that what it does?” Briar asked the attendant.

“That's what we're here to demonstrate.”

“Who painted it?”

“What?”

“Someone thinks it does that already.”

The attendant looked past her for a customer with simpler questions.

Kaze had stopped beside a covered wagon at the far wall.

Something under its canvas had caught his attention before he saw it. An unpleasant, intimate tug behind the ribs. He knew that feeling. The forbidden thing behind a locked door. The drop worth looking over.

His danger sense gave him a reason to pay attention, not an explanation.

He stepped closer.

A man in a long blue coat slapped the wagon's wheel with his gloves.

“That remains covered until the sale.”

“Then start.”

“You are?”

Kaze glanced down at the name tattooed across his stomach.

The man followed his gaze.

“Very convenient,” he said.

“Had it put there for you.”

“Master Veyr. This is my yard.”

Veyr had a narrow beard, fine boots and six guards wearing his blue sash. He looked at Kaze's torn trousers, then at the two women behind him.

“Inspection is for buyers.”

“I'm looking at buying.”

“At what price?”

Kaze considered his remaining coins.

“Five.”

“Hundred?”

“Look at you. Getting excited.”

Veyr's smile folded away.

Samira laughed. Briar was still chewing, watching both men with interest.

“You may enjoy the ordinary lots,” Veyr said. “From behind the rope.”

Kaze looked at the rope. It stretched between two posts at knee height.

“That's what separates us?”

Veyr walked away.

For a moment, Kaze looked genuinely disappointed. The man hadn't even stayed to defend his fucking rope.

Then the bell sounded, and servants pulled the canvas from the wagon.

The weapon beneath it looked older than everything around it.

A ballista: a great bow mounted lengthwise on a heavy frame. Its dark, curved limbs rose around a red central body, the shape too much like bone to be comfortable. New timber brackets held it to a wheeled carriage. Iron bands secured it for transport. Those bands belonged to the men around it. The thing they held did not share their workmanship.

Kaze's amusement sharpened into attention.

Veyr climbed onto a low platform.

“Recovered from eastern agricultural workings. Examined, secured, and offered with carriage and fittings.”

“By whom?” a woman in a red military coat asked.

“Recovered?”

“Examined.”

“Qualified people.”

“Name one.”

There was a small, pleased sound from Samira.

The woman stood with four soldiers near the open gates. Her coat had a captain's rank pins. Veyr addressed her as Captain Dresk, then began explaining how many laborers had been required to move the weapon.

“You were supposed to hold this sale at the coast,” she said.

“And I would have, if the harbor gate hadn't been brought down.”

Briar looked at Kaze. He was studying the ballista with sudden, exaggerated concentration.

Veyr pointed toward a worker beside the carriage.

“My driver came back with a broken wagon and half his load. Tell her.”

The driver rubbed his jaw.

“Couldn't get through the harbor road. Stone everywhere. I tried the side lane. Axle went in the ditch.”

“Days rearranging transport,” Veyr said. “Days feeding crews who weren't moving anything. This yard was available. So here we are.”

Dresk looked unimpressed by the hardships of a man trying to sell them to her.

“Your inconvenience isn't a feature of the weapon.”

“It is a feature of the price.”

Samira glanced between Briar and Kaze.

“Something you want to tell me?”

“Busy listening,” Kaze said.

Briar swallowed another bite of her meat.

“That was a big gate.”

He turned toward her slowly.

She looked back, quite innocent of any intention to help his performance.

“What? It was.”

Samira's grin arrived by degrees. Across the yard, Veyr had resumed his sales pitch, unaware that one of his less promising customers had already cost him a considerable sum.

Dresk raised her voice over him. “My company is buying something that opens a fortified gate. The ridge hold has refused surrender twice. I don't need a difficult ornament.”

“Then the demonstration will satisfy you.”

“It had better.”

There was your Noxian sales pitch, brothers. A particular army, a particular wall, and somebody hoping ancient sorcery came with a dependable receipt.

Veyr waved toward the weapon.

“A relic of the Darkin.”

Briar stopped chewing.

Kaze looked at her, then back at the ballista.

Darkin were ancient warriors bound into weapons. Some found flesh to wear again. That did not make every prison the same, or every hand that touched one its next body. Kaze's own nature had a Darkin inheritance, but the thing on that carriage wasn't family he automatically knew.

“Does it talk?” he asked.

Veyr ignored him.

“Does it?” Captain Dresk asked.

The merchant's jaw tightened.

“There have been reports.”

“From your qualified people?”

Kaze laughed hard enough that Briar nearly lost her mouthful.

“I like her questions.”

Veyr signaled his guards. Two moved closer to Kaze without drawing weapons.

At the far end of the yard, workers raised a square shield of thick timber in front of the stone wall. A crewman went to the ballista's rear. Another began loosening its transport bands.

Kaze stepped over the rope.

The nearer guard caught his upper arm.

Kaze looked at the hand.

“Before you finish that,” he said, “what happens next?”

“You step back.”

“And if I don't?”

“You leave.”

“You got a lot of plans for me.”

The guard tightened his grip. His partner drew a short sword halfway from its scabbard.

Kaze turned the first man by the wrist and put him through the nearest shield cart's wheel.

Wood burst around the guard's head and shoulders. The cart dropped onto its broken axle, pitching its shields across the stones. The man stopped moving beneath them. Red flecks joined the old black blood across Kaze's hand.

People shouted. Briar stepped away from the falling shields before they reached her feet.

The second guard finished drawing.

Kaze punched him beneath the chin. The man's boots left the ground; his body struck the underside of the gallery and broke through its floor. Above them, a chair and a pair of startled legs disappeared into the new hole.

Samira moved before the debris fell. She hauled a nearby woman clear with her sound arm, then ducked beneath the gallery's edge.

“Well,” she said, drawing her pistol, “the rope worked beautifully.”

Captain Dresk pulled one of her soldiers behind a cart.

“Leave him the yard! Back to the gate!”

She had seen enough to know the merchant's guards were badly outmatched. Veyr had not reached the same profitable conclusion.

“Kill him!” he shouted from his platform.

A guard beside the crane raised a heavy crossbow.

Kaze's attention snapped toward the threat. He could see the man's finger closing. Behind that man, one of the ballista crew was trying to drag its carriage toward the rear doors.

Kaze went for the carriage.

He turned his right forearm across the bolt's path as he crossed the yard. The point struck the black structure along the arm and skipped away. The shaft splintered against the wall. He hadn't needed to stop moving.

The crossbowman fumbled for another bolt.

Kaze kicked the support out from beneath the crane.

Its timber leg broke at the base. The upper arm swung down across the yard, dragging its hanging chains sideways. One chain caught the crossbowman across the body and threw him into the wall. He fell with his head at an angle it would never hold again.

The falling beam struck the merchant's platform.

Veyr jumped clear. His cash chest did not. Coins burst across the stones under a shower of broken boards, and the remaining guards abruptly found more than one thing worth protecting.

Briar had been watching the spearman nearest her.

He lunged while her head was turned toward the falling crane. She heard his boot scrape and twisted inside the thrust. The point sliced her upper arm instead of entering her chest.

She caught the shaft against her pillory and stepped toward him.

He tried to wrench the spear free.

“Too close,” she said.

Her forehead struck his nose. He reeled. Briar caught his arm, pulled him down to her, and bit into the side of his neck.

His shout stopped halfway out.

She braced a foot against his knee and took him down, keeping him between herself and the weapon rack. When she lifted her face, blood ran from her mouth. The man's hands twitched against the stones, then lay still.

Her pillory remained closed. Blood magic had made Briar, and fresh blood fed her recovery. The cut on her upper arm drew together as she fed, leaving blood down her skin.

The last two blue-sashed guards ran through the front gates.

Samira watched them go, pistol still loaded in her hand.

“Good choice.”

She lowered it.

Kaze caught the ballista carriage before its crew could reach the rear doors. Both men let go and fled around him. He planted a foot against the axle and stopped its motion so abruptly that the front wheels hopped off the ground.

The transport bands creaked.

“Where we going?” he asked.

Neither crewman stayed to answer.

Captain Dresk stood outside the gate with all four of her soldiers. Buyers crowded the street behind her. Within the yard, dust drifted through the broken gallery and a man above it was calling for somebody to help him down.

Veyr crouched beside the remains of his platform.

Kaze looked around.

“Sale's got quiet.”

“You killed my men,” Veyr said.

“Yeah.”

The answer left the merchant nowhere pleasant to go. He looked toward Dresk.

She didn't come back inside.

Kaze crouched beside the ballista and put his right hand against its red central body.

He went still.

Briar saw the change and approached, wiping her mouth on the back of her hand. Samira followed, keeping her pistol pointed at the stones.

“What?” Briar asked.

Kaze raised one finger.

The voice was low. Close enough that, at first, I took it for somebody speaking beside his ear. It came from the weapon under his palm.

“Those fittings are new.”

Kaze looked at the iron bands.

“They bothering you?”

“They make it harder to move me.”

“You move yourself?”

“Not like this.”

He leaned closer. There was no borrowed smile on his face now. The curiosity was plain.

“What's your name?”

“Naganeka of Zuretta.”

Briar glanced at Samira. Samira had heard the name too. Her smile disappeared as she studied the old weapon.

“I'm Kaze.”

“Now I know.”

“You heard the five?”

“Yes.”

“Good offer.”

“For the carriage.”

Briar laughed once, then looked surprised at herself.

Naganeka had a voice as dry and controlled as an officer correcting a badly drawn map. Whatever she wanted from these strangers, panic wasn't how she intended to obtain it.

“Take me beyond these walls,” she said.

Kaze glanced toward the broken platform.

“He thought he owned you.”

“He owns timber.”

“You hear that?” Kaze called to Veyr. “You been selling wood.”

The merchant stood. He had blood on one sleeve and several coins gripped in his hand.

“That thing has said whatever kept people near it since we brought it here.”

Naganeka said nothing.

Kaze looked back at her.

“That true?”

“Would you have preferred I spent the time thanking him?”

He smiled.

“No. I would've hated that.”

He took his hand away and straightened, still studying the weapon. Naganeka had given him a name and a request. He seemed to find both more interesting than Veyr's claim of ownership.

Captain Dresk called through the gate.

“Leave it. You've made your point.”

Kaze looked over his shoulder.

“What point?”

“Whatever this is.”

“I wanted to look at it. Now I'm looking.”

“You can't drag a Darkin relic through the town.”

He glanced at the wheels.

“Why not?”

She seemed to consider answering the question literally. I respected the effort it took not to.

“My men will not let you take that weapon.”

Kaze stepped away from the carriage.

Every soldier near the gate shifted. One lowered his spear toward the opening. Another lifted a shield high enough to hide his face.

Dresk watched Kaze notice them.

He looked delighted.

Then she turned to her men.

“Clear the street.”

Kaze stopped.

“That's not what you just said.”

“I know.”

She backed them away. The spear rose. Buyers stumbled aside, grateful for an order that pointed somewhere beyond the man in the yard.

Kaze watched the soldiers go with a small, offended shake of his head.

“People keep doing that.”

“Changing their minds?” Briar asked.

“After saying shit.”

“You should try it.”

He looked at her.

She was smiling through the blood on her lips, pleased with that one.

Samira put her unused pistol away.

“The carriage is wider than the gate,” she said.

Kaze looked at the gate. Then at the carriage.

“Yeah.”

“I know that expression.”

“Use the service doors!” Veyr shouted. “There's room behind you!”

Kaze looked at the merchant, then deliberately walked to the right gatepost and drove his shoulder into it.

The stone pier broke away from the wall. Iron hinges screamed. The gate fell outward, striking the street hard enough to throw dust over the nearest stalls.

Outside, a stallholder shouted what the falling iron had just done to his display. Kaze stepped over the broken pots without looking down.

Kaze returned to the carriage and lifted its front by the timber drawbar. The rear wheels rolled as he pulled it through the enlarged opening. They left deep tracks in the churned stones and ran over the fallen gate with a grinding clatter.

Veyr followed him as far as the threshold.

“You haven't paid.”

Kaze stopped. He looked back at the merchant, genuinely trying to find the thought that had carried him that far.

“You still want the five?”

Veyr stared.

“I can leave you the carriage,” Kaze offered. “She said that's what it's worth.”

“Go,” Samira said, laughing now. “Just go.”

He kept the five silver in his pocket and pulled the weapon down the street.

The crowd parted. Briar walked beside him, carrying the last piece of bread. Samira took the other side, watching the rooftops while she enjoyed herself.

They passed Orren's wagon outside an ironmonger's shed. He was unloading a bundle of metal fittings when he saw them.

He looked at the ballista, the blood, the broken gate behind them, and finally at Samira.

“Your letter has gone in the satchel.”

“I watched.”

“Good. Then we're finished.”

He lifted his bundle and went inside.

Kaze watched him disappear.

“Nobody's excited for me.”

“I am,” Briar said. “I want to hear her talk again.”

“See? Somebody appreciates a good purchase.”

“You didn't purchase anything.”

“Good decision, then.”

Outside the eastern houses, the road widened beside the timber racks. Kaze pulled the carriage onto a patch of hard ground and lowered its front. The wheels stopped. Behind them, people were coming out of the market streets to look, though none came particularly close.

He had left three men certainly dead in Veyr's yard. The guard thrown through the gallery hadn't come back down. There were injured people among the fallen boards, a merchant's business wrecked, and a captain who had chosen to keep her soldiers alive long enough to decide what to do next.

I would have liked to call the silence after it victory. Mostly, I could hear somebody in the distance asking where the town watch had got to.

Briar finished her bread.

“You wanted that before he touched you,” she said.

Kaze looked up from the drawbar.

“Yeah.”

“I thought so.”

“He could've let me look.”

“Would you have left it?”

He considered the ballista.

“Probably not.”

Briar nodded, satisfied to have found the actual answer. She stepped close enough to address the weapon without touching it.

“I'm Briar. You asked to come outside. We're outside.”

For a moment, nothing answered.

Then that low voice came again.

“There is still a town behind us.”

“There was always going to be.”

Kaze barked a laugh.

Samira rested her sound hand on her belt.

“Where do you want to go?” she asked.

“Away from men who believe a soldier can be bought with her prison.”

“That covers a lot of Noxus.”

“Then keep moving.”

Kaze sat on the timber tongue of the carriage. It bent beneath him but held.

“You keep giving directions.”

“And you keep finding them useful.”

“I brought you out.”

“You brought the weapon you wanted out. We benefited from the same act.”

His smile widened slowly.

“All right. I like that answer.”

Briar reached toward one of the newer iron bands, then stopped short of touching it.

“What happens if those come off?”

“They are his restraints,” Naganeka said. “Not the ones that hold me.”

Briar looked down at the red pillory enclosing her own wrists.

“Oh.”

There was no bright question after it. She looked at the old weapon for a while, taking the distinction seriously.

“Mine helps,” Briar said eventually.

Samira's gaze moved to the heavy red restraint.

“That does?”

“I can think. I like thinking. There were people who liked it better when I just killed what they pointed at.”

Still seated on the drawbar, Kaze leaned back against the carriage and listened.

“You missed a few,” he said.

“I killed too many.”

“Ah.”

Briar looked at the blood on her forearm. The wound underneath had closed. She wiped at the stain with her thumb, making it broader.

“Then they were angry about that too.”

For once, Kaze let her finish without improving the story.

“I want to keep this,” she told Naganeka, raising her wrists slightly within the pillory. “That doesn't mean I want someone holding the other end.”

The weapon was silent for several breaths.

“Then do not give them an end to hold.”

“That's harder than it sounds.”

“Yes.”

Briar seemed to appreciate the answer more than she would have appreciated advice.

Samira shifted her braced wrist. “And you? What do you intend to do if somebody finds a way to let you out?”

“Make my next choice standing.”

Kaze smiled at that.

“You keep talking like I already agreed.”

“You tore down a wall to continue this conversation.”

“Could've used the other door.”

“I heard the man.”

His laugh came suddenly. He looked at the ballista as if she'd finally made a proper bid of her own.

“All right. You can come along. Doesn't mean I'm volunteering anybody for you.”

“Nor have I volunteered to fight your battles.”

“Good. I'd get bored watching.”

Kaze got up. With both hands on the loose end of a transport band, he tore it away from the carriage. The iron shrieked as its bolts pulled through new timber. He dropped it on the ground, then removed the other band. The ballista remained seated in its brackets.

The freed straps lay twisted at his feet. The older bindings remained beyond anything he had changed. Naganeka had acquired movement and company; she still had no body of her own.

Samira glanced back toward Lask. “We should move before the captain finds an idea she likes.”

“You coming?” Kaze asked.

“Until this becomes boring.”

Briar looked at the weapon, then at the broken skyline above the market street.

“It might take a while.”

Kaze lifted the drawbar again.

The carriage rolled forward. Briar stayed beside him as they took the road past the last timber rack, and Samira walked on the other side with her braced wrist held clear of the turning wheel.

Behind them, Captain Dresk appeared at the edge of town. She stopped beside the torn iron bands. One of her soldiers knelt to inspect them; she kept watching the three figures and the weapon moving away.

Kaze looked back once.

“Now that,” he said, “was a market.”

From the carriage came Naganeka's quiet reply.

“You have not learned the price yet.”

He laughed and kept pulling.
