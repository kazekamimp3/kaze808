Season 3 — Episode 34: “A Bad Influence”

Master Shake wanted a piece of the new man.

Not emotionally. That would have required an organ he had spent his entire life outsourcing. He wanted a cutting.

“Just a little branch,” he told Kay Kay. “We put it in another pot, grow another you, then we've got somebody to do the stuff you don't feel like doing.”

The twenty-foot wooden figure leaned over him. His walking stick rested beside the broken garden tub. Chris was crouched at the other end, rescuing the ordinary seeds from the spilled soil.

“Would it be me?” Kay Kay asked.

“Close enough.”

“Then I still wouldn't feel like doing it.”

Shake opened his mouth. Frylock looked up from the cultivation lamp and waited with the exhausted hope of a man watching stupidity approach its own locked door.

“That's why we don't tell the other one,” Shake said.

There it is. He found a window.

Kay Kay lowered a hand. Long fingers opened around Shake's straw without touching it.

“May I have a piece of you?”

“Absolutely not.”

“Does it hurt?”

“It would diminish the original.”

Kay Kay considered that, then withdrew his hand.

“Keep your original. I shall keep mine.”

Chris laughed into the soil tub.

Shake pointed at him. “This is why nobody starts a business here.”

“You were going to charge me to enter my kitchen yesterday.”

“And you've been using it all morning. You're welcome.”

Daynuh moved the lamp before Kay Kay's elbow could brush its hot housing. Mahnuh watched from beside his legs, the giant pit bull's nose following every unfamiliar creak of living wood.

“You don't have to let everybody touch you,” Daynuh told the newcomer.

“I've noticed they ask after reaching.”

“Some don't ask.”

Charles Manson, who had been reaching toward Kay Kay's back with an empty bowl, slowly brought both hands into view.

“What?” he said. “I thought he might make syrup.”

“I might make a fist,” Kay Kay replied, pleasantly interested in the possibility.

Meatwad rolled closer. “Can you make pancakes?”

Chris stood. “Nobody's eating the man until we establish that isn't what we're doing.”

Frylock turned toward him.

Chris held up his muddy hands. “You know what I meant.”

I did. Unfortunately, in this household, clarification was sometimes the only thing between hospitality and a tasting menu.

Kay Kay's attention had moved to the second monitor. On it, morning sunlight lay across the shoulder of a road. Beyond Billy's hat-front view, a gull rose against an open sky.

“Where is that?”

“Outside,” Chris said.

Kay Kay looked toward the flesh ceiling, then back at the little patch of sky.

“I'd like the larger light.”

Chris used the banjo channel. Outside on the dry ground above the harbor road, Billy's instrument abruptly spoke in the chef's voice.

“Billy. Remember the vegetables?”

“I remember swallowing the supplies.”

“Well, one of them's got opinions. He wants out.”

Kaze turned toward the banjo. Arthur lowered his cigar. Daniel, hanging by his knees from a low branch, took another drink and waited for the explanation to become worse.

Chris supplied it.

Billy listened without playing a note.

“Does he have a name?”

“Kay Kay.”

“Then bring the gentleman over.”

Inside, the newcomer had to pass a doorway much shorter than himself. He put his walking stick through first. His narrow torso flattened, shoulders turning into an arrangement Chris's eyes refused to finish understanding. Knees folded beside his ribs; long fingers gripped the far frame. Bark flexed over shifting wooden fibers as he drew the rest of himself through.

He unfolded on the other side and retrieved the stick.

Chris stared at the doorway.

“I couldn't get a whole couch through that.”

“Did you ask it to bend?”

“No.”

“Perhaps you hurt its feelings.”

He was smiling. Chris noticed. The tree knew exactly what he had done.

Billy arranged his exit. Kay Kay passed through the living route Billy opened and emerged from his enormous mouth, one long arm reaching into daylight before the rest of his folded body followed. He put his feet on the ground, straightened, and kept straightening until even Billy had to look up.

Daniel swung down from his branch.

“Chris,” he said to the banjo, “that's not a vegetable. That's the man who comes to repossess the vegetables.”

Kaze walked around the newcomer once, grinning despite himself. His bare shoulders still carried dried traces of yesterday's black blood. Black Marrow remained part of him, unmanifested.

“You grew this?”

“Apparently,” Chris answered.

“On purpose?”

“No, nigga. I specifically requested something that couldn't look down on my life choices.”

Kay Kay turned his face into the sun.

For a while, he said nothing.

The moving air found every tiny space between his fingers. His feet pressed into the roadside soil. Around one heel, pale root tips entered the damp ground and stopped there, testing it. He looked toward the sea, where a distant ridge of the World Serpent's body interrupted the horizon.

“That one has found a great deal of growing room.”

Billy's smile disappeared.

“He has been making that everybody's problem.”

Kaze watched Kay Kay rather than the sea.

“You all right?”

“Yes.” The answer came without a joke. “This is much larger than I expected.”

Arthur tipped his hat back. “Gets worse the farther you go.”

“I would like to go farther.”

“We're heading inland,” Kaze said. “Soon as we stop finding reasons to stand beside this fucking harbor.”

Behind him, Billy accepted Daynuh's request to leave and brought him and Mahnuh out in turn. Chris stayed inside with his household and the live monitor.

Kay Kay eased his little roots free of the soil. Dirt clung to them as they folded back against his feet.

“May I walk with you?”

“Yeah,” Kaze said. “Just don't let Chris charge you for being planted.”

“I paid for those seeds,” the banjo complained.

“I paid for those seeds.”

“Then take it up with the fucking turnips.”

They started down toward the upper market, where the inland road passed between freight offices and garden stalls. Kay Kay shortened his stride to stay with them. He had already learned that moving at somebody else's pace could be a choice rather than a restraint.

Across the district, Clifford Marston was discovering that walking away from a medical bill did not make it stop existing.

Sava stood in her clinic doorway, watching him negotiate the shallow step with his new leg supports. He shifted his weight, let the powered joint answer, and brought the second boot down beside the first. His neck remained bent above the open black collar. He had left the ridiculous helmet off.

“Rest when you need it,” she said.

“I heard you.”

“I'm looking for evidence you listened.”

Tracy Morgan pushed the wheelchair down the ramp, with the helmet and cape resting on its otherwise empty seat. Brian rode on his shoulder, overseeing an operation in which he had contributed nothing but the word oversight.

“The chair presents an unfortunate image,” Brian announced.

“The chair presents somewhere to sit,” Tracy said. “You should try it sometime without borrowing my fucking skeleton.”

Dutch came out carrying the stolen harbor ledger and the new bill. He glanced from one to the other, then tucked the bill away.

“There is a man named Pell two streets over. Freight broker. Three of his shipments were diverted before the harbor closed.”

Clifford looked at the ledger. “And he pays to know where they went.”

“He pays to know before his competitors.”

It was a workable idea. Try not to sound disappointed. Dutch could find an opportunity without first inventing Tahiti inside it.

The Coon had a different proposal.

“We announce that the harbor disaster has attracted a supervillain. Then we defeat the supervillain.”

He looked at Clifford's black apparatus.

Clifford looked back.

“No.”

“You didn't hear the whole thing.”

“I heard the part where you looked at me.”

Jimmy adjusted his grip on a crutch. “Could we do a show where nobody has to get d-defeated?”

Cartman gave him a look of profound artistic disgust.

“That is called a meeting, Jimmy.”

Timmy wheeled toward the route back to headquarters. Jimmy went with him, preferring to find a place for an actual act rather than wait for Cartman to manufacture a catastrophe. Cartman hurried after them, insisting that venue selection fell under his command.

Dutch watched the boys go before turning to the adults.

“No costumes. No speeches about world domination. We sell the information and leave.”

Brian nodded solemnly.

Tracy looked at him. “He was talking to you.”

“I understood the subtext.”

“Then stop getting ready to violate it.”

By the time they reached Pell's office, Kaze had stopped in front of a garden stall nearby.

Not because he wanted a garden. He had noticed Kay Kay watching the plants.

Young trees stood in tubs beneath a canvas awning. A woman was binding damp cloth around exposed roots while her assistant loaded the finished plants onto a handcart. Their destination was uphill, away from the damaged lower streets.

Kay Kay crouched until his face was level with a small pear tree.

“Is this one sleeping?”

“Growing,” the woman said, giving his face a careful second look.

“It is being very quiet about it.”

“They generally are.”

He touched the soil, not the tree. The woman watched his long hand, then looked at Kaze.

“Is he yours?”

“No.”

Kay Kay turned toward him. Kaze shrugged.

“I didn't grow you. And even if I had, what the fuck would I do with that?”

“Charge storage,” Chris suggested through the banjo.

“Don't help.”

The woman recovered herself. “One silver for the little pear. Tub included.”

Arthur gave Billy's banjo a meaningful glance.

Chris declined to participate.

Kay Kay looked at the coin in a customer's hand, then at the little tree.

“What is the silver doing?”

“Buying it,” Kaze said.

“I can lift it without silver.”

“Yeah. She'd call that stealing.”

“And then?”

“Depends what she can do about it.”

Daynuh shifted the handcart clear of Mahnuh's path.

“Or you pay her,” he said. “She did the work.”

Kaze smiled at him. “Also an option.”

Kay Kay studied them both. “You disagree without leaving.”

“Most days,” Arthur said.

A raised voice carried from the freight office across the lane.

Kaze recognized Dutch's cadence through the open window and turned with the immediate interest of a man discovering that his neighbor's argument had become free entertainment.

“Oh, this'll be good.”

He crossed toward the low courtyard wall. Kay Kay remained beside the garden stall, one hand resting on his walking stick. Billy turned with Kaze, and the interior monitor followed.

Inside BELLY, Chris pulled his new chair closer to the screen.

Frylock looked over. “What are you watching?”

“Dutch doing business. Everybody shut up. It's either going to be beautiful or fucking expensive.”

Pell's office had a street window, a desk, and two hired men pretending not to be hired men. One stood beside the rear door. The other kept finding reasons to inspect a rack of packing tools near the courtyard entrance.

Pell himself wore an ink-stained waistcoat. He compared an entry in the stolen ledger with a folded shipping notice from his desk.

Then he read it again.

“Sixty silver,” Dutch said. “For the book.”

“Twenty.”

“You've already found more than twenty's worth.”

Pell closed the shipping notice.

Dutch waited. He didn't fill the silence. That was the useful thing about confidence: occasionally, he let somebody else do the sweating.

Pell counted sixty silver onto the desk. Tracy collected the coins into a leather purse Pell supplied. Dutch put the ledger down.

A trade. Stolen goods, certainly, but no smoke, no mirrors, no requirement for the moon to be in a particular financial quarter.

They could have left.

Brian leaned forward.

“And for an additional consideration, our organization can secure the remaining records.”

Tracy stopped moving.

Pell looked at Brian. “You still have access?”

Dutch said, “We are not discussing another job.”

“Not without terms,” Brian added.

There. A man had watched somebody close a door successfully and decided it needed his head in it.

Pell's attention moved from the ledger to the purse in Tracy's hand.

“So I pay, and tomorrow another of you comes in with another book.”

“One book,” Dutch said. “One transaction. Finished.”

Pell nodded toward the man by the courtyard entrance.

“That money stays until I know who else has access.”

The man lifted a short packing hatchet from the rack.

Dutch's hand settled beside his holster.

“You purchased a book.”

“And I am not buying the same secret twice.”

Clifford saw the second man's arm move at the rear door. A pistol came clear of his coat.

Clifford didn't need to run. He planted his supported feet, drew his revolver and fired once.

The man's wrist burst red. His pistol struck the floor, followed by his knees and a noise that made the clerk outside abandon his stool.

Dutch had his own gun out before Pell could stand.

“Sit down.”

Tracy drove his shoulder into the courtyard man before the hatchet came down, shoving him against the tool rack. Brian yelped as their shared body hit the doorway. Tracy kept the money in his fist and backed into the courtyard, refusing to stay trapped inside for somebody else's larger opportunity.

The hatchet man came after him.

Kaze stood at the open gate, smiling without welcome.

The man changed direction.

He ducked through the side passage toward the garden stall, choosing what looked like an unguarded exit instead of the tattooed stranger watching the doorway. Kay Kay stepped away from the plants to make space for him, one long arm moving above the narrow passage.

The man saw the wooden limb before he saw the face. He hacked at what he took for an obstruction, trying to force his way through.

The blade bit Kay Kay's forearm. Bark split; pale sap welled along the cut.

Kay Kay recoiled, surprised by pain.

The man wrenched the hatchet free and drove it toward the narrow trunk above the hips.

Wood opened around the incoming blade. Not mist, not an illusion: living fibers parted, leaving a real gap where the edge passed. Then the gap closed around the hatchet head.

The handle stopped dead.

Kay Kay looked at the man's hands.

“That's enough.”

The man let go and punched the split forearm.

Kay Kay's fingers closed around his face.

The long arm lifted. Boots scraped a furrow through the roadside dirt, then left it. The man's fist struck Kay Kay's wrist, then scrabbled over the tightening bark.

For a second, his weight hung from the wooden hand.

Kay Kay squeezed.

The jaw cracked sideways. Blood ran between long fingers; the back of the skull broke against the closing grip with a thick, final crunch. The man's arms dropped. Kay Kay lowered what remained of him to the ground.

The garden seller stood perfectly still.

Kay Kay looked at the blood on his fingers, then at the sap on his arm.

“Oh,” he said.

Nobody rushed to explain that sound for him.

At the freight-office window, Pell tried to climb past his curtains. Arthur stepped into view outside, one hand resting on an UNSHAKEN grip.

Pell slowly put his foot back on the floor.

Dutch took the ledger off the desk.

“You've lost the book,” he said. “You can keep the lesson.”

Clifford backed toward the courtyard, his revolver steady while the wounded man clutched his wrist. He moved carefully over the threshold, bringing the powered legs with him instead of trusting them to find it for him.

Then he saw Arthur.

For an instant, the paid job, the stolen book and the blood behind him ceased to matter.

Arthur noticed the shift in his attention.

“You're on your feet.”

Clifford's muzzle remained low, but his fingers tightened.

“Don't sound proud of yourself.”

“I ain't.”

Tracy moved between their lines of sight without entering either muzzle's path.

“We got the money. We are leaving with the money. Nobody is spending my morning on another fucking revenge discount.”

Clifford looked at Arthur again, then chose the road out of the courtyard. Every step was something he had wanted back. He wasn't prepared to throw it away for the satisfaction of lifting his gun first.

Kaze watched him pass.

“I didn't know he could walk.”

“Neither did I,” Arthur said.

From the banjo came Chris's voice.

“Is that a fucking cape bracket?”

Billy turned away before the question could become the reason Clifford came back.

Dutch stopped beside Kay Kay. The tree's injured arm hung slightly apart from his body; the captured hatchet remained lodged in his side.

“Seems we owe you our thanks.”

Kay Kay looked down at him.

“Did you send that man?”

“No.”

“Then you owe me nothing for stopping him.”

“One silver, Busta,” Dutch said.

“Tracy,” came the furious reply. “There is a dead man right there, and somehow you're still the part of this morning that's fucking testing me.”

He gave Dutch the coin anyway. The small kindness was his choice, not Brian's.

“For the inconvenience.”

Kay Kay accepted the coin in his clean hand.

He looked toward the pear tree.

“I understand this part.”

Dutch's group departed with fifty-nine of the broker's silver and the original ledger. They had made money, kept their leverage, and acquired a man with a very good reason to hate them.

That counted as an unusually tidy morning for Tampon Pandemic Productions.

My standards are disgusting. I know. My instinct is to repair a relationship with an apology that still has blood on it. Marvel Jesus contains multitudes, and several of them should not be allowed near a witness stand.

But the tree was still standing beside his first dead man.

Let's stay with him.

Kaze came over. Daynuh kept the frightened garden assistant from stumbling into the body while the seller pulled her cart farther back. Daniel watched the office door, bottle lowered, spear in his other hand.

Kay Kay turned his injured forearm. The cut remained open. He pulled at the trapped hatchet, found it caught, then deliberately loosened the wood around it and let it fall.

“You could've put him down,” Kaze said.

“I did.”

“Before that.”

Kay Kay looked at the body again.

“Yes.”

No confusion this time. No convenient little story about the tree not knowing his own strength.

“He hurt me,” Kay Kay said. “Then he tried again. I wanted him to stop. After I had him, I didn't want to let go.”

Kaze nodded once.

“Yeah. I know that one.”

“Does that make it right?” Daynuh asked.

“No,” Kaze said. “Makes it something I know.”

Kay Kay studied the distance between them, the way neither stepped away.

Then he looked directly at Kaze.

“You watched.”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“You'd caught him. I wanted to see what you decided.”

The little growths along Kay Kay's shoulder drew tight against the bark.

“You could ask me things without waiting for them to hurt.”

Kaze's smile vanished.

For a moment, he had nothing clever to put in its place.

“Fair.”

Daynuh handed Kay Kay a cloth the garden seller had agreed to spare. The tree wrapped it around his forearm and tucked the loose end beneath the cloth. Nobody declared him harmless. Nobody told him the man had secretly deserved everything that happened.

Daniel looked at the hatchet lying in the dirt.

“First day outside. Already had a disagreement with a tool.”

Kay Kay glanced at him.

“Which one?”

Daniel let out a surprised laugh.

“All right. That's on me.”

The garden seller accepted the silver only after Kay Kay set it on her counter and stepped back. She gave him the little pear tree in its tub, avoiding the hand that had killed a man. He didn't reach for her.

“What does it need?” he asked.

She answered because he waited, because it was an ordinary question she knew how to answer, and because the little tree still needed the same things it had needed before the violence.

Water. Light. Room for its roots. Not too much of the first simply because he was worried.

Kay Kay listened carefully.

Behind them, somebody was running toward the harbor guard post. Pell's surviving employee had gone out through the rear. There would be accounts of this street, and not all of them would agree.

Arthur noticed the runner. Kaze did too.

“We staying for that?” Arthur asked.

Kaze looked at the shattered head, the office, the new tree holding a much smaller tree.

“Nah. I wanted to leave before we got here.”

They took the inland road. Billy offered Kay Kay a return to the sheltered interior, but he chose to keep walking in the sun. His long stride became careful again. Daynuh stayed near enough to speak without shouting, not close enough to steer him.

Inside BELLY, Chris moved the rescued vegetable seeds into the portion of the tub that still held soil. Frylock lowered the cultivation lamp over the remaining bed and checked the cell connection. Meatwad watched the little pear tree moving through the exterior picture.

“He got a friend,” Meatwad said.

“He bought a plant,” Shake replied.

“That's how he got here.”

Shake looked toward the screen, then at the bowl Charles was still holding.

“I am withdrawing my proposal.”

“Good,” Chris said. “Try withdrawing from my pantry next.”

Back at the rented headquarters, Timmy held the door while Clifford brought his chair over the threshold. He had chosen to sit for the longer return, with his helmet in his lap. It was a rest, not a surrender, and nobody who had seen his shot at Pell's office mistook it for one.

Jimmy moved aside to let him through. Cartman inspected the purse Tracy put on the table.

“So the first operation under The Coon's leadership was a success.”

“You weren't there,” Clifford said.

“Exactly. Delegation.”

Brian reached for the purse. Tracy moved it out of reach.

“Rent. Medical bill. Food. Before either of you buys a fucking throne.”

“A headquarters needs a command chair,” Cartman said.

Timmy tapped his own chair and grinned.

Jimmy laughed before he could finish the joke he'd been forming. Cartman did not appreciate the collaboration.

Dutch laid the blood-spotted ledger beside the purse. He had an unpaid landlord, a hostile broker, new members who considered agreement a temporary inconvenience, and enough fresh money to make decisions again.

He began by counting it himself.

Outside, ordinary harbor workers passed the door. They had no idea TPP had just improved its position. Pell would not need a worldwide audience to make trouble for them. One reachable guard would do.

On the road, Kay Kay asked Kaze where they were going.

“Inland.”

“That is a direction.”

“It's what I've got.”

“Will there be more people?”

“Probably.”

Kay Kay looked at his bandaged arm. “I'd like to meet one before it goes badly.”

Kaze walked a few paces before answering.

“You met us.”

“That is what made me think to ask.”

Arthur laughed. Kaze looked up at the tree, then began laughing too, not because the answer was kind but because it had actually caught him.

Kay Kay carried his small pear tree carefully. He had learned how to take a life and how to buy something on the same morning. The second lesson had required more explanation.

Nobody asked him to swear to anything. When the path narrowed, Arthur moved ahead and Daynuh dropped back, leaving room for Kay Kay and the little tree he carried.

I know. Found family. Always gets me.

Although most families ease you in with dinner before letting you discover which member thinks a fatality is a useful icebreaker.

These bastards started with the icebreaker.
