Season 3, Episode 23: The Wrong Story

Kaze looked down the valley, shifted Briar higher in his arms, and chose the next place to land.

“That one,” she said.

She meant a red roof beside the river.

“That's somebody's house.”

“Is that important?”

“Depends who's home.”

He chose the grassy bank above it instead.

His legs compressed. Stone cracked beneath his shoes, and the shelf fell away behind them. Briar's delighted shout carried across the valley as they sailed over a stand of poplars. Kaze kept one arm beneath her knees and the other behind her back, clear of the heavy pillory enclosing her neck and wrists. He turned his body before landing, took the slope with bent knees, and slid through a yard of damp grass.

A woman hanging washing watched two strangers arrive from the sky.

Kaze straightened.

“Morning.”

“Nearly noon,” she said.

“Appreciate you.”

He carried Briar down the bank and set her on the road. Behind them, the woman took her sheets indoors.

I couldn't fault her. When a shirtless man lands beside your laundry wearing somebody else's blood, you bring in the whites.

The blood belonged partly to Baldur, the cursed bastard Kaze had left digging himself out of a ruined quarry. Kaze's own split lip and broken nose had healed. His trousers had managed no such recovery; one knee remained torn open, and a brown-red smear stretched across his chest beneath the dust.

Briar examined him as they walked toward the crossing.

“You still have some in your ear.”

“You offering?”

“To clean it?”

“Was hoping you'd get creative.”

She leaned close enough to inspect the dried blood, then grinned.

“No. I want to see how long it bothers you.”

They'd known each other since the previous afternoon. A night together had made them comfortable touching; it hadn't made either of them predictable. I considered that a promising arrangement, assuming the surrounding buildings could survive it.

At a public pump, Kaze worked the handle while Briar caught water in her restrained hands. She washed the dust from her face, spat out a mouthful, and declared the river tasted like coins.

“That's the pipe,” he said.

“The pipe tastes like coins.”

He bent under the stream and scrubbed his face and arms. Briar worked the handle for him, awkwardly at first, then with enough enthusiasm to rattle the bolts.

“Easy. You trying to wash me or launch me?”

“Both could work.”

The water took the loose blood away. It left his trousers stained, his locs damp, and a stubborn red crescent behind one ear.

Briar wiped that off with her thumb.

“Bothered me first,” she admitted.

He caught her chin lightly and kissed her before she could retreat.

Across the road, a man shouted that he had killed Samira.

Kaze stopped smiling against her mouth.

“What?” Briar asked.

The man shouted it again.

“With this very pistol!”

Kaze turned toward the voice.

“Oh, we gotta hear this.”

The settlement straddled a stone bridge, its shops crowded along the approaches. Flat riverboats waited below the arches while mill carts crawled through the street above. Beyond a cooper's yard, an open-fronted tavern had pushed its tables into a patch of shade. A dozen people stood around a man on a low platform.

He wore a cavalry coat with the insignia cut away. One pistol sat in his belt; the other was raised beside his head. A narrow knife hung at his hip. He had a clean beard, a battered nose, and the easy projection of a man accustomed to making strangers listen.

“Berren,” he announced, tapping his chest. “Remember it when you're hiring. You can buy six frightened boys, or you can buy the man who put down the Desert Rose.”

That was Samira's name in the mouths of people who wanted to sound as though they'd met her: the Desert Rose, a Shuriman-born mercenary who'd found plenty to enjoy in Noxus, most of it dangerous.

Arthur had killed her during the Sins' robbery of a private sale down on the coast. He'd broken her wrist and shot her through the chest. I remembered the blood beneath her body. Kaze remembered it too.

Berren apparently remembered being there instead.

“She came over a table,” he said. “Blade out. Pistols blazing. I caught her wrist.”

Kaze made a small, delighted noise.

“Caught her fucking wrist.”

Briar glanced at him. “Did he?”

“Keep listening.”

“She begged,” Berren said.

From a table near the back came a woman's voice.

“Oh, this part should be good.”

She sat with her boots crossed beneath the bench. Dark hair fell over one shoulder. A plain scarf partly covered the lower half of her face, and an eyepatch covered one eye. Her left wrist rested in a splint against the table. There were no pistols at her waist, no great blade against her chair.

But when she lowered the scarf, I knew her.

So did Kaze.

Samira was alive.

For a moment, he stood utterly still. Then his gaze dropped to her braced wrist and returned to her face.

“Well, shit.”

She looked him over. The tattoos. The locs. The unmistakable grin slowly coming back.

Recognition tightened her mouth.

“You.”

“Me.”

“Where's your friend?”

“Which one?”

“The one who owes me a wrist.”

Kaze pulled out the opposite bench with his foot.

“Arthur. Not here.”

He sat. Briar settled beside him, turning carefully so her pillory didn't strike the table.

Samira's good hand remained in view. She had nothing in it. Her attention kept moving between Kaze and the man on the platform.

“You were dead,” Kaze said quietly.

“I remember the shot.”

“I was there after.”

“Then we agree it was a bad afternoon.”

There was no smile with that answer.

I had no explanation for the living woman at that table. Neither, from the look he gave her, did Kaze. Her death had happened. So had the robbery that caused it. Seeing her breathe didn't make either thing kinder.

“How'd you get here?” he asked.

“On a cart. Before you ask the next question, I don't know.” She touched the cloth over her chest, then stopped herself. “I woke up. I still had this.”

She lifted the splinted wrist a fraction.

“Everything useful was gone.”

Kaze leaned back.

“Yeah. Arthur took your guns.”

Her eye fixed on him.

“And my sword?”

“That too.”

“Does he have names for them yet?”

“Don't know.”

“Tell him not to get attached.”

On the platform, Berren described how Samira had dropped to both knees and offered him a fortune.

She closed her eye briefly.

“I leave the world for five minutes, and the quality goes to shit.”

Briar turned fully toward her.

“I'm Briar.”

“Samira.”

“I heard. Several times.”

“He's very proud of me.”

“Are you going to tell him?”

Samira watched Berren kiss the barrel of his pistol.

“Not yet. I'm learning so much about myself.”

Samira's gaze moved to Briar's pillory.

“Does he have the key to that?”

Briar bared her teeth in a smile that wasn't friendly.

“No.”

“Good.”

“I know how to open it.”

Samira glanced at the crowded yard, then back at her.

“And you're keeping it on?”

“I like being able to listen. People say strange things when they think they're good at talking.”

On the platform, Berren was describing the terrified look in Samira's eyes. Both of them, apparently.

Samira raised her cup toward him.

“They certainly do.”

Briar leaned across the table, interested now.

“You don't like the story, but you're laughing.”

“I'm deciding which part annoys me most.”

“I don't like the begging.”

“Neither do I.”

“The wrist part happened?”

Samira looked down at the brace.

“Yes.”

Briar's smile disappeared. She looked at Kaze, then back at Samira.

“That part isn't funny.”

“It will be when he tries to show you.”

Samira shifted her cup farther from the injured hand. It was empty, but she kept it on the table between herself and Kaze.

“Are you with them?” she asked Briar.

“I'm with him.”

“For work?”

“No.” Briar glanced at Kaze, and her smile returned. “He was much more relaxed afterward.”

Kaze turned to her.

“You telling my business now?”

“Should I put you on the platform?”

Samira laughed into her empty cup.

“I'd pay to hear that story.”

“Oh, I charge for that,” Kaze said.

“Then you'll have to impress me for free.”

Kaze laughed, and she looked back at him.

“You enjoying this?”

“Which part?”

“Careful.”

“The part where you're sitting here looking dangerous with no weapons. That's working for me.”

“You should see me with mine.”

“I did. Good entrance.”

“Poor ending.”

“I'd watch another.”

She held his gaze long enough for the invitation inside that answer to become plain. Then she nodded toward the platform.

“Watch this one.”

She stood, leaving the scarf on the table.

Now, a sensible man might have let a fraudulent story pass while nursing a broken wrist. Samira had never struck me as a woman interested in earning that particular compliment.

Berren was taking questions from a broad-shouldered merchant who wanted an escort for three wagons.

“Proof?” the merchant asked.

“Go down to Hadran's place at the port. Ask what happened to her.”

“You were at Hadran's?” Samira said.

Berren looked toward her. “I just told you.”

“Which door did you enter?”

“Front.”

“And the horse?”

He hesitated.

“What horse?”

At their table, Kaze folded over his own grin.

The Sins had stolen a bronze horse from that house as well. Briar knew that much of the story already. She looked at Kaze, saw his face, and began laughing before she knew the rest.

Berren heard them.

“You got something to add?”

“Nah,” Kaze said. “Your shit. Finish.”

Samira stepped closer to the platform.

“I heard there were two pistols.”

“She had two. I needed one.”

“Show us.”

He looked at her splint.

“You want a demonstration?”

“I want to know what the merchant is buying.”

That pleased the merchant. He crossed his arms and settled back to watch.

Berren lowered his raised pistol. His glance lingered on the eyepatch. Something uneasy moved across his face, but Samira tipped her head and smiled as though she had all afternoon to be impressed.

“I heard the story in the port two days ago,” he said. “People are already getting it wrong.”

“Then you're fortunate to be here correcting them.”

The merchant interrupted.

“Can you actually shoot, Berren?”

Berren turned sharply. “What?”

“I have wagons to move. I don't need a recitation at every bridge.”

He picked up a wooden cup and put it on a fence post beside the empty yard.

“Hit that.”

Berren brought his pistol up, aiming one-handed. The barrel settled. I knew enough men who lived by a weapon to recognize the difference between that stillness and the swagger he'd been selling us.

Samira watched it too.

“A cup?” she said. “I thought we were talking about someone who shoots back.”

He lowered the gun without firing.

“You offering?”

She offered her right hand, palm up.

“Lend me the other pistol. I'll be Samira.”

Someone near the bar chuckled.

Berren could have stopped there. He could have put both weapons away and announced that a professional didn't perform tricks for drunks. But the merchant was watching, and Samira had made the request sound so easy.

He pulled the second pistol from his belt and held it by the barrel.

“It's loaded.”

“Then don't point it at your stomach.”

She took it from him.

I felt a powerful urge to reach through the afternoon and slap that man.

Samira turned the weapon in her right hand, examining the lock and hammer. A cheap single-shot pistol, well enough maintained. She kept its muzzle toward the dirt as she checked the priming pan with her thumb.

“You killed her with something like this?”

“That exact make.”

“Terrible last thing to see.”

His audience laughed. Berren didn't.

Kaze's grin eased. He watched Berren's free hand close against his coat, then open again.

Briar leaned forward.

“He's angry now.”

“Yeah.”

“You going to help her?”

“She told me to watch.”

He shifted his foot under the end of their bench, ready to move it. The easy posture remained. The attention behind it had changed.

Samira climbed onto the platform. A narrow space separated her from Berren, with the tavern wall behind them and a long table below the front edge.

She pointed toward the empty cooper's yard beyond one side.

“Make room there.”

Two spectators stepped away. The merchant moved with them, frowning now.

“Why?” Berren asked.

“For the demonstration.”

She turned sideways, keeping her braced arm close against her ribs.

“You caught my wrist.”

Silence spread out from the platform.

Berren stared at her.

“Say that again.”

“You caught my wrist. That's the story, yes?”

He looked at her eye. Her hair. The scarred confidence of that smile.

“You're not her.”

“I was hoping you'd make that difficult to prove.”

His pistol began to rise.

Samira watched his shoulder, not the muzzle. She stepped off the platform to his right as he brought the weapon around, landed on the long table, and kicked its water pitcher into his face.

The shot cracked across the yard.

It went through the space she'd just left and punched a pale scar into the cooper's wall. Berren stumbled backward, blind with spilled water. The pitcher struck the planks beside him and rolled away unbroken.

Samira dropped from the table to the ground. Her boots hit together; her bad wrist stayed tucked against her chest. She came up laughing.

“You missed that part.”

The spectators scattered. Kaze pulled their bench backward out of the scramble with one foot, taking Briar with it. She let out a surprised laugh, then stood to get a better view.

Berren threw the spent pistol at Samira.

She ducked. It struck a chair behind her and split a rung. He drew the knife and jumped down from the platform, trying to close before she could level the weapon he'd lent her.

He was quick. I won't rob him of that. He drove her around the end of the table with two short cuts, crowding her injured side, and she had to give ground.

Then he reached too far.

Samira planted a boot on the bench, rose over his next low slash, and kicked him across the jaw with her other foot. His head snapped sideways. She landed behind the bench and shoved it into his knees.

He folded over the seat.

Her pistol was already aimed when he looked up.

“She didn't beg,” Samira said.

She fired once.

Berren fell backward beside the platform. The knife slipped from his hand. He tried to draw breath, managed a wet choking sound, and went still.

Samira stood over him until she was certain.

There'd been no grand exchange, no ten-minute contest for the soul of the empire. He'd threatened a wounded woman with a gun, missed, and discovered that the wound hadn't made her slow enough.

She lowered the empty pistol.

From behind the bar, the tavern owner stared at Berren's body. Then he looked at the fallen pitcher.

“That was my good one.”

Samira picked it up with the pistol still hooked in her fingers. She checked the rim and set it on the platform.

“Still is.”

Kaze began laughing.

The owner didn't join him.

“Get out.”

Samira crouched beside Berren, carefully keeping weight off her braced wrist. She took his knife, a small powder flask, and a pouch of lead balls from his belt. His purse held four silver coins and a few coppers. She counted them onto the platform, left the lot beside the pitcher, and stood.

“For the trouble.”

“Does that pay for a corpse?” the owner demanded.

“Ask the man who wanted to hire him.”

The merchant was already walking away.

Kaze retrieved Berren's first pistol from beneath the broken chair. He offered it to Samira grip first.

“You forgot one.”

She looked at it, then at him.

“That habit runs in the family?”

“Taking shit?”

“Other people's guns.”

“Only got one family. Hard to compare.”

She took it.

Outside the yard, a little crowd had collected around the sound of the shots. Nobody applauded when Samira came through. People moved aside, staring at the pistols, the brace, the woman several of them had just heard described as dead.

One of the original listeners pointed at her.

“He said she was—”

“He said quite a lot,” Samira replied.

Kaze and Briar followed her toward the river. Behind them, the tavern owner began demanding that somebody fetch the watch. Whether anybody would do it promptly, I couldn't say. Nobody seemed keen to volunteer for the errand while the three of them were still in sight.

They stopped beneath the bridge approach, on a stone landing above the moored boats. Samira sat on a bollard and looked at her new weapons with undisguised disappointment.

“These are awful.”

“You just killed somebody with one,” Briar said.

“That doesn't make it pleasant.”

Kaze rested his shoulder against the bridge wall.

“You always this particular?”

“You always this pleased with yourself?”

“I been behaving.”

“I saw you sitting on a bench.”

“Looked good doing it.”

She let herself smile at that, briefly, before setting one pistol between her knees to reload it with her good hand.

Kaze nodded at the brace.

“Want a hand?”

“From your family? I've seen the price.”

“Mine come with better manners.”

“I doubt that.”

“You're right. Better face.”

She glanced up, considering him with an insolence that matched his own.

“A crowded field of disappointments.”

Briar laughed. Kaze put a hand over his chest.

“Damn. We just getting acquainted.”

“We got acquainted at Hadran's.” Samira seated the ball with the ramrod. “You were enjoying that too.”

The smile left his face without turning into shame.

“Yeah.”

She finished loading and priming the pistol, lowered its hammer safely, and set it aside. The second took longer. Her jaw tightened when she jostled the splint.

Briar watched her work.

“What did Arthur take besides your weapons?”

“A necklace I was recovering. Six red stones. Gold links.”

“For you?”

“For someone who paid me.”

“They'll want it back too.”

“I expect they will.”

Samira looked at Kaze.

“Where is he?”

“I'm not giving you that.”

“Afraid I'll hurt him?”

“No. You asked where my people are. Answer's no.”

She nodded once, as though she'd expected it but wanted to hear how he'd say it.

“Then tell him I'm coming for my weapons.”

“If I see him.”

“You can add a compliment if it makes the conversation easier.”

“On his shooting?”

Her eye hardened.

Kaze held it. For a moment the river, the ropes knocking against the hulls, and a carter swearing overhead were the only things making noise.

Then Samira picked up the second pistol.

“He should have kept the first shot out of my reach.”

“You should've left the necklace.”

“And spent the afternoon doing what?”

Kaze's grin returned slowly.

“Could've found something.”

She shook her head, almost laughing, and tucked the pistols into her belt.

“You don't get to make that afternoon charming.”

“Was talking about this one.”

“This one's improving. I have two bad guns and one fewer liar.”

She rose from the bollard. A ferryman on the next landing had been pretending not to listen; she called across to him and asked where the road beyond the bridge joined the inland trade route.

He pointed upstream, then toward a gate between the houses.

“Past the old mill. Keep the water on your left until the road climbs.”

“Good.”

She looked down at the cheap weapons once more.

“Lani and Miel would throw these in a furnace.”

Her own weaponsmiths, those two. Artists with unpleasant materials. Arthur had stolen more than a pair of things that went bang.

“You know where to find them?” Kaze asked.

“I know who to ask.”

She gave him nothing more specific. Fair exchange, I thought.

Briar stepped closer, examining the pistol in Samira's belt without touching it.

“Will you tell people the right story now?”

“Which one?”

“How you died.”

Samira was quiet for a moment.

“I think I'll start with how I walked out.”

That answer pleased Briar. She nodded as though she meant to keep it.

Kaze pushed away from the wall.

“Next time you want an audience, find me.”

“Bring a better present.”

“You give all your admirers shopping lists?”

“Only the ones connected to the thieves.”

She walked up the landing steps without waiting for another reply. At the top she paused, looked back at Briar, and tipped her head toward Kaze.

“Don't let him tell the story without you.”

“I won't.”

Then Samira joined the traffic heading across the bridge, one wrist braced, two inferior pistols heavy at her waist, and her stolen weapons still very much on her mind.

Kaze watched until a covered wagon passed between them.

“She's fun.”

Briar looked up at him.

“She might shoot Arthur.”

“Might try.”

“And you still like her.”

“Liked her before she tried the first time.”

Briar considered that while a boatman below them pushed away from the landing. Her mouth twisted, working through the shape of it.

“That's inconvenient.”

“You hungry?”

“That's also inconvenient.”

He laughed and offered his arm. She bumped her shoulder against it instead, then stayed there.

At the top of the steps, a woman was lifting skewers from a little charcoal grill. Briar stopped so suddenly that Kaze took two more steps before realizing he'd lost her.

The woman watched Briar study the meat. Then she looked past her toward the tavern.

“You with that lot?”

Kaze came back.

“Which lot?”

“The shooting.”

“We watched.”

“Watch somewhere else.”

Briar pointed at the grill.

“I want those.”

“Then pay and take them. I have customers.”

Kaze set a silver coin beside her cutting board. The woman slid two large skewers onto a square of rough paper and pushed them toward him.

“Both. Keep moving.”

He lifted one, offered it to Briar, and took the other.

She bit into the meat before they reached the corner. Hot fat ran over her lip; she caught it with her tongue, eyes closing in concentration.

“Better than the pipe?” he asked.

She held her skewer in front of his face.

“Try mine.”

“Same grill.”

“Mine's better.”

He took a bite. She watched his expression and laughed when he nodded.

“You don't have to agree.”

“Then give me mine back.”

She'd taken his skewer while he was biting hers. Now she sampled that one too, unhurriedly, while he watched.

“Briar.”

She offered both. He chose the one she'd declared inferior, and she leaned into his side again as they walked.

From the corner, they could still see people gathering near the tavern yard. Someone had covered Berren's face with his own cavalry coat. Samira's name passed between two men who hadn't been there when she said it.

Aye. By evening, somebody would swear she'd shot him twice. By tomorrow, she'd have killed six men with a broken arm. I could already see the first embellishment forming on one bastard's lips.

Briar tugged Kaze toward the upstream road.

“What would you say about me?”

“To who?”

“Someone who hadn't met me.”

He studied her for a few steps.

“Bad at washing ears. Excellent company. Wants to land on people's houses.”

“You chose the bank.”

“Saving the roof for our anniversary.”

“What's that?”

“Usually when somebody reminds you of a thing you were supposed to remember.”

“Then tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow?”

“You can remember yesterday.”

He looked at her, and the answer came without a joke.

“Yeah. I can do that.”

She smiled, caught the back of his neck with one restrained hand, and pulled him down for a kiss that made a passing carter complain about the road being narrow enough already.

Kaze broke away just far enough to glance at the man.

“Plenty room. Go around.”

The carter did, muttering.

Briar kept her hand at Kaze's neck a moment longer.

“And don't leave out that I asked for the second jump.”

“Wouldn't dare.”

They walked upstream together. This time, with no cliff beneath them and no stranger demanding a fight, neither seemed in any hurry to leave the ground.
