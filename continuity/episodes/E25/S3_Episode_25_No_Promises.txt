Season 3, Episode 25: No Promises

Briar watched Kaze look toward the road for the third time.

“Say it.”

“Say what?”

“Whatever keeps making your head go that way.”

The river had gone black beyond the fishing shelter. Up the slope, carriage lamps moved between the trees. They'd spent the evening together beneath that roof, and Kaze had managed nearly an hour without wanting something troublesome.

I thought he'd done remarkably well.

“I want to go back,” he said.

Briar sat upright.

“To her?”

“Yeah.”

Earlier that afternoon, Elise had entertained them in a fine countryhouse, offered them a place in her unpleasant business, and turned into a giant spider when the visit went badly. Kaze had thrown her through a balcony. That seemed a fairly decisive end to an introduction.

Apparently I'd underestimated his interest in a second impression.

“Her man tried to stab me,” Briar said.

“I remember.”

“You said you wanted her before the knife.”

“Thought I was finished wanting it.” He rubbed his mouth, irritated by his own answer. “I'm not.”

She studied him.

“Don't make up a nicer reason.”

“Was about to.”

“I know.”

Kaze laughed once. She didn't join him.

“I won't be put in another room because she wants to talk to you.”

“Then don't go in one.”

“And don't tell me she's harmless now.”

“Briar. I threw her through a fucking roof. I know who we're visiting.”

She stood, adjusting the heavy restraint around her neck and wrists.

“Good. I have something to tell her too.”

They followed the river path back uphill, passed the orchard wall, and found the footman sweeping glass from the steps. He recognized the man who had ruined his afternoon.

He looked at Kaze, then at the broom in his hand.

“I don't think—”

“She here?”

“In the west sitting room.”

The answer escaped him before his courage could intervene.

Inside, servants were lifting broken glass into wooden tubs. The musicians had gone. Two remaining guests sat with their coats on, waiting beside luggage while a steward spoke quietly to them.

Through a doorway opposite the damaged hall, Elise stood before a mirror, removing a splinter from the edge of her dark headpiece.

Human again. Composed again. Neither detail made the broken roof imaginary.

Her eyes found Kaze in the mirror.

“You came back.”

“So did you.”

“I used the orchard gate. I still have matters to settle here.”

“I can see through one of them.”

She glanced toward the missing glass overhead.

“Do you always revisit your damage?”

“Sometimes I miss a bit.”

Briar stepped past him.

“Nobody takes me aside. Nobody touches this.” She tapped the pillory. “If you want to ask me something, ask where I can hear it.”

Elise put down the splinter.

“You made yourself understood earlier.”

“You didn't act like it.”

The silence was long enough for a servant outside to reconsider bringing in a tray.

Elise faced her fully.

“I should have allowed you to leave.”

“Yes.”

Briar waited. She wasn't going to improve that answer for her.

“You are free to do so now,” Elise said. “Both of you.”

Kaze leaned against the doorway.

“Your man still breathing?”

“When they carried him out.”

That was all she offered. It was all he asked.

Elise looked between them.

“Have you returned to collect an apology?”

“She had something to say. I wanted to hear your answer.”

“And now you've heard it.”

“Now I'm deciding whether we can have a conversation you don't turn into a job.”

“You objected to the work before you knew its benefits.”

“See? Doing it already.”

Elise's mouth moved toward a smile. He stayed where he was.

“I'm not bringing you people. I'm not handing you Briar. Whatever you wanted from her, that shit's finished.”

“I can speak for myself,” Briar said.

He looked at her.

“Yeah. You can.”

She turned back to Elise. “And I'm finished discussing it.”

“Then we shall discuss something else,” Elise said.

I had heard more convincing settlements between men already reaching for their knives. Beyond the doorway, her guests still waited with their luggage. I doubted they'd been promised a pleasant retirement somewhere sensible.

Kaze saw them when he shifted away from the doorway.

“Still going on your trip?”

“Eventually.”

“They see what happened?”

“They saw.”

He looked at the waiting pair again. One clutched a little travelling case against his knees. The other stared determinedly at the floor.

Kaze shook his head, almost amused.

“People really will walk into anything if you dress it nice.”

He made no attempt to stop them. Whatever might wait across the sea, he'd returned for a woman, not her passengers.

Briar noticed a table behind Elise, laid with the remains of supper.

“I'm eating that.”

“You're welcome to it.”

“I wasn't accepting another invitation.”

She carried a plate into the adjoining room herself, chose a chair facing both doors, and dragged it across the expensive floor with a sound that made the steward wince.

Kaze watched her settle.

“You good here?”

“I'm deciding how much your host owes me.”

“Start with the ham.”

“I have.”

Elise waited until he looked back.

“You find that charming.”

“I find her charming. You got some catching up to do.”

“And yet you returned.”

“Because I still want to sleep with you. Now you don't have to guess why I'm standing here.”

She approached him slowly enough to make the choice visible.

“You are unusually direct.”

“You were unusually big.”

Her laugh surprised me. It seemed to surprise him too, though he recovered faster.

“There he is,” she said.

“Who?”

“The man who was enjoying himself before we began discussing employment.”

“You fired him out a window.”

“I believe that was you.”

“Shared afternoon.”

Elise stopped within reach. She hadn't touched him.

“You realize that wanting me is a poor reason to trust me.”

“Didn't say trust.”

“And you expect me to trust you?”

“I haven't hidden what I want.”

“You have hidden plenty else.”

He tilted his head.

“You interested in me, or the parts you haven't figured out?”

“I don't find them so easy to separate.”

For once she hadn't offered him a title, a door, or an introduction to something ancient. Kaze seemed to like that better.

“Then give me an invitation with nothing attached.”

Elise studied him, her expression losing the hostess's practised warmth without becoming cold.

“Come upstairs with me.”

“Because you want me there?”

“Yes.”

He smiled.

“That'll do.”

She took his hand. He went willingly, pausing at the adjoining room to tell Briar where he was going.

Briar looked from him to Elise, then back to her plate.

“I'm leaving when I'm finished.”

“Meet you at the gate.”

“Then don't mistake the ham for a clock.”

I liked her chances of making him hurry without asking him to.

Elise led him up the undamaged western staircase. At the bedroom door, she turned and kissed him. He answered, then drew back far enough to look at the room beyond her.

“No audience this time?”

“Would you like one?”

“I'd like you to stop making every question dangerous.”

“You would be bored within the hour.”

“Try me.”

She drew him inside and closed the door.

They slept together that night.

Afterward, Kaze sat beside the window while Elise repaired the loose fastening of her headpiece at the mirror.

“You're looking rather pleased,” she said.

“You keep looking to check.”

“I'm trying to decide whether this has made you more difficult.”

“You could ask for a second opinion.”

“From you?”

“Experienced witness.”

She laughed and set the ornament down again.

“You do work very hard to appear effortless.”

That caught him. His grin stayed, but it changed.

“And you work hard to make everything look like your idea.”

“Was returning not your idea?”

“That's why you liked it.”

Elise came to the window. Below them, a carriage was being brought around for the waiting guests.

“I liked that you came back knowing more.”

“Don't turn it into a lesson.”

“Must you spoil every graceful sentence?”

“Only the ones trying to charge me.”

She rested her hand briefly against his cheek. He let her, watching her face rather than the carriage.

“If I see you again,” she said, “shall I dispense with the reception?”

“Keep the food. Lose the man by the door.”

“And the voyage?”

“Still haven't said yes.”

“I know.”

She glanced at the tattoo across his forehead.

“Most of the people I entertain are afraid of an ending. You seem determined to provoke one.”

“You offering to study me?”

“I'm wondering what you would ask of Vilemaw.”

“How big is he?”

Elise stared at him. Then she began to smile.

“You're serious.”

“I'm curious.”

“Those are not always the same thing with you.”

“You bring him people who want to live longer. Ever bring him somebody who just wanted to look him in the face?”

“Not someone I expected to enjoy the experience.”

Kaze looked out over the broken roof. His attention had sharpened in a way she plainly recognized.

“See, now you making the trip sound interesting.”

“Then perhaps I should stop talking.”

“First good business decision all night.”

She gave his cheek a light, dismissive pat. He caught her hand, kissed her knuckles with exaggerated ceremony, and released it when she laughed.

“Appalling manners,” she said.

“Still got invited back.”

When he left the room, she made no attempt to hold him there.

Briar was waiting at the front gate with a wrapped portion of ham and an expression that suggested she'd conducted an exacting negotiation with the kitchen.

“Finished?” she asked.

“You?”

She lifted the parcel.

“Not even close.”

He offered his arm. She didn't take it immediately.

“Did you promise her anything about me?”

“No.”

“Good.” She stepped beside him. “Don't make me explain that again.”

“You won't have to.”

On the road, they stepped aside for the carriage they'd seen being readied. Elise's two remaining guests sat inside with their luggage. Kaze watched it pass, then followed Briar down to the river. They returned to the fishing shelter, ate what she had taken, and slept there until morning. Where those guests would go next was still Elise's business, and still somebody else's danger.

By daylight, Kaze was ready to find Samira.

“The woman with the bad guns?” Briar asked.

“You got a better description?”

“The woman who made a man lend her a pistol so she could shoot him.”

“See? That's why I like having you around.”

They walked back to the river crossing, crossed the bridge Samira had taken the day before, and followed the road beyond the old mill. Kaze knew her direction. He didn't know how far she'd gone.

At a cart yard above the mill, he asked a driver about a woman with an eyepatch and a splinted wrist.

“Samira?” the man said.

“Yeah.”

“Took her up to the Copper Cup yesterday. She wanted Orren. He hauls metalwork inland.”

“He there?”

“Due this morning. If his wheels hold.”

He pointed up the climbing road. “Half a day's walk. Less if you know somebody with a cart.”

Kaze looked at the empty seat beside him.

The driver looked at Briar's pillory, then at his cart's narrow bench.

“No.”

“Didn't ask.”

“Saved us both time.”

Briar laughed as they started uphill.

“You could have jumped.”

“Could land past the place.”

“You could jump back.”

“You just want another jump.”

“Yes.”

He stopped, considering the slope ahead. Then he lifted her, set her on the broad top of a roadside wall, and stepped away.

She stared down at him.

“That wasn't one.”

“Complaints department's up there.”

Briar jumped down herself and landed beside him hard enough to shake loose stones from the wall. She caught his hand before he could dodge away laughing.

They walked on together.

They reached the Copper Cup that afternoon, where the river road met a wider inland route. Its sign was an actual copper pot with a handle shaped like a drinking cup, which struck me as a public argument nobody had won.

Wagons crowded the yard. Samira sat beneath an awning, facing a broad man with brass fittings spread on a cloth between them.

Her left wrist was still braced. One ordinary pistol lay on the cloth. The other remained at her waist beside the knife she'd taken from the man who'd lied about killing her.

“A ride and the letter,” she told the carrier.

“The ride's worth more.”

“Then tell me how much you enjoy being shot at with no gun.”

Orren picked up the pistol, keeping it pointed away from them.

“Is it loaded?”

“Yes. I'm selling you convenience.”

He turned it carefully, inspected the lock, and nodded.

“One seat as far as the market town. Your letter goes with the springs to Lani and Miel's workshop.”

“Into their hands.”

“I'll put it in their hands.”

“Tomorrow morning?”

“We leave tomorrow morning. Delivery takes longer.”

“I know how roads work, Orren.”

“People forget when they've paid.”

She pushed the pistol toward him. He wrapped it separately from the fittings and put it in his locked wagon box.

Samira's own weapons were still with the Sins, stolen after Arthur killed her. Being alive again hadn't returned her wrist to health or put an arsenal in her lap. This was a cheap gun buying a route back to the people who made the good ones.

Kaze stopped beside the awning.

“Down to one disappointment?”

She looked up. Her eye moved over him, then over Briar behind him.

“One just arrived.”

He grinned.

“You been saving that?”

“You made it easy.”

Orren gathered his fittings. “Have the letter ready tonight.”

Samira watched him go before giving Kaze her full attention.

“You followed me.”

“Asked a man with a cart.”

“Was he difficult?”

“Little bit.”

“I like him already.”

Briar pulled a stool out with her foot and sat.

“He wouldn't let us ride.”

“I like him more.”

Kaze leaned a hand on the table.

“I came to ask you out. You gonna spend the whole time flirting with somebody who ain't here?”

Samira's smile broadened. Her gaze dropped to the torn knee of his stained trousers.

“Out where?”

“Food. A drink. Somewhere we don't have to talk over a man announcing your death.”

“And after?”

“I'd like you in my bed.”

“Do you have one?”

He glanced toward the inn.

“Working on it.”

“Ambitious proposal from a man sleeping in the grass.”

“Shelter. Had a roof.”

“A sound one,” Briar added, enjoying herself now. “He broke a different roof.”

Samira looked between them.

“Tell me that part.”

They took a table inside. Supper wasn't ready yet, so Samira stayed over a cup of water while Orren settled his wagon business. Kaze counted six silver onto the counter for two rooms and food for three, then put his remaining eight away. The landlord counted the payment twice after hearing the word roof.

“Nothing comes through mine,” he said.

“You keep saying shit like that, people get ideas,” Kaze replied.

The landlord took the candle off their table and replaced it with a cheaper one.

I respected the instinct, even if the saving seemed optimistic.

As the afternoon wore into evening, Briar told the story herself: Elise's invitation, the man reaching for his knife, and the spider going through the shutters. She demonstrated the man's expression after losing his hand. The landlord moved another candle.

Kaze interrupted once to describe the landing.

“I wasn't there for that bit,” Briar said. “I was finding the stairs.”

“You should've seen it.”

“I saw the hole.”

Samira laughed, then shifted her injured wrist when the plate arrived. The movement hurt; she waited for it to pass before picking up her knife.

“And then?”

Briar looked at Kaze.

“He went back to sleep with her.”

Samira put down the knife.

“Of course he did.”

“You say that like you know me,” Kaze said.

“I'm beginning to recognize a fault.”

“You interested in a closer look?”

“I'm interested in whether you ever finish one bad idea before starting another.”

“Finished that one.”

Briar shook her head. “He thinks.”

That got them both laughing. Kaze watched the two women enjoy being in agreement at his expense, then stole a piece of meat from Briar's plate.

She took two from his.

Samira ate with them, asking about the house rather than pretending the story had settled her opinion of him. He answered without making himself its hero. When she asked why he hadn't stopped the remaining guests leaving, he shrugged.

“They heard enough to make their choice.”

“You believe that?”

He considered it.

“I didn't care enough to argue with them.”

“Better answer.”

“Nicer one was available.”

“I met you at Hadran's. Save it for someone else.”

The name changed the table. Briar kept eating, but more slowly.

Samira held Kaze's gaze.

“My weapons are still with your people.”

“Yeah.”

“I'm still taking them back.”

“Heard you yesterday.”

“And if I go through Arthur?”

“I'm not standing there watching you kill him.”

“He killed me.”

“I know.”

Neither softened it. The unfairness sat between them, plain enough that I couldn't mistake it for a misunderstanding.

Samira lifted her cup with her good hand.

“Then we haven't solved anything.”

“Wasn't offering your shit back in exchange for a night.”

“Good. I'd be insulted by how badly you valued it.”

He laughed before he could help himself.

Her expression eased, though the anger hadn't gone anywhere.

“I can want my guns back and enjoy your company. Don't make either one difficult to understand.”

“You enjoying it?”

“Occasionally. You interrupt the good parts.”

“I can shut up.”

Samira leaned across the table, kissed him, and sat back.

“Let's see.”

Briar watched his surprised grin.

“That worked much faster than asking.”

Outside, somebody shouted over a game in the wagon yard. Briar turned toward the open door, curious.

Two carters were throwing little wooden discs at a row of cups on a bench. One had just missed all six and blamed the wind. There wasn't enough wind to move the inn's hanging sign.

Briar stood.

“I'm going to see if he's always bad at it.”

Kaze slid one room key toward her.

“Yours.”

She picked it up, examined the teeth, and smiled.

“A door I choose when to close.”

“That's the idea.”

She touched his cheek as she passed, then went outside without waiting for either of them to plan her evening.

Samira watched her leave.

“She's better at that than most people.”

“What?”

“Being clear.”

He looked back at her.

“Your turn.”

“I want to come upstairs with you.”

“Now?”

“Unless you need another speech.”

He rose. “Had a good one ready.”

“Keep it. You can disappoint me with it later.”

She took his hand and led him to the stairs.

Their room overlooked the wagon yard. Samira put her remaining pistol and knife on the bedside table, then turned when Kaze closed the door.

“You were quieter downstairs than I expected.”

“Didn't want to ruin my review.”

“You haven't earned one.”

“You kissed me.”

“An investigation.”

“Thorough woman.”

“You should hope so.”

He kissed her this time. She answered with her good hand at the back of his neck. When her splint bumped his shoulder, he shifted so she could keep the wrist comfortably against her own chest.

“Don't fuss,” she said.

“You planning to complain if I don't?”

“Probably.”

“Just wanted the rules.”

She laughed and pulled him back to her.

They slept together that evening, by their own choice. The door stayed closed; the argument about Arthur remained exactly where they'd left it.

Afterward, Samira borrowed the room's writing board and sat at the little table by the window. Kaze lay across the bed watching her struggle to keep a folded sheet flat with one working hand.

“Want help?”

“Can you write a decent letter?”

“I can threaten somebody beautifully.”

“This is to people I like.”

“Then you're on your own.”

She looked over her shoulder.

“Come hold the paper, you useless bastard.”

He pulled up a chair beside her and pinned the corners with two fingers.

“We moving fast. Already doing your office work.”

“Try not to let the responsibility change you.”

She addressed the letter to Lani and Miel, the weaponsmiths who'd built what Arthur took. Then she sat with the pen over the empty space beneath their names.

Kaze waited longer than I expected him to.

“Bad opening?” he asked.

“Several.”

“Start with the guns.”

“I'm trying to tell them I'm alive before I tell them I need something.”

He looked from the paper to her face.

“You think they heard?”

“I don't know. That's what makes it difficult.” She put the pen down. “If they haven't, I sound mad. If they have, they'll want an explanation I don't possess.”

“Tell them that.”

“Very elegant.”

“You asked me to hold it flat. Elegance costs extra.”

She laughed, then looked at the page again.

“I don't want every conversation to belong to that shot.”

Kaze's smile faded.

“Didn't mean—”

“I know what you meant. I'm saying what I want.”

He let her finish.

“My wrist hurts. I hate these guns. I'm angry about what happened. None of that means I intend to spend every decent evening explaining why I'm allowed to enjoy it.”

“You liked this one?”

She turned toward him, exasperation giving way to amusement.

“You were doing so well.”

“Need something for my letter.”

“To whom?”

“People who ought to know how impressive I am.”

“Send them a blank page. Let them enjoy the mystery.”

He laughed, and she picked up the pen again. This time she wrote without stopping. Kaze held the sheet steady, his fingers still at the corners. When she finished, she folded the message before he could become interested in reading it.

“Secret admirer?” he asked.

“Two.”

“Damn. I should've brought better guns.”

“We've been over that.”

Her smile stayed as she sealed the letter. Then she looked at him properly.

“This doesn't make you my messenger to Arthur.”

“Didn't deliver anything yet.”

“I know. I'll speak to him myself when I find him.”

Kaze nodded. She hadn't asked him to approve it.

Down in the lamplit yard, Briar whooped. A cup struck the boards, followed by several men protesting at once.

Samira opened the window.

Briar stood beside the throwing line, one hand still extended beyond the pillory. A cup lay broken beneath the bench. She looked up at them.

“They said knock it down!”

“You did,” Kaze called.

“Now they want me to do it less.”

Samira leaned her good forearm on the sill, laughing.

“Try a little mercy. It confuses them.”

Briar considered the cups still standing.

“I'll ask the next one.”

Samira put her pistol and knife back in her belt before they went downstairs. Orren was closing his wagon box for the night. Samira put the sealed letter in his hand and watched him tuck it into the leather packet beneath his coat.

“First light,” he said.

“I'll be here.”

Orren still had a road to travel before that letter reached anyone who could help her. Samira watched him secure the packet, then turned toward the laughter in the yard.

Kaze joined Briar at the bench. She held out the last wooden disc.

“Your turn.”

He looked at the cups. Beyond them, the landlord stood in the doorway with his arms folded.

“How much they charging for these?”

“A copper if you break one,” Briar said. “The man who showed me paid for mine. He said he wanted the lesson to end.”

Samira came to stand beside them.

“Can you hit something without demolishing the address?”

Kaze turned the disc between his fingers.

“You leaving with him tomorrow?” he asked her.

“As far as the next market. After that, I'll decide.”

He glanced at Briar.

“Want to see it?”

“Yes. I want to see somewhere before you break it.”

“Everybody got jokes.”

He tossed the disc. It clipped the nearest cup neatly off the bench, and Samira caught it before it hit the ground.

For a moment they looked at each other, equally pleased with themselves.

Then she set the cup upright again.

“Again,” she said. “I'm beginning to enjoy watching.”
