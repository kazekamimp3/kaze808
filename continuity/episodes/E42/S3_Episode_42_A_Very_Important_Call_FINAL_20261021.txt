Season 3, Episode 42: “A Very Important Call”

The piece of firewood struck Chris's empty seat.

At the stream, Chris looked up from washing his hands.

“What?”

“Nothing,” Daynuh called.

The banjo at Chris's belt remained suspiciously quiet.

Chris narrowed his eyes at it.

“Whatever you said, remember you live with me.”

Inside BELLY, Billy leaned back from the headlamp monitor.

“An arrangement I have considered at length.”

Five mornings earlier, at the port, Dutch van der Linde had paid six silver to keep the net-mender's back room for another three nights.

Seventy-two became sixty-six.

He counted it twice.

Then he put the purse away before Brian could describe the expenditure as an investment.

The room held one bed, Clifford's chair, a basin, and four men with very different ideas about who was taking up too much space.

Tracy sat on the bed.

Miniature Brian was still conjoined to his shoulder.

“Quit moving,” Tracy said.

“I'm not moving.”

“Something moving.”

Brian looked down.

A narrow ridge beneath their joined skin contracted against his little palm.

He had felt it before. That morning it was warm.

He pressed.

Tracy flinched.

“That's you.”

“I'm examining—”

“Examine your fucking attitude. Take your hands off me.”

Brian lifted them.

The ridge slackened.

A cramp passed through the tissue beneath his waist.

He pressed again, gently.

Something on Tracy's side tightened.

Something on Brian's side unfolded.

Not a miracle.

Not a benevolent little surgeon awarding independence for good behavior.

The separate organism in their shared flesh had found two bodies and a very selfish man willing to keep the useful side open.

By midday, a small pale foot pressed against the skin beneath Brian's torso.

Tracy saw it in the mirror.

For several seconds he said nothing.

Clifford looked over from his chair.

“What?”

Tracy pulled his collar down.

“There is a fucking heel in my shoulder.”

Brian looked too.

His toes moved.

Tracy's hand trembled.

Brian's didn't.

He noticed that.

Then he pulled the collar up.

“Could be temporary.”

Tracy pulled it down again.

“That's a foot. Where it's going temporarily?”

Dutch put the cargo ledger aside.

He came closer, looked at the swelling, then at Brian.

“Does it hurt?”

“Not particularly.”

“I was asking him.”

Brian's face tightened.

Tracy sat back on the bed.

“My arm feels like somebody been drinking it.”

Dutch looked at the red marks spreading below the join.

He went for help.

Clifford stayed.

He could stand with his supports. He could also remember what it cost when somebody with a convenient explanation got careless with another man's body.

“Hands where I can see them,” he told Brian.

Brian folded his arms.

“I am not a fucking child.”

“Then this ought to be easy.”

Tracy tried to laugh.

It came out wrong.

Brian felt the laugh pass through the joining tissue. He also felt his new knee bend.

He moved it again.

Tracy caught his wrist.

“Stop.”

“I didn't make this happen.”

“Didn't ask who started it.”

Clifford rolled closer.

Brian looked between them.

“I can feel my feet.”

Tracy stared at him.

“Good. Feel them away from my lungs.”

The ridge tightened.

Brian's second leg pushed through a thin sheath beneath his waist.

He made a sound he hadn't intended anybody else to hear.

Delight.

Tracy heard it.

His hand closed around Brian's middle.

“Get your hands off that thing.”

He pulled Brian away from the shoulder.

The joining skin opened.

A dark wet cord stretched between them, then contracted, dragging the little man back.

Both screamed.

Clifford caught Tracy's wrist.

“Don't tear it.”

Brian's feet began curling back into the sheath.

He could feel them going.

That was the moment.

Not the mysterious twitch. Not the first swelling. Not whatever ancient fucking biology had attached a miniature executive to a comedian like a skin tag with a business proposal.

This.

Tracy was trying to breathe.

Clifford was telling him to let go.

Brian could stop pressing.

For one breath, he did.

Tracy's chest rose.

Brian's legs began folding backward.

“No.”

He put both palms against the cord and pushed.

Tracy grabbed his arm.

Clifford grabbed the other.

Brian twisted his head and bit into the dark tissue between them.

The organism convulsed.

A red fold dragged out of Tracy's shoulder, stretched white, then tore.

Clifford shoved a rolled section of shirt against the opening.

Brian fell onto the bed.

His legs came with him.

Tracy's hand found the edge of the blanket.

He tried to sit forward.

Couldn't.

“Brian.”

It was the first time all day he had said the name without an insult attached.

Brian looked at him.

Clifford shouted for Dutch.

Then he shouted again.

Tracy's fingers loosened.

Brian's new feet kept moving.

When Dutch returned, the old net-mender was behind him.

They were too late.

Clifford remained beside the bed with both hands pressing the shirt.

Dutch touched his shoulder.

Only then did he sit back.

The net-mender looked at the wound, at Brian, and at the little legs kicking free of the wet sheath.

“What is he?”

Brian drew himself upright.

“I am a person.”

The old man looked at Tracy.

“Wasn't asking about the shape.”

Brian climbed down the bedframe.

His first foot found the floor.

The second followed.

He stood.

For the first time, nobody else's body was holding him up.

He took a step.

His knees folded.

He landed face first.

Beneath the wet edge of his new waist, the dark seam twitched without his asking. He pressed a palm against it until it settled.

Nothing about the organism had promised him it was finished.

Clifford didn't move.

Dutch covered Tracy's face.

Brian pushed himself onto his hands.

“I didn't know it would—”

“You knew it hurt,” Clifford said.

Brian looked up.

“You don't understand what it's like.”

Clifford looked down at his own chair.

Brian stopped.

Now, there's an inspirational poster for you.

A little man finally standing on his own two feet, having manufactured both of them out of somebody who repeatedly asked him to stop.

Put it above the gym's water fountain. See if anybody still wants to improve themselves.

Dutch spoke quietly with the net-mender. Tracy's body would remain covered in the room while the old man arranged for it to be taken out. The net-mender would bring him the bill. Dutch nodded.

Brian reached the door long after they finished talking.

He hadn't asked anybody to carry him.

Nobody had offered.

The next morning, the harbor bells began ringing.

Clifford put on his helmet before they went outside.

The black suit and supports had given him a way to walk. They had not made walking free. He used the chair down the uneven road, his gloved hands working the wheels.

Brian walked beside him.

For seventeen steps.

Then Clifford stopped.

“Get on.”

“I can walk.”

“I can die waiting. Get on.”

He lifted Brian onto the closed ledger across his lap.

The little man had wrapped a strip of cloth around his lower body. One corner trailed behind him, making his arrival resemble a furious napkin being served to a man in mourning.

Dutch went ahead.

Beyond the broken outer berth, the water rose.

Not a wave passing through.

Something beneath it was continuing upward.

A long black hull broke the surface. Water ran down steel flanks. A raised command tower emerged, followed by a broad stretch of deck.

Ropes snapped taut on the nearer boats.

Dockworkers ran to ease them.

The vessel's stern churned, holding it off the damaged stone while men in gray came through a hatch.

Dutch took the handles of Clifford's chair and pulled him back from the quay.

Brian grabbed the ledger.

For once, he didn't introduce himself.

The submarine slowed beside the surviving berth.

A mechanical gangway lowered.

At its upper end stood a bald man in a high-collared gray suit.

Scar beside one eye.

Hands folded.

The expression of somebody whose entrance had been designed before any consideration was given to the people expected to witness it.

A crewman came beside him with a small case.

The bald man nodded.

The case opened.

Nothing happened.

He waited.

The crewman touched something inside.

A thin squeal came from a deck speaker.

Three gulls left the quay.

The man in gray closed his eyes.

Then opened them.

“Stop.”

The squeal stopped.

He descended without music.

On the quay, a dockworker was already approaching with questions.

The gray-suited man held up one finger.

“Please. I've just arrived. Allow me to be mysterious for a moment.”

Dutch watched the worker hesitate.

Then he stepped forward.

“Perhaps I can help.”

The bald man looked him over.

“Can you?”

“I know the harbor. I have its cargo records. And you appear to have brought several things this harbor presently lacks.”

“Such as?”

“A building that still works.”

The man looked back at his submarine.

Then at the broken waterfront.

“Yes. Very good.”

He offered a hand.

“Doctor Evil.”

Dutch shook it.

“Dutch van der Linde.”

Dr. Evil waited.

Dutch added, “Doctor.”

“Thank you.”

Clifford wheeled closer.

Dr. Evil saw the helmet.

His expression changed.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

The controlled little smile acquired an entirely different problem.

Brian stood on the ledger.

“We represent Tampon Pandemic Productions.”

Dr. Evil continued looking at Clifford.

“I see.”

“I'm the founder.”

“Are you?”

“Yes.”

“How unfortunate for everyone who has had to say it.”

Dutch lifted the ledger from Clifford's lap and opened it.

“The name is one part of the operation.”

“One hopes.”

He examined the pages.

The figures and destinations meant little to him yet. They meant something to the crewman who had brought the chart.

Dr. Evil gestured.

The crewman came down.

Dutch kept a hand on the ledger while the man compared its harbor headings with their own chart.

The coast didn't match.

The machinery worked.

The familiar world wasn't waiting on the other side of a badly drawn line.

Dr. Evil looked at Dutch again.

“You understand the authorities here?”

“Some.”

“And the people who supply them?”

“Enough to begin.”

“Excellent. We shall begin.”

Brian raised his voice.

“Before we begin, there is a question of structure.”

Dr. Evil looked down at him.

“Is there?”

“I run conceptual development.”

“Then develop the concept of waiting.”

Dutch closed the ledger.

“We require quarters. Equipment. A place to operate without paying for every night as though we're purchasing the darkness individually.”

“I have quarters.”

“And what would you require?”

“Information. Local contacts. People capable of carrying out an instruction without turning it into a childhood memory.”

Clifford's helmet turned slightly.

Dutch saw it.

Dr. Evil didn't.

“Useful work,” Dutch said. “In exchange for useful resources.”

“Yes.”

“Then I deal directly with you.”

“Obviously. I'm not discussing regional strategy with the gentleman dressed as a discarded bandage.”

Brian looked down at his cloth.

Clifford made a small sound inside the mask.

It might have been a laugh.

Dr. Evil looked at him with immediate, misplaced warmth.

“Please. Come aboard.”

Brian pointed at himself.

“I named this organization.”

“Yes,” Dr. Evil said. “And now we must all be brave.”

Dutch didn't shake on the arrangement yet.

“Before we go up there. My people don't become your property because you have beds.”

Dr. Evil considered him.

“Do your people presently enjoy a great many beds?”

“No.”

“Then let us avoid making this sound like a sacrifice.”

Clifford put one gloved hand against his wheel.

“I keep my weapons.”

“Naturally.”

“And the chair.”

Dr. Evil glanced at the gangway.

“We have lifts.”

Clifford looked toward the deck. A crewman was guiding a wheeled equipment case over the broad metal ramp.

“Doors wide enough?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He rolled forward under his own power.

Dutch watched him.

That wasn't devotion. It was a man choosing an entrance he could actually use.

Dr. Evil had noticed the practical question too.

He simply believed it meant something considerably more famous.

Brian raised both hands.

“And my position?”

Dr. Evil looked down.

“You're on a book.”

“Within the organization.”

“We shall find something suitable.”

“I designed its entire presentation.”

Dr. Evil examined the strip of cloth.

“Perhaps we should begin by finding something suitable for that.”

A dockworker approached with a coil of rope over his shoulder. He looked at the gangway, then at Dr. Evil.

“Who pays for this berth?”

Dr. Evil turned.

“Who owns it?”

“Harbormaster.”

“Then why am I negotiating with the rope?”

The worker's face hardened.

Dutch stepped between them before either could improve the encounter.

“He's asking who to send.”

“Then he should send them.”

The worker looked at Dutch.

Dutch nodded once.

The man went.

Dr. Evil watched him leave.

“This place appears very comfortable asking strangers for money.”

“It was comfortable before the ground broke,” Dutch said. “Now it has reasons.”

He opened the ledger again.

“Your machine can bring people here. It doesn't tell you which warehouse has a floor, which road ends in a hole, or who kept collecting after their goods stopped arriving.”

The crewman with the chart leaned closer.

Dutch turned the book toward him, keeping hold of it.

Two harbor headings matched names on the surviving pier markers. The crewman copied them.

The third referred to a road that now ended at the fissure.

Dutch struck a finger against that line.

“Don't send your men there with a load.”

The crewman marked it.

Dr. Evil watched the change appear on the chart.

At last, something useful had happened in front of him without needing applause.

“Very good.”

He looked at Dutch.

“You will deal with the people onshore.”

“Through you.”

“Through me.”

“And when I advise you against something?”

“I shall consider it.”

“Before it's underway.”

Dr. Evil paused.

“Yes. That would be the traditional sequence.”

Dutch closed the book.

Brian folded his arms.

“I have been asking for a planning meeting for weeks.”

“And look,” Dr. Evil said. “We've had one standing beside you.”

Brian looked at Dutch for support.

Dutch was examining the gangway.

A functioning headquarters. Actual staff. Dry quarters. A man with resources who needed somebody to make those resources useful.

He could work with that.

He could also see exactly how much the little man hated being unnecessary.

“We'll hear your proposals inside,” Dutch told him.

“My proposals?”

“Your useful ones.”

Dr. Evil gave Dutch a small, approving nod.

There it was.

His right-hand man had performed his first unpleasant task before either of them had named the position.

Brian climbed down onto the ledger.

“I'm not agreeing to a subordinate creative role.”

Dutch lifted the book.

Brian grabbed its binding.

The distinction continued up the gangway without improving his height.

The gangway took Clifford's chair without difficulty.

That alone did more for Dutch's opinion of their host than another ten minutes of promises could have achieved.

Inside, lights remained lit without lamps being carried between them. Crew worked at fixed stations. Somebody was washing an actual plate.

Dr. Evil gave Dutch a room near his own and ordered food brought for the visitors.

Clifford asked for water to wash his gloves.

It arrived.

Brian asked who would be responsible for creative direction.

Dr. Evil asked whether anyone had found him trousers.

The difference was not lost on him.

A crewman led them into a compact briefing room.

Gray walls. Metal table. Chairs that matched.

One end of the table had a raised seat.

Dr. Evil inspected it.

Then looked at the crewman.

“Why?”

“For visibility, sir.”

“I'm visible.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I have been visible throughout this conversation.”

The man lowered the chair.

Brian watched this with the dangerous interest of somebody discovering a much better version of his own worst habit.

Dutch sat where Dr. Evil indicated.

Clifford stayed in his chair.

Dr. Evil accepted that immediately.

Brian pulled himself onto the table by the edge.

No one had supplied a smaller chair.

He stood beside an empty glass, breathing harder than he wanted anyone to notice.

Dr. Evil folded his hands.

“Now. Our objectives.”

Dutch opened the ledger.

Dr. Evil glanced at Clifford's helmet.

Then glanced again.

He had been waiting for the appropriate moment.

Unfortunately, his appropriate moment was currently obstructing the useful meeting.

He rang a small bell.

The crewman returned.

“The long case.”

Dutch looked up.

Brian looked toward the door.

Clifford remained still.

The case arrived carried in both hands. Dr. Evil took it personally, set it on the table and opened the clasps.

Inside lay a metal hilt.

He turned the case toward Clifford.

“I have admired your work for a very long time.”

Clifford's helmet tilted.

Dr. Evil smiled.

“A very long time.”

Dutch looked at the weapon.

Brian stepped closer.

“Is that—”

“Not for you.”

Brian stopped.

“I hadn't asked for it.”

“Then we've saved time.”

Dr. Evil lifted the hilt.

“A genuine weapon. Real laser beams.”

He let that sit.

Then, unable to resist:

“Real frickin' laser beams.”

He activated it.

A bright blade sprang from the hilt.

The room's gray surfaces caught its light.

Brian stepped backward so quickly that he struck the empty glass.

It rolled.

Clifford caught it before it fell off the table.

Dr. Evil looked delighted.

Dutch looked at the blade's distance from the ceiling.

“I'd give him room.”

“Of course.”

Dr. Evil switched it off and presented it hilt first.

Clifford accepted it.

The metal settled in his glove.

No mystical recognition passed between man and weapon.

No power filled his limbs.

He had acquired a dangerous object from somebody who had apparently decided that he ought to have one.

“That's quite a gift,” he said.

Dr. Evil's smile widened.

“I thought you would appreciate it.”

Clifford rested the hilt across his lap.

“What's it need?”

The crewman explained the activation control and showed the secured power assembly. He did not promise an endless charge. He pointed out the safe grip and the clearance the blade required.

Clifford listened.

Then asked him to repeat the control sequence.

Brian stood beside the glass.

“His weapon has a briefing.”

Dr. Evil ignored him.

The crewman stepped clear. Clifford pointed the hilt toward the empty end of the room and activated it once.

Light filled the space.

He held it steady.

Switched it off.

“Again,” he told the crewman.

The man repeated the sequence.

Clifford checked the recessed control with his thumb.

Dutch watched the door while another crewman found a strap that could secure the hilt to Clifford's chair without fouling the wheel.

Brian sat on the edge of the case.

“Just so I understand the criteria.”

“No,” Dr. Evil said.

“I haven't stated the criteria.”

“And already they're exhausting.”

It took another half minute to find a place where the strap wouldn't catch Clifford's coat.

He tested the wheel.

Moved his hand past the hilt.

Tested it again.

By the time he looked up, nearly two minutes had passed since Dr. Evil had handed him the weapon.

Clifford put both hands beside his helmet.

“I ought to thank you properly.”

Dr. Evil inclined his head.

Clifford removed the mask.

The room remained exactly the same.

Which was unfortunate.

Some part of Dr. Evil had clearly expected the universe to intervene.

Instead, he was looking at an adult cowboy with a slightly crooked neck, rough unfinished tattoos, and a very sincere expression of gratitude.

“Thank you,” Clifford said.

Dr. Evil stared.

Then looked at the mask.

Then at the hilt already secured to the chair.

Dr. Evil had thought he was giving Darth Vader a lightsaber.

He had instead armed an injured cowboy with a lifetime of unsolicited follow-up questions.

The gift had acquired a strap.

He had personally supervised the strap.

For the first time that morning, the man who owned a submarine appeared to have misplaced the means of leaving a room.

“Yes,” he said.

A pause.

“You're very welcome.”

Clifford waited.

Dr. Evil smiled harder.

Clifford waited patiently. That made it worse. A less considerate man might have interrupted with something useful, such as his fucking name.

“Very welcome, Mister…”

He left enough space for a name.

More than enough.

Brian looked from one face to the other.

Dutch sat back.

Clifford said, “Jack.”

Dr. Evil nodded with immediate, fraudulent recognition.

“Jack.”

“Jack Marston. Though nowadays I go by Clifford.”

“Of course.”

“Not a name I chose.”

Dr. Evil's smile held.

Clifford looked down at the mask.

“Arthur and Chris started that. Before the cliff business. There was a dog involved, originally. Then the tattoos.”

Brian lifted one hand.

Dr. Evil pointed at it without looking.

“No.”

“I wasn't—”

“No.”

Clifford continued.

“My father wanted me to be something else. A man with books, a life that didn't end with a gun in his hand. Then the government killed him. My mother died after. I found the man responsible.”

Dutch's attention sharpened at the mention of John.

Dr. Evil glanced toward the door.

It was still there.

No incoming emergency had come through it.

Clifford rested one hand on the hilt.

“After that I thought I'd finished something. Instead, I came here. Chris pushed me off another cliff, and Arthur—”

Dr. Evil picked up the receiver beside his chair.

It hadn't rung.

“Yes.”

Clifford stopped.

Dr. Evil listened intently to absolutely fucking nothing.

“Yes. This is he.”

Brian's mouth opened.

Dr. Evil looked straight at him.

The little man closed it.

“I understand,” Dr. Evil said.

He covered the receiver.

“My apologies, Jack. A very important call.”

Clifford held the mask against his chest.

“Didn't mean to take up your time.”

“Not at all. Fascinating work.”

Dutch's eyes moved to Dr. Evil.

Dr. Evil kept smiling.

“One of the men will show you where to secure the case. We shall continue shortly.”

Clifford looked at the weapon, then at him.

“Still. Thank you.”

“You are most welcome.”

He waited until Clifford had wheeled out.

Then he pointed toward Brian.

“One of the men has something you can wear. Ask him.”

Brian looked down at his cloth.

Dr. Evil waited until Brian had climbed down the chair beside the table.

Waited while the little man crossed the room on his new legs, visibly furious that an exit could take this long.

Brian stopped at the door.

“I'm still the founder.”

“Excellent. Found the corridor.”

Brian left.

The latch clicked.

Dr. Evil lowered the receiver.

Dutch remained seated.

A long silence.

Then Dr. Evil said, “Who the fuck was that cowboy?”

At the stream, four days later, Chris dried his hands on his pants and came back to the fire.

Billy said, “I believe we should discuss the menu.”
