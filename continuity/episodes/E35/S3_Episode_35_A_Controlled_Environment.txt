Season 3 — Episode 35: “A Controlled Environment”

The lump on his preparation table moved. A wet, dragging breath lifted the cloth, then lowered it. Frylock hovered beside the table with the expression of someone about to insist the situation was educational.

“No. It addressed me. That's a complete fucking problem.”

Under the cloth, something coughed.

Oh, good. We're doing parenthood again. Apparently growing one twenty-foot gentleman in a vegetable patch is enough to get your kitchen classified as a maternity ward. No application. No inspection. Just one biological impossibility and suddenly the milkshake's giving you reproductive advice.

Listen to me. I'm already assuming Chris is the victim. That's how this bastard gets you: put him beside a worse man and your brain starts issuing humanitarian medals. I tried that with Wolverine. He told me to shut the fuck up and, devastatingly, had supporting evidence.

Let's establish what actually went into the bowl.

Earlier that afternoon, Billy had stopped at a roadside cook's awning on the inland route. The harbor was behind them now, its broken masts no longer visible between the hills. Kay Kay stood clear of the canvas, holding his little pear tree while Mahnuh drank from a trough.

Chris saw the hanging meat through the exterior monitor and asked Billy to turn his head.

“Not that side. Other side. The ribs.”

Billy looked at the cook's ribs.

“The animal's ribs, Billy.”

“You must learn to specify.”

Kaze put his last three personal silver on the counter for meat, onions and bread. Billy swallowed the wrapped purchase whole and directed it into the dry kitchen inside him. Then the group kept walking.

Chris opened the parcel, inspected it and began separating supper from the things supper had previously been attached to.

Meatwad watched from the table's clean end.

“Was that somebody?”

“A sheep.”

“Did you know him?”

“No.”

Meatwad nodded. “Probably for the best.”

Chris put the good cuts aside. Frylock asked for the scraps, ostensibly to examine what could be composted for the surviving garden bed.

“Take those,” Chris said. “Leave the meat I'm using alone.”

That permission would subsequently be stretched so far it should have qualified as a separate species.

“You know, growing a tree isn't that impressive. They do that in dirt.”

Chris glanced at him.

“What have you grown?”

“A following.”

“Where?”

Meatwad turned to look behind Shake.

Frylock's mouth twitched. Shake caught it.

“Oh, that's funny? You could make something better, right? With your enormous collection of explanations?”

“At least I could explain the result.”

Chris carried his portion of the food to the cooking area before answering.

“Good. Explain it after dinner.”

Then he'd returned to RDR2 while the food cooked. On the first monitor, a stranger beside a horse made the mistake of existing near something Chris could loot. On the second, real daylight moved across the inland road.

Behind Chris, the conversation continued without him.

An hour later, the theory was breathing under his dishcloth.

Frylock lifted a corner.

Two uneven eyes stared out of a greasy face. The rest of it lay bundled in cloth, narrow lengths of wood supporting a body that seemed to have been assembled during an argument about which direction people went.

Chris leaned closer.

The eyes shifted toward Frylock before returning to him.

“Dad?”

“Don't encourage it,” Shake said. “It'll become dependent.”

“You told me to let it finish!”

“I meant developing.”

Chris looked at Frylock.

“What did you do?”

“I arranged the available material into a recognizable configuration.”

“That's a fucking sentence you say before somebody finds teeth in a swimming pool.”

Charles lowered his spoon over the face.

“I can guide him.”

Chris caught his wrist.

“You can't guide yourself past my refrigerator.”

The thing on the table whispered, “I don't want the spoon.”

Chris let go of Charles and moved the spoon out of reach instead.

For the first time, Frylock stopped watching the thing and watched Chris closely.

Back in the harbor district, Tracy Morgan had his head under a curtain and a much smaller man's head sticking through a hole above it.

“Raise the box,” he said.

“Your posture is compromising the illusion,” Brian replied.

“My posture is compromising my fucking spine.”

They were in TPP's rented headquarters. Dutch had spent four of the morning's fifty-nine silver on cloth and a handful of ordinary supplies. An existing empty crate became a stage. Draped fabric concealed Tracy's large body while Brian, still conjoined at his shoulder, presented the appearance of a tiny severed head resting in a wooden frame.

Brian's little arms were hidden beneath the cloth. He had objected to that until Dutch explained that severed heads weren't normally expected to clasp their hands while negotiating.

Clifford sat nearby, his black helmet beside his wheelchair. He examined the arrangement with his permanently crooked neck tilted toward it.

“You look like something that comes free with a bad purchase.”

“Jealousy,” Brian said, “is an understandable response to a superior public presence.”

Tracy raised his shoulders. The entire display rose with him.

“Oh, shit. The superior presence can levitate.”

Jimmy laughed. Brian glared down at him.

The Coon had written the announcement. Jimmy was supposed to read it outside, but stopped after the opening claim.

“A head that has s-survived the complete destruction of its body?”

“Exactly,” Cartman said.

“His body's still there.”

“Jimmy, please. You're describing the production, not the experience.”

Dutch took the announcement, read it and smiled.

The boy had an instinct for the thing Dutch understood best: offer people a larger world, then stand directly in front of the entrance holding a bag.

“We give them a show,” Dutch said. “Nothing more.”

“Under my direction,” Cartman added.

“And my name,” Tracy said from under the curtain.

“Your name would confuse the audience,” Brian replied.

There was a dangerous silence beneath the fabric.

Clifford reached down and moved his helmet farther from the stage.

Jimmy agreed to perform his own material before the reveal. He would not announce a medical fact he knew was false. Timmy chose the entrance, where he could see the room and open the door without waiting for Cartman to remember other people had legs, wheels or opinions.

For a little while, it worked.

Jimmy drew a small audience from the street. People with an afternoon's worth of harbor frustration stopped to listen. Some paid to come inside, more interested in laughing at a talking head than believing in one.

Dutch welcomed them with the warmth of a man who had never personally met a refund.

Nine silver entered the collection bowl.

Under the curtain, Tracy's thigh began to cramp.

Inside BELLY, Chris was trying to establish whether his lunch had a spine.

He didn't touch it yet.

“Can you move?”

The bundled figure shook.

“He's frightened,” Charles said.

“You're standing over him with kitchenware.”

“Symbols matter.”

“So does backing the fuck up.”

Shake moved around the table.

“We should establish useful habits early. A person with no work ethic is a burden on the entire household.”

Chris stared at him.

Even the thing on the table turned its eyes.

“What?” Shake asked.

Frylock intervened before anyone could answer honestly.

“Chris, why did you immediately accept that it might be alive?”

Chris pointed at Charles.

“He used to hang above my television.”

“That's one exceptional event.”

“There's a tree outside carrying a smaller tree, and I'm having this conversation in the gastrointestinal tract of a banjo player. You want me to start being skeptical at the lamb?”

A beautiful moment for science. The subject had identified every confounding variable and told the researcher to shove the clipboard up his ass without there actually being a clipboard. Efficient. Sustainable. I'd call it green research, but the researcher is mostly French fries and has already started defending himself in the tone people use when their experiment has learned the word lawyer.

“The important distinction,” Frylock began, “is whether you recognize personhood because of the material, or because it resembles—”

“I'm recognizing that it asked Charles to put a spoon down. That is the first sensible thing anything's said in this room.”

Under the tablecloth, something shifted sideways.

Chris heard a small scrape against the bowl.

His eyes narrowed.

Shake noticed his attention changing and hurried to reclaim it.

“Obviously, as the person who initiated this project, I would retain a controlling interest.”

“In what?”

“Whatever he becomes.”

The figure said, “I don't like that.”

“Nobody likes their first job.”

“He hasn't even sat up,” Chris said.

“Exactly. Poor initiative.”

The cloth twitched. A length of string became visible beside the bowl, disappearing beneath a fold.

Chris reached toward it.

Frylock moved between his hand and the table.

“Let the interaction develop naturally.”

Chris looked at the hovering carton. Then at the string.

Then he smiled.

Not his nice smile. The little professional smile he wore when somebody explained that a piece of equipment was broken and he could see their fucking thumb holding the wrong switch.

“Where's Meatwad?”

Nobody answered quickly enough.

The bundle said, in a newly cautious voice, “He's busy.”

Chris nodded.

“All right.”

He picked up his kitchen knife.

Frylock's expression changed.

“Chris, don't—”

“There's a string caught under his collar. I'm cutting the string.”

The supposed chest burst open.

Meatwad shot out through the front, shoving wet trimmings aside. A strip of fat slapped Shake across the face. The wooden supports collapsed. What had looked like a breathing rib cage became a bowl, two offcuts and a very offended ball of meat sliding across the table. Frylock's string held the strips of workshop scrap together; Meatwad had been pushing them apart from inside, wearing the trimmings around his face.

“I don't wanna be your son no more!”

Chris held the knife perfectly still.

“I wasn't cutting you, Meatwad. I was reaching for the knot.”

“Well, nobody told me where surgery starts!”

Meatwad rolled onto a clean plate and shook himself. The face he'd been making relaxed into his own.

For a moment, the kitchen contained only the soft noise of something dripping.

Then Shake peeled the fat off his cheek.

“He gets that cowardice from his father.”

Outside, the group had left the main track to stop beside a narrow stream. Billy lowered himself onto a broad patch of firm ground while Arthur found shade and Daniel hung his spear across two forked branches.

Kay Kay put down his pear tree. He washed the dried human blood from his fingers downstream of Mahnuh, watching the red loosen into the current. The cloth around his forearm stayed wrapped; he used his other hand to wet the plant's soil.

Daynuh watched him pause before pouring more.

“Enough,” Kay Kay said, remembering the seller.

“Good. Can't drown it into being grateful.”

Mahnuh moved into the shade beneath Kay Kay's arm. The tree held it there, studying the dog's decision, then spread his fingers a little wider.

Daniel took a long drink from his frosted bottle, swayed, and caught the forked branch with the toes of one bare foot.

“He's training you.”

“Is that different from teaching?”

“Depends who gets the treat.”

Kay Kay considered the sleeping dog.

“He seems satisfied with the arrangement.”

Kay Kay lifted the pear tree and studied the roots visible at the tub's drainage hole. Then he looked along the stream.

“I think I bought something that doesn't want to travel.”

Kaze sat on a rock nearby. “You just got it.”

“Yes. That was before I looked at the bottom.”

He chose a patch above the wet bank, set down the tub and pressed his heel into the soil. Thin roots spread from his foot, prying loose earth aside. He widened the small hollow with his uninjured hand, then eased the pear's root ball out of its container.

Daynuh steadied the trunk while Kay Kay brought soil back around it. The twenty-foot creature kept the new roots clear of his own, then withdrew his living tendrils from the ground.

“You're leaving it?” Kaze asked.

“I'd like to walk. It appears to prefer standing.”

“Somebody might take the pears.”

“Isn't that what you were going to do?”

Arthur looked over from the shade. “Got you there.”

Kaze grinned. “I'm not arguing with a tree about fucking pears.”

He watered the disturbed soil, stopping before it pooled. Mahnuh followed his moving shadow and settled again. The empty tub remained beside the walking stick.

“Your fucking roommates built a man out of dinner.”

“Another one?”

“No. This one was fraud.”

“Chris. Begin with what remains edible.”

At TPP headquarters, Dutch pulled the curtain aside just enough to reveal Brian's framed head.
