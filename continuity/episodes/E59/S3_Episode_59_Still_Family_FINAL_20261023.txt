Season 3, Episode 59: “Still Family”

Chris lifted the duck's lid for a third time.

“Chris,” Daniel said.

“What?”

“We believe it closes.”

Chris set it down.

Late afternoon lay warm across the shelf of rock. Below them, water still shone on the road where the aqueduct had emptied itself. Arthur's cleaning cloth moved along the Count. Mahnah slept against Daynah's leg, one scraped paw turned upward.

Daniel sat up.

His tail caught the bottle before it rolled. He looked at the amber liquor, took a drink, and held it a moment longer than necessary.

“I need to tell you guys something.”

Kaze, still crouched beside him, looked over.

“Don't say you're pregnant.”

“Nah. Just retaining spirits.”

Daniel smiled at his own stupid joke. Then he rubbed his thumb over the bottle's frost.

“I'm not staying.”

Arthur's cloth stopped.

Chris turned from the food.

“What do you mean, not staying?”

“I'm going to the spirit realm.”

Kaze watched his face.

“You just got here.”

“I know.”

“This morning.”

“I was here. Strong opening. Very confused breakfast.”

Kaze didn't laugh.

Daniel let the joke go.

“I'm going to find Master Oogway. Get absolutely wasted with him. Sit somewhere nobody's trying to turn a staircase into a fucking crater.”

He glanced down at the broken crossing, then back at Kaze.

“A turtle. You understand the commitment to sitting down I'm making here.”

“And you're just... going?”

“Yeah.”

Daniel stayed seated on the same warm rock, drunk, barefoot, with grit in his clothes. He rubbed one heel against the stone while the others caught up with a decision he'd already made.

Daynah shifted carefully out from under Mahnah's head.

“You sure?”

Daniel nodded.

“Yeah. I am.”

Chris's fingers tightened around a scrap of food wrapping.

“You could've said something before we walked all the way up this bastard hill.”

“Would you have picked a nicer one?”

“I'd have complained earlier.”

Daniel laughed. Chris did too, reluctantly, and looked away.

Kaze stood. He took two steps toward the edge, stopped, then came back.

“So what was today?”

Daniel looked up at him.

“Today was good.”

“You were pissed at me ten minutes ago.”

“Yeah. You killed a guy who couldn't get up.”

“I know what I did.”

“Then don't make me explain it.”

Kaze's jaw worked once.

Daniel reached for his spear and brought it upright beside him.

“I can be glad I got today with you and still think that was fucked. I'm drunk, man. I can hold two things.”

He lifted the bottle.

“Three, if I use the tail.”

Kaze looked at it despite himself.

Daniel stood, swayed, and found his balance with a small adjustment of his bare feet.

“You kept my stuff,” he said. “All of you. Spear. Money. Chris kept the bottle extremely well lubricated.”

“It wasn't gonna go bad.”

“Then why'd you drink it?”

Chris opened his mouth.

Daniel pointed at him.

“Exactly. That's family.”

The grin stayed, but his voice changed beneath it.

“You made a place for me. Even when I was being a pain in the ass. Even when I disagreed with you. I got to come back and find you still being you.”

He leaned the spear securely against the rock and curled his tail around the bottle, leaving both hands free. His eyes moved across them.

“I love you guys. Thank you for being my family.”

Nobody improved the sentence for him.

Mahnah got up first.

The enormous dog pushed his head beneath Daniel's arm. Daniel bent around him, both arms sliding against the thick neck. Mahnah's tail struck Daynah's leg with a heavy thump.

“You keep an eye on these idiots,” Daniel murmured.

Mahnah leaned harder. Daniel staggered back half a step, laughing into his fur.

“All right. You can be one of the idiots too.”

Daynah waited until the dog eased away, then bent low and gathered Daniel against him. He didn't lift him this time.

Daniel's cheek pressed against the big man's shoulder.

“Take care of yourself,” Daynah said.

“You too.”

The hug lasted until Daniel patted his back.

“And the dog. Obviously. He's the one holding this whole outfit together emotionally, which is alarming because he eats people.”

Daynah's laugh came out rough.

“Go on, you little shit.”

Arthur put the Count aside before Daniel reached him.

They caught each other's forearms. Daniel pulled him into a hug; Arthur's free hand settled between his shoulders.

“Glad I got to give that purse back,” Arthur said.

“I checked. Still tied.”

“Told you.”

“Yeah. That's what made it weird.”

Arthur smiled against his shoulder, then let him go.

“Have yourself a good time.”

“That's the whole plan.”

“Well. Finally found somebody with a sensible one.”

Chris was already standing.

“Come here,” he said, before Daniel could make him ask twice.

Daniel hugged him. Chris held on with both hands, his face turned sideways.

Chris flinched against the solid ice of Daniel's forearm.

“You're cold as shit.”

“Cryomancer.”

“I know what the job is. I'm telling you how the service feels.”

Daniel laughed, and Chris's grip tightened for a second.

Then Chris stepped back and wiped his nose with the back of his wrist.

“Billy wants out.”

“I didn't say it yet,” Billy's voice came through the headgear.

“You were going to.”

“Well. I would like to finish one goddamn farewell request before you put words in my mouth.”

Chris opened down the front. His body folded inward as Billy's huge, scaled shoulders rolled out through the inversion. The catfish settled on the shelf, one fresh wound still drawing tight across his shoulder.

He set his banjo safely beside Arthur's gun.

Then he crouched.

Daniel walked into the reach of those enormous arms. Billy held him with a care that looked strange on hands which had pulled a wagon backward through its own wheel.

“Been a pleasure havin' you, son.”

Daniel rested a hand below the healing shoulder.

“You make a weird fucking house.”

“And yet you kept comin' back.”

“Good company.”

Billy's barbels shifted against Daniel's hair.

“Well,” he said quietly. “We tried.”

Inside, Chris stood by the live monitor. Charles, Meatwad, and Shake were there too. The camera stayed lowered. For once, the room did not need a better angle.

Daniel looked up toward Billy's hat-front aperture.

“You lot inside. Look after each other, yeah?”

Meatwad raised one small hand toward the screen.

Daniel couldn't see it. Chris could.

Outside, Billy released him.

Kaze hadn't moved closer.

Daniel crossed the space himself.

For a moment they stood face-to-face, both trying to get something said without making it sound like something somebody would say at a funeral.

“You don't have to turn it into a whole thing,” Daniel said.

“I'm not.”

“You're doing that face.”

“It's my fucking face.”

“Yeah. Been meaning to bring that up.”

Kaze breathed a laugh through his nose.

Then he stepped in and hugged him.

Hard.

Daniel caught the back of his shoulder. Kaze's other hand spread against Daniel's upper back, fingers still stained from the crossing.

“Love you, nigga,” Kaze said.

“Love you too.”

Kaze held on a moment longer.

Daniel didn't hurry him.

When they separated, Daniel picked up his spear. Kaze looked down at it.

“Taking it?”

“Yeah. Bottle, spear, money. For once I'm leaving somewhere with everything I brought.”

He touched the little purse tucked securely into his clothes. The hundred and ten silver Arthur had kept for him went with its owner. The bottle curled safely in his tail; the spear remained in his hand.

“I don't know what a turtle charges for drinks,” Daniel added. “I'm not arriving broke.”

A white light gathered around his bare feet.

Kaze glanced down, then back at him. Daynah put a hand on Mahnah's harness as the dog stepped forward to sniff the brightness.

The light climbed softly. It didn't blast the hillside or throw anybody away. Daniel stood inside it, his dark hair and blue clothes losing their edges.

He raised the bottle once.

“Take it easy, boys.”

His smile was the last clear thing.

Then Daniel, his spear, his purse, and his bottle faded completely, leaving the warm rock empty.

No body fell. No doorway remained.

Billy stayed crouched a moment after there was nobody left in his reach.

Mahnah sniffed the place where Daniel's feet had been. Kaze watched the last of the light disappear from his own hand.

They let him go.

And there he goes. Beautiful goodbye. Everybody got a hug.

Now that he's gone—Kaze, you fucking fraud.

The real one. Our creator. The man outside this story with executive control over whether a monkey gets tomorrow.

You wanted Daniel out again. You knew killing him twice would be lame, especially after bringing him back with the narrative effort of finding a french fry under the seat. So, last minute, you slapped together the spirit-realm bullshit.

Suddenly there's a vacancy beside Master Oogway. A beautiful white light. A dignified spiritual departure. Oh, look, the turtle's accepting walk-ins at his fucking celestial liquor cabinet.

You didn't want the cast member, you didn't want another murder, and you absolutely wanted credit for a touching farewell. You greedy little bastard. You ordered emotional depth with the same thumb that went, “Actually, get him out of here.”

“I'm standing right here,” Kaze said.

Other Kaze. Keep your shirt off and wait your turn.

You brought Daniel back, looked at him for one afternoon, and decided eternity had better seating. You caught yourself reaching for the same corpse twice and panicked. So now the exit has mood lighting. Put a fucking dimmer on a firing squad and this man would call it character development.

And the nasty part? The goodbye worked. They meant it. Daniel meant it. You made everybody have actual feelings while you quietly pushed a drunk uncle into the Kung Fu Panda departure lounge.

“He's going to the spirit realm.”

Motherfucker, you fired him with a sunset.

Billy let out a low, unwilling laugh.

Kaze looked toward my voice.

“You done?”

With that specific offense. I am never done as a person.

“Yeah. I noticed.”

Arthur looked between Kaze and Billy.

“Something I ought to know?”

“No,” Kaze said. “He's being a dick.”

Arthur picked up his cleaning cloth.

“Well. That narrows it.”

They ate before they moved.

Billy admitted the pot and the salvaged captain's sword into storage, then picked up his banjo. He carried it inward as the inversion brought Chris back out with portions of their remaining food. Chris divided them, ate his own, and sat with the others while the sun slipped lower.

For a little while, nobody tried to fill Daniel's place.

Then a dining room went past on the road below.

Arthur saw it first.

“Now, that's peculiar.”

Six horses came downhill from inland, pulling a long, high-sided wagon beneath a painted canopy. Its side panels were folded upward. A polished table stood inside, surrounded by upholstered chairs. Glass lamps swung in restrained little arcs above silver dishes and a cabinet with carved doors.

A man walked beside the rear wheel, keeping an eye on a load that probably cost more than his house.

Chris rose slowly.

“Is that a room?”

“Looks like one,” Arthur said.

“A whole room.”

Kaze joined them at the edge.

“On wheels.”

“I can see the wheels.”

“Just checking. You sounded like your brain was trying to climb into it.”

Two closed freight wagons followed. Armed escorts rode before and behind. At a turning below the shelf, the little procession left the main road for a broad posting yard enclosed by a low stone wall.

Arthur's red eye narrowed, magnifying the painted lettering along the canopy.

“Vey. Private dining interiors. Coastal export.”

Chris followed the disappearing cabinet with his whole head.

Daynah stood beside him.

“That's a nice piece.”

“Which one?”

“The way the side folds up. See those braces? Takes the weight back into the frame instead of letting it hang off the roof.”

Kaze looked at him.

“You have been watching it for four seconds.”

“Long enough to see someone knew what the fuck they were doing.”

Chris nodded vigorously.

“Thank you.”

“Cabinet's good too,” Daynah added.

Kaze looked from one to the other.

“You two are about to make this my problem.”

“You walked over here,” Chris said.

Arthur watched the final escort turn through the gate.

“Silver looked real.”

Kaze turned toward him.

Arthur smiled.

“Thought I'd contribute.”

They packed the remaining food and walked along the ridge until they could see into the posting yard without standing above its entrance. Mahnah moved with them, favoring the scraped paw briefly before settling beside Daynah behind a low outcrop.

Below, porters were lowering the display wagon's steps. A woman in a dark traveling coat stood beside it, directing them. Two guests climbed aboard and took seats at the table.

One of them rang a little silver bell.

A servant stepped forward.

Chris stared.

“He was right there.”

“Maybe the fellow's shy,” Arthur said.

“Shy? He's making a smaller man answer a doorbell that lives in his hand.”

The guest rang again. The servant, who was already speaking to him, waited for the sound to stop.

Chris pointed down the slope.

“That. That's what you do when money starts growing mold on your fucking manners.”

Kaze grinned.

“You want the bell.”

“I want to see what it's made of.”

“You want a little come-here-dickhead button.”

Daynah chuckled.

“Could put it by his game.”

Billy spoke through Chris's headgear.

“He rings that at me, I'm puttin' it somewhere he'll have to jump to ask for dinner.”

Chris ignored him with the effort of a man imagining a bell beside his game.

A porter came through the yard carrying a folded notice. He fastened it beside the stable entrance.

Arthur focused on it.

His smile faded.

“Your names again.”

Kaze crouched lower, more interested than alarmed.

“Both?”

“Yours and Chris's.”

Chris looked toward the display room, then the notice.

“That fucking book.”

“Your handwriting,” Arthur said.

“I know whose handwriting.”

Below, the woman in the traveling coat read the notice. She spoke to one of her escorts. He walked to the gate and changed places with the man who had been watching the horses.

Kaze's grin thinned.

The room, the silver, the polite little performance: all of it remained right there. Getting close without turning the place into another fortified refusal had just become harder.

Arthur eased back from the ridge.

“I'll have a look.”

Chris started to rise.

“I'll come.”

“I was hoping to reach the table before somebody screamed.”

Chris settled again.

Arthur looked at Kaze.

“You too.”

“Didn't say anything.”

“You were wearing it on your face.”

Kaze looked almost offended, then amused.

“Bring me something nice.”

“Bring yourself some patience.”

Arthur pulled the Count apart into its two revolvers and holstered them. Then he went down alone.

At the gate, he tipped his hat. The escort looked over his coat, the revolvers at his belt, and the red machinery around his eye.

“Business?”

“Curiosity.”

“Costs extra.”

“Usually does.”

The man let him through.

Arthur walked past the posted descriptions without stopping. At the display wagon, the woman in the coat was explaining a sliding cupboard to her guests.

“Everything is secured for transport. The cabinet stays upright through the coastal road. The table is fixed here. The chairs secure beneath the rail.”

She demonstrated the latch.

Arthur leaned against a wheel.

“Planning to eat while you run away?”

The woman glanced down.

“Planning to deliver what was ordered.”

“Whole room?”

“The installation travels assembled. The buyer sees it working, we dismantle what the destination requires, and no local carpenter gets to tell me he lost three pieces and improved the design.”

Arthur looked at the joinery.

“Know a man who'd appreciate that.”

“Then introduce him after delivery.”

One guest opened a silver dish.

Inside lay a magnificent roast bird with glazed skin and carefully arranged fruit.

He touched the breast.

His finger squeaked.

“Wax,” Madam Vey said.

The man withdrew his hand.

“I knew that.”

“Then you may admire it without testing it again.”

Arthur lowered his head so the brim hid his smile.

Madam Vey opened the little brass-fronted box beside the cabinet. She wound its handle, released a catch, and a delicate mechanical tune began beneath the table talk.

No musician. Just a pinned cylinder turning inside a well-made case.

“For privacy,” she said. “Conversation carries less.”

Arthur listened.

“Reckon it helps if the conversation ain't worth carrying.”

The woman shut the lid.

“You would be surprised how much people pay for that.”

He asked about the road. She told him the direct route had been closed after the crossing collapsed; her drivers would take the eastern quarry road at first light, circle the damaged stretch, and continue toward the coastal export depot.

“Long way round,” Arthur said.

“Longer still if I put six horses and a dining room into a river.”

One of her escorts approached with a harness strap in his hand. She excused herself, inspected a split near the buckle, and sent him to have it replaced before morning.

Arthur watched her check the replacement strap herself before accepting it.

Then a porter began backing the first freight wagon toward the lower wall.

Arthur looked past it. A horse trough against the wall had split at one joint. Its leaking water was spreading across the apron where the rear wheels were headed.

“Wouldn't leave that there,” he said.

The porter kept walking backward.

Madam Vey turned.

“Why?”

Arthur pointed.

“Ground's taking water. Looks firm on top. Won't stay that way under a loaded wheel.”

She stepped off the wagon, pushed the toe of her boot into the darkening earth, and called the porter to a halt.

“Upper side. Beside the display.”

The man grumbled but brought the team around. One wheel began to sink before it cleared the wet patch. Arthur took hold of the rear corner and helped it over the lip without making a show of how little the weight troubled him.

Madam Vey watched him release it.

“Useful sort of curiosity.”

“I have my moments.”

“Looking for work?”

Arthur brushed the mud from his palm.

“Got something lined up.”

“Does it pay?”

“Hoping it will.”

She nodded toward the spare chair on the display steps.

“Sit while you decide.”

He did. The cushion accepted him so pleasantly that his expression became suspicious.

Madam Vey noticed.

“Horsehair. Properly packed.”

Arthur shifted once.

“Horse did better for himself than most folks.”

“The chair has no complaints.”

“Give it time.”

He stood before he got comfortable, thanked her, and walked out past the gate guard.

Above the yard, Kaze had watched the whole thing.

“Did he just help them?” he asked.

Daynah followed the freight wagon into its new position beside the display.

“Got both loads close together. On solid ground.”

Kaze considered that.

“That's thoughtful.”

“Real considerate son of a bitch,” Daynah said.

When he returned to the ridge, Chris had moved six feet closer to the edge.

“How's the cabinet?”

“Well secured.”

“Good hinges?”

“Didn't ask their names.”

Daynah laughed.

Arthur crouched and told them what he'd learned: the morning departure, the quarry detour, the escorts, the two freight wagons, the assembled dining room.

“She offered me work,” he said.

Kaze looked delighted.

“You take it?”

“Didn't care for the hours.”

“What hours?”

“Any she had in mind.”

“Food's wax,” he added.

Chris's face changed.

“All of it?”

“The bird I saw.”

“Looked too shiny,” Chris muttered. “Like somebody taught a chicken how to sweat expensive.”

“Man poked it.”

“Of course he did. Rich motherfuckers don't believe texture until somebody's died making it available.”

Kaze glanced down at the wagon.

“You disappointed?”

“Little bit.”

“In the fake chicken?”

“In the real man. He had eyes.”

Arthur described the music box.

Billy's voice came through immediately.

“What's it play?”

Arthur hummed a few uneven notes.

Billy waited.

“That's how it goes?”

“That's how I remember it.”

“Well, either that thing's broken or you are.”

“Might be both.”

Chris was looking at the road now, tracing the line of it with one finger.

“If they're taking the quarry turn, they're going away from the water first.”

“Yes,” Arthur said. “Around what you fellas broke.”

Kaze smiled again.

“So we made ourselves a shortcut.”

“You made everybody else a longcut.”

Daynah studied the eastern track. From the ridge they could see it climbing through a shallow cut before disappearing behind the next hill.

“Wide enough for the wagon. Rock under it. We stop them past that bend, they're not turning that bastard around without unhitching.”

Chris tapped the headgear.

“You could take the room.”

“I can see the room, Christopher.”

“Whole thing.”

“You want me to swallow someone else's dining room so you can eat in my dining room in their dining room?”

Chris considered that.

“Yeah.”

A pause.

“I do like the cabinet,” Billy admitted.

Kaze laughed.

Daynah rubbed a hand over his jaw.

“Have to get the people off first. And the team clear. Don't wrench the running gear through the floor trying to hurry it.”

“Listen to him,” Chris said. “Respect for workmanship.”

“Respect for the thing you're about to steal,” Arthur said.

“Somebody should have some.”

Kaze squatted beside Chris, close enough to be irritating on purpose.

“What if we just take the silver?”

Chris kept looking forward.

“We could.”

“Leave the big furniture.”

“We could.”

“Maybe buy a little folding stool someday. Start small.”

Chris turned.

“You have a sword living inside your fucking bones. Let me want a chair.”

Daynah made a pleased sound.

Arthur tipped his hat back.

“Got you there.”

Kaze's grin spread.

“Yeah. He did.”

He let Chris have it, which bothered Chris enough to make him check whether there was a second insult coming.

There wasn't.

Kaze looked down into the yard again.

Madam Vey was closing the music box. One of the guests finally left the bell alone. The porters began lowering the side panels for the night.

“All right,” Kaze said. “I want the whole fucking room too.”

Chris blinked.

“Really?”

“Yeah. I want to sit in there while they explain why I can't.”

Arthur stood.

“Then let's look at that bend before dark.”

No vote needed dragging out of anyone. Daynah got Mahnah moving gently. Chris took one last look at the folded canopy, then followed Arthur along the ridge. Billy began criticizing the tune Arthur had hummed, and Arthur refused to improve it on principle.

Kaze stayed behind for a moment.

The shelf where Daniel had vanished was hidden now by the shoulder of the hill. He looked that way anyway.

Then Chris called back.

“You coming?”

Kaze turned.

“Yeah.”

Ahead, Arthur and Daynah were already arguing about where the wagon could stop without breaking its axle. Chris was insisting the cabinet mattered. Mahnah nosed a loose stone off the path and watched it bounce away.

Kaze caught up.

By the time they reached the quarry bend, the sunlight had gone red. Arthur chose a position above the road. Daynah checked the footing below. The gang settled out of sight of the posting yard, with the morning's target chosen and none of it theirs yet.

Chris settled beside a stone with his hands behind his head, still looking back toward the hidden yard.

“That little bell,” he said.

“I'm taking that first.”
