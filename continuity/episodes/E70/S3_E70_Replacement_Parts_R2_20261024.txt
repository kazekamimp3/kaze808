Season 3, Episode 70 — Replacement Parts

“Get your foot off my arse.”

Daynah looked down.

Under his sandal lay a straight offcut, one end split by a nail. He moved his foot. There was nobody underneath it.

“Other foot.”

The voice came from the lane.

A wooden man stood at the top of the steps with a sack over his shoulder. He had an adult man's height, a narrow carved face and mismatched grain running through his limbs. One knee clicked whenever he shifted his weight.

Pinocchio was fully grown and apparently at the age when a man starts coming back for building materials he already sold.

Daynah lifted his other foot.

The wooden man limped over, picked up the curved plank beneath that foot and rubbed a muddy print off it.

“Look at that.”

“Looked,” Daynah said.

“That was good wood.”

Bob made a congested sound from beside the stone support.

Pinocchio turned on him.

“I sold you the old pieces for shelving.”

Bob glanced at the wrecked platform.

“Plans changed.”

“You put my backside in a floor.”

Richard Simmons sat on the surviving coffin base, holding a towel against his wounded side. He looked at the plank with sincere interest.

“At least people were going to use it.”

Pinocchio put it in his sack.

He went through the nearest pile, turning pieces over until he found a narrow strip with an old knot near one end.

“Shoulder.”

That went in the sack too.

He reached for a brace beneath Richard's seat.

Richard put a hand over it.

“I'm using that.”

“So did I.”

Daynah caught the brace before Pinocchio could wrench it free.

“Leave bottom alone.”

Pinocchio looked up at the green face above him.

“You break everybody else's things, but I can't take my own?”

“Take loose pieces. That holds him.”

Pinocchio let go, then pointed at the mud on his reclaimed plank.

“And that held you.”

Bob raised his good hand slightly.

“I paid for that lot.”

“For shelves.”

“You said it was seasoned.”

“It was my arse. I'd been sitting on it for years.”

Daynah made a low sound that might, under less irritating circumstances, have become a laugh.

Pinocchio heard it.

He pulled the sack closer and kept watching Daynah as he moved.

Daynah set the red block of compressed Kool-Aid on a dry ledge, well away from Richard's water bottle. The block remained still. His calf had stopped dripping, but the cut pulled as he stepped over the fallen rail.

Pinocchio moved across his path.

Click. Catch. Drag.

Daynah looked at the knee.

Then deliberately looked away.

Click. Catch.

“No,” Daynah said.

“I haven't asked you anything.”

“Good.”

Pinocchio planted himself in front of the wrecked coffin. His bad knee bent sideways a fraction before he caught it.

Daynah shut his eyes.

Here we are. A thirteen-foot gamma powerhouse, trying to defeat a loose fitting by not looking at it. I tried that with my face. Dermatologists still send Christmas cards to my burns.

“Your pin's walking out,” Daynah said.

Pinocchio glanced down.

“It's supposed to move.”

“Leg moves. Pin stays.”

“I've been walking on it.”

“Heard you.”

Pinocchio took another step to prove the point. The joint caught, tipped him forward and made him grab Daynah's wrist.

Daynah caught his upper arm before he fell.

He looked at the joint from the side. A pale chip was wedged between the two moving wooden faces; the projecting pin had nearly lost its far support.

“Hold still.”

“I didn't say you could—”

Daynah lowered him onto the edge of the stone footing. He held the leg straight, worked the chip loose with a broad thumbnail and pressed the pin back through its seat. Then he bent the knee once, watching the joint instead of Pinocchio's face.

It moved cleanly.

Pinocchio tested it himself.

His expression changed.

“There,” Daynah said. “Now walk.”

Pinocchio kicked him in the mouth.

The wooden heel hit hard enough to drive Daynah's head back. A cut inside his lip opened again. He stumbled into the broken steps, crushing one beneath his heel.

Pinocchio stood on the repaired leg.

“Works.”

“Fixed it,” Daynah said through the blood.

“Didn't ask you to put your hands on me.”

Daynah spat blood onto the dirt.

Bob looked down at his own injured hand, wisely withdrawing from the customer-service portion of the afternoon.

The second kick came higher.

Daynah caught it between both gauntlets.

Pinocchio twisted on his supporting foot, trying to drag the trapped limb across Daynah's guard. Daynah stepped with the turn, pulled the leg straight and drove his shoulder into the hip above it.

The socket split.

Wood cracked deep inside the pelvis. Pinocchio hit the ground with one leg still attached to him and the other held in Daynah's fist.

Daynah looked at the torn end.

“Pin stayed.”

Pinocchio swung his sack at the wounded calf.

The curved plank inside struck like a club. Daynah's knee buckled; he lowered one gauntlet to catch himself, and Pinocchio rolled beneath the returning fist.

The blow punched a hole through a fallen floor panel.

Pinocchio planted both hands, kicked with his remaining leg and drove himself backward across the debris. He came up seated against a broken exercise frame, sack clutched to his chest.

Daynah advanced with the torn leg.

“Wait,” Pinocchio said. “That's mine.”

Daynah hit him with it.

The first blow broke the arm he'd raised. The second drove his shoulder through the thin panel behind him. Pinocchio fell among the wrecked coffin walls, scattering loose fasteners.

Daynah came after him. Pinocchio grabbed the top of a fallen panel and hauled it upright between them. The torn leg struck its edge. Boards burst against Daynah's chest; he turned a gauntlet toward his eyes as the smaller fragments came up.

Pinocchio drove the panel's lower edge into the dirt and shoved.

It scraped beneath Daynah's planted sandal, lifting his heel. Daynah shifted before it could lever him backward, but the change put his weight onto the foot cut by the pitcher glass. Pain ran up the arch.

Pinocchio lunged at that ankle.

Daynah kicked the panel into him.

It slammed the wooden man flat. His remaining leg thrashed beneath it, knocking one corner high enough to expose his face.

Daynah stepped on the panel.

Pinocchio's chest creaked under the load. He twisted onto his side, slipping from beneath the narrow end while Daynah's weight drove the wide end into the ground. A long strip peeled from his ribs and stayed behind.

Daynah lifted his foot and broke the panel with one downward blow of Kahuhu.

The leg in his other hand had split along the shin. He changed his grip to the thicker thigh, keeping the solid knee and heel beyond his fist.

Pinocchio crawled out beside the coffin wreckage, looking back at the ruined leg.

“You're wearing it out.”

“Got another.”

Pinocchio drew his remaining knee beneath his chest.

His broken forearm hung from a strip of wood. He pulled it the rest of the way off.

Daynah reached down.

Pinocchio rammed the jagged forearm into the crease beneath his raised gauntlet.

Splinters punched into green skin. Daynah snarled and jerked back, but Pinocchio held on to the severed arm and dragged its broken edge sideways. Blood ran down the inside of the gauntlet.

Daynah stamped on the hand holding the splintered weapon.

The fingers cracked against the dirt. Pinocchio released the splintered weapon, rolled away and hooked a length of broken rail beneath his intact elbow.

He swung it low.

Daynah stepped over it and brought the stolen leg down across his shoulders.

Pinocchio's chest struck the ground. His head stayed turned toward the exercise frame. Two thick uprights still stood there, joined by a crosspiece above his nose.

He hooked the rail around the base of one upright and hauled himself underneath it.

Daynah followed, bent low to reach him.

Pinocchio tipped his face up. His nose pointed through the gap beside Daynah's neck, directly at the crosspiece behind him.

“Never touched you.”

The nose shot upward.

Daynah turned his face before the wooden point reached it. His left forearm caught the growing shaft and forced it away from his throat.

It struck the crosspiece and bored into it.

Pinocchio heaved his torso forward beneath the other end.

The anchored nose became a lever. It drove Daynah's forearm up under the crosspiece, trapping the gauntlet between them. The upright groaned. Daynah braced, feet sliding through red-stained grit.

Pinocchio grinned up at him from the dirt.

“Now put my leg down.”

Daynah still held it in his free hand.

He drove its broken hip end into Pinocchio's face.

The grin burst sideways. A wedge of cheek flew out. Pinocchio's jaw hung crooked, but he shoved harder against the nose, forcing Daynah's trapped arm higher.

Daynah's shoulder tightened. Blood squeezed from the splinter wounds.

Behind the frame, Bob tried to drag himself farther away from its leaning upright.

Daynah stopped pushing against the nose.

He looked at the crosspiece holding it.

“Bad choice.”

Pinocchio tried to answer. His loose jaw clicked twice.

Daynah struck the crosspiece beside the embedded nose with the heel of the torn leg. The first impact split the grain. The second tore the joint from the upright.

The lever lost its anchor.

Daynah's arm came down. Pinocchio lurched forward with it, suddenly pulling against nothing, and Daynah drove a knee through the middle of his chest.

The hollow body folded.

Daynah seized the intact shoulder and threw him across the work area, away from Bob. Pinocchio struck the collapsed coffin wall. His torso broke across its jagged edge; the long nose hit the ground and snapped near the face.

His head rolled twice and stopped beside his sack.

The remaining leg landed farther away.

For a moment there was only the sound of small pieces settling.

Then the head said, “You could've just left.”

Daynah pulled the broken fragment from beneath his gauntlet. Blood ran over his fingers.

He looked at it, then at the face still talking from the dirt.

“Put me back,” Pinocchio said.

Daynah dropped the torn leg across the split torso.

“No more fixing.”

He picked up the head by the back of its skull and set it between the broken shoulders. Pinocchio's eyes moved toward the detached leg lying across him.

“Not like that.”

Daynah stepped over the scattered body and raised both enormous fists.

His shoulders bunched. Green muscle tightened across his back; the dark coils above his brow shook as he drew breath.

“daynah SMASH!”

Kahuhu came down together.

The head burst between the gauntlets. The blow drove through the chest, the loose leg and the wrecked coffin wall beneath them. Earth jumped around Daynah's feet. Wood splinters sprayed across the pavilion and hammered the remaining uprights.

Pinocchio's voice stopped inside the impact.

Daynah lifted his fists from the crater. Carved features, joint faces, broken ribs—all of it was pulverized into the dirt. The wooden man was dead.

Bob dropped his tape.

Daynah turned toward him.

The builder scrambled backward from the stone support, injured hand clamped against his chest. His good hand found the hammer lying beside the wrecked bench.

“Wait.”

Daynah caught him by the front of his shirt.

Bob swung. The hammer struck Kahuhu and bounced. Daynah tore it from his hand, flung it into the rubble and lifted him clear of the ground.

The little man's boots kicked against Daynah's wrist.

Daynah smashed him down against the stone footing.

Bob's back broke over its edge. Daynah dragged him onto the flat top and drove a gauntlet through his head. Blood struck the support behind him. His boots stopped kicking.

Richard was already trying to get up from the coffin base.

His bad knee folded. He caught the edge, shoved himself upright with his good leg and staggered toward the open side of the pavilion.

Daynah reached him before the lane.

Richard turned into the grab, catching Daynah's wrist with both hands. His good foot drove into the wounded shin. Daynah's weight shifted; Richard used the opening to pull free and threw himself toward the steps.

Daynah caught the back of his shirt.

The cloth tore across one shoulder. Richard landed on his hands, tried to crawl, and Daynah hauled him back by the ankle.

“Stop!”

Daynah swung him across the empty work area.

Richard hit the ground on his back. The impact emptied his lungs. He tried to roll, still fighting for a breath, as the shadow covered him.

Daynah's fist drove through his chest into the earth beneath.

The ground cracked outward. Richard's body jerked once and lay still. Daynah withdrew the gauntlet; the crushed chest did not rise again.

He stood between the two bodies, breathing hard. Blood dripped from Kahuhu onto the red stains already drying in the dirt.

The water bottle remained at the far end of the footing.

Daynah looked past it to the dry ledge.

He picked up the compressed red block and brought it down under his fist against the stone. It split. He ground the fragments beneath the gauntlet, then gathered the red grit in his cupped hands.

A tight flare of ki burned white between his palms.

When he opened them, the last red grains were gone. The Kool-Aid Man was dead, his surviving substance destroyed. The broken pitcher remained where it had fallen.

Daynah shook dust from his hands.

Nobody offered another correction.

The reinforced coffin base still lay flat beside the footing. He'd preserved it through every stupid thing that afternoon had thrown at him. Now there was nobody sitting on it.

Daynah lowered himself onto the broad stone beyond Bob's body. He pulled the remaining splinters from his arm and pressed a fold of his waist cloth against the bleeding cuts. His jaw hurt. His shoulder hurt. The wound under his foot tightened when he took his weight off it.

His body kept working on the damage.

Around him, nothing got back up.

Down at the ropewalk apron, Chris had found something worth smiling at.

It came on a handcart.

The carton was almost as tall as he was, with a bright Eyeholes logo stretched across its front. A letter and a small black device were tucked beneath the top strap. The carter eased the load down beside the parked Subaru and pulled his cart free.

Chris walked around the box.

“Oh, look at you.”

The shot mirror still hung crooked from the car. Chris ignored it. This was an entirely different category of damaged judgment.

“Somebody finally understands portions.”

Arthur stood near the ropewalk wall. He looked from the cereal to Chris.

“You order that?”

“No.”

Chris smiled wider.

The carter pointed to the address printed on the side, waited until Chris looked at it, then took his empty cart back toward the lane. No payment was requested.

Chris's smile stopped moving.

He leaned closer.

“Belly of the Billy.”

Billy's voice came through the headgear at Chris's temple. Inside, he'd been watching the delivery on the live screen.

“That'll be mine.”

“You have an address?”

“Evidently, it's legible.”

Chris looked at the empty apron around his boots.

Then back at the label.

“I've been sleeping in a man with mail.”

“You've been living here, Christopher. Sleeping's only the portion you do quietly.”

Chris slid the letter out from under the strap. The device came with it, a palm-sized black rectangle with a short cord and a tiny dark display. It gave one sharp beep when he lifted it.

He turned it over. No explanation on the back.

“That better not be a fucking doorbell.”

He tore the envelope open.

The first sentence took the pleasure out of his face.

Arthur noticed.

“What is it?”

Chris read it again without answering.

Then he lowered the page.

“Billy. Bring the box in.”

“Gladly.”

Chris stepped clear of the Subaru, keeping the letter and device in his hand. His jaw opened; the continuous roll of flesh brought Billy outside and carried Chris, full-sized, into the familiar living space within.

Billy stood beside the carton in his hat, banjo against his body.

Arthur looked up at him.

“Trouble?”

“Whatever it is, he's keeping the cereal.”

Billy opened his enormous mouth and swallowed the carton whole. It passed down intact, routed into the dry living area where Chris waited. The straps settled against the flesh floor. Nothing spilled into the acid beyond the raised rooms.

Outside, Billy licked a grain of cardboard dust from his lip.

“Very pretty box.”

Inside, Chris stood looking at the letter.

Meatwad rolled closer from the lounge.

“You get a present?”

“I don't know yet.”

Chris carried the page and device to the gaming station. The second monitor showed Arthur from Billy's height, with the depot wall behind him. The first screen remained untouched.

He read aloud.

“We have received information that you are the murderer of the Eyehole Man.”

Meatwad stopped beside the chair.

Chris stared at the printed words.

“Well, that's a hell of a way to say thank you.”

He read on.

“This is not a warrant or a threat. It is a recruitment.”

He lowered the sheet.

“You can't put a dead man in the first sentence and make the second sentence a fucking party hat.”

Meatwad looked at the enormous carton.

“Might be a good party.”

“Since you are the murderer of the Eyehole Man, you are legally required to fill his role. Refer to the fine print if you are confused about this situation.”

Chris turned the page over.

Blank.

He turned it back.

“I'm confused where they got my address.”

Through the exterior channel, he called to Billy.

“Did you give these people my name?”

Billy's reply arrived from outside through Monitor Two.

“I've given no cereal manufacturer your particulars.”

“They know about him.”

Outside, Billy's voice lost some of its amusement.

“How much?”

Chris looked down the page.

At the bottom, his full name waited in clean black type.

“All three fucking names.”

“And a fine-print claim that I'm supposed to take his job. I'm checking that next.”

He sat.

There it was. Not a man yelling across a road. Not another drunk threatening to recognize him. Somebody who wasn't standing here knew exactly whom to send breakfast to.

Chris lifted the little device between finger and thumb.

“He have a hotline? You kill him and a lady in another universe goes, oh, Christopher's available now?”

He held it away from his face.

“Are you listening?”

The device did nothing.

Chris watched it a little longer before laying it on the desk, screen down.

Meatwad had gone back to the carton. He pushed against its lower corner. It didn't move.

“I can't get in.”

Chris stood, took a knife from his workbench and cut the packing straps. He opened the front seam without damaging the smaller cereal boxes stacked tightly inside, then set the knife back beyond Meatwad's reach.

Rows of Eyeholes filled the carton.

Chris took one out and turned it in his hands. The familiar colors. The familiar smiling prick.

He looked into the carton again.

“That's a lot.”

It came out quietly, almost reverently.

Meatwad squeezed past his boot.

“I like your problem.”

Chris set the small box on the desk and resumed reading.

“With great power comes great responsibility.”

Oh, fuck off. I know that sentence. You don't get to wheel in six feet of sugar and borrow the dead uncle's letterhead.

“Any person who opens a box of your Eyeholes cereal brand, which you are now sponsored by upon opening this message, shall be beaten. You are to demand your Eyeholes and tell them to give back your Eyeholes.”

Chris went back to the middle.

“Upon opening.”

He looked at the ripped envelope.

“I opened it because it had my fucking name on it.”

He pushed it away, then pulled the letter back to finish.

“Death is not mandatory. The minimum requirement is to beat the shit out of them.”

On the floor, Meatwad slid a little box out of the bottom row.

“Do they give you a shirt?”

“Doesn't say.”

“You oughta get a shirt.”

Chris read the final paragraph.

“You will be paid a lifetime supply of Eyeholes cereal. That is the large box to which this letter was attached. Thank you for understanding and unknowingly volunteering to work with us, Christopher Grey Graham.”

He set it down.

No one spoke for a moment.

Then he addressed the exterior channel.

“Arthur. They want me doing what that asshole did.”

On Monitor Two, Arthur lifted his eyes toward Billy's banjo, where Chris's voice was coming from.

“What're they paying?”

Chris glanced at the carton.

“The cereal.”

Arthur considered that.

“How much you eat before you're working for nothing?”

Chris looked at the little box already on his desk.

Then at Meatwad's.

“Stop helping yourself to my wages.”

Meatwad hugged the box closer.

“I thought it was breakfast.”

“It was, like, thirty seconds ago.”

Arthur's mouth pulled slightly to one side. He didn't need to explain the question again.

Chris picked up the letter, read the fine-print direction once more and headed for his existing stash.

“No. I bought cereal. I didn't join a fucking gang.”

He dug out an older Eyeholes box and carried it beneath the lounge light. It had the creased corner and folded top of something he'd actually been eating from.

He read the front, the back, then the little print running along the bottom edge.

His thumbnail stopped beneath a line.

“Killing the current representative constitutes notice of availability.”

Chris brought the box closer.

Read it again.

“That was on there?”

Meatwad peered up.

“It is now.”

“I can see it now. I mean before.”

He rubbed the printing with his thumb. Nothing came off.

Outside, Billy waited beside Arthur, his voice returning through the live feed.

“Did you find the provision?”

“I found a job hiding under the ingredients.”

Chris put the old box beside the letter. He aligned the bottom edge with the new company's paragraph, as if putting them next to each other might make one admit it was taking the piss.

Neither did.

“He nearly killed me over a bowl.”

Outside, Billy stopped shifting his weight. The picture on Chris's screen steadied.

“I remember.”

Chris pressed a thumb into the old cardboard crease.

Then he looked at the new supply.

“Lifetime,” he said. “That's what they think?”

He went to the carton and counted the boxes across the first row, then looked at the depth behind them.

“They either don't expect much out of me, or they expect a fucking lot out of the next guy.”

He stopped counting.

Meatwad opened his box.

The black device shrieked against the desk.

Chris spun around.

Meatwad froze with the cardboard flaps spread beside his face.

The beeps came faster. Chris picked the device up and turned it over. A small arrow had appeared on its display.

He turned his wrist. The arrow shifted.

He moved it toward Meatwad.

The beeping sharpened.

Meatwad looked from the box to Chris's face.

“I ain't doing it again. It's already open.”

Chris stared at him.

Then at the letter.

Then at the flesh floor beneath them, the couch, his two screens, the familiar room where the little bastard kept turning up to ask questions.

He set the device down.

“No.”

Meatwad pulled one cereal piece from the open bag.

“No what?”

“Not in here.”

Chris spoke through the banjo channel.

“Billy. Inside's different. Anybody eating it in here gets left alone.”

Billy's answer came promptly.

“You're damn right they do.”

“I'm saying that.”

“Then I'm pleased with what you're saying.”

Chris looked back at Meatwad.

“Eat it.”

Meatwad did.

The device kept beeping.

Chris picked it up and pressed the raised button on its side. The sound stopped. The arrow remained.

“You can point all fucking day.”

He laid it beside the letter.

Meatwad chewed slowly, watching him.

“You ain't gonna hit me?”

“No.”

“Even if I get another?”

“You're already holding a whole box.”

“For later.”

“Finish that one.”

Meatwad rolled nearer the carton and rested against it, satisfied with the practical shape of his freedom.

Chris returned the older box to his stash. He kept the letter on the desk.

He could have shoved it away with the envelope. Instead he smoothed the page, weighed one corner beneath the silent device and read the demand again.

Your Eyeholes.

He glanced at the fresh rows.

His shoulders settled a little.

Through the outward channel, he admitted to Billy,

“They are giving me quite a bit.”

On the exterior screen, Arthur looked up again.

Billy gave a warm, dangerous chuckle.

“There he is.”

“What?”

“Nothing, Christopher.”

Chris looked down at his bandoliers. He moved the short cord aside and tried the device at his belt, testing whether it sat where his hand could reach without fouling a holster.

It did.

He left it there.

Meatwad held his open box upward.

“You want some?”

Chris looked at it.

“Give me back my Eyeholes.”

Meatwad lowered the box.

“You said I could have 'em.”

Chris stood there for a second, wearing his new equipment.

Then he held out his hand.

“Pass the fucking cereal.”

Meatwad tipped some into his palm.

“That's better.”
