Episode 63 — Force-Fed

The engine came apart before Brian reached the gate.

Four bolts tore out of its mounting plate. The heavy casing rose from the sand, turned in the air, and split along a rusted seam. Beneath it sat a pump, two cracked cylinders, and absolutely fuck-all that could carry anybody to the stars.

Starkiller stood in the narrow shade of a salvage shed, one armored hand extended.

He lowered the wreck onto its supports. Metal groaned. A little rain of rust fell through the frame.

Then he took the bent release handle off the casing. For a moment he held it so tightly that his glove creaked.

“Not back there.”

Quiet. Barely louder than the wind under the roof.

He dropped the handle onto the workbench.

I don't know where there was. I do know a man looking at a pump like it just personally renewed his fucking employment contract.

Beyond the shed, the northern Shuriman yard opened between heaps of dismantled machinery. A broad gate faced the caravan track; low dunes rose across it to the east. Behind the shed, scrap climbed almost to the roof. Nothing resembling a runway. Nothing resembling a ship.

Brian arrived through the gate with sand whitening his shoes and Clifford's stolen lightsaber wrapped against his side.

He had crossed the water, then followed the caravan road. Days, not a heroic little dissolve between adjacent fucking countries. Repairing the coaster's loudhailer had bought his passage. Work along the road had bought provisions. The black-armored traveler asking about unfamiliar engines had left an easier trail than Brian's supposed music career.

Now he'd found him.

Brian put his bag beside the fence, adjusted the journal under his arm, and stepped into the shade.

“Lord Starkiller.”

The armored head turned.

Brian unwrapped the hilt.

“I believe this establishes a certain level of practical—”

The red blade snapped out.

Starkiller moved before Brian finished raising it. One step took him outside its line. His hand closed around Brian's wrist, pointing the weapon into empty sand; the other hand caught the journal before it fell.

Brian stopped breathing.

“Off.”

The blade vanished.

Starkiller released him and held out the book.

Brian took it with both hands.

“Demonstration,” he said. “We were both demonstrating.”

Starkiller looked at the hilt again.

“Do you know how to use it?”

“I've used it.”

That was true. Shake was dead. It just wasn't an answer to the question.

“Then you should know better.”

He turned back toward the engine.

“Chris has an X-wing.”

Starkiller stopped.

Brian saw it. The stillness. The complete disappearance of the engine from the conversation.

“Who is Chris?”

“One of the Seven Sins. Personal acquaintance. Complicated working history, but the information itself is excellent.”

“Where?”

“First, it isn't sitting in a hangar. It changes into a blue car. Gold wheels. Subaru. He keeps the same vehicle in a little ball.”

Starkiller faced him.

“You saw this?”

“Yes. I'm not selling you something somebody's cousin saw blinking over a field.”

“Can it get off this world?”

Brian opened his mouth, then glanced at the torn engine.

“I can tell you what it is and who controls it. I can't promise you a destination I haven't seen it reach.”

That answer got a longer look than the sales pitch.

“And you know where to find him.”

“Where he was. Where they said they were going. Somebody who dealt with them. An actual place to start.”

“Why come to me?”

“Because I'm looking for somebody who recognizes potential.”

“Why leave them?”

Brian adjusted the cloth around the hilt, covering a bit of exposed metal.

“Different priorities. They had a very narrow idea of what I was useful for.”

“And what was that?”

“Technical work. Information. Things they needed.”

Starkiller glanced at the engine, then back at him.

Brian caught the glance.

“I understand the irony. I'm choosing the arrangement this time.”

He took the broken pencil out of his journal and sketched the vehicle's two configurations on a loose page. The car came out squat. The spread wings were clearer. He put an arrow between them, then crossed out a second arrow that had made it look like there were two vehicles.

“Same one. And Chris is the person you need. If you scare him into putting it away before you even talk, you've made your own problem.”

Starkiller took the sketch. Brian let him.

“You're offering an introduction?”

“Information. I'm not selling you another man's yes.”

For all the shit he was about to demand, that part he could actually deliver.

Starkiller stepped away from the bench.

Brian held his ground, although one shoe dragged backward before the rest of him voted.

“I need something too.”

“Payment?”

“Development.”

Oh, Jesus. There are people you should never say development to. A landlord. A Hollywood executive. A tiny man carrying handwritten lyrics toward someone who can crush his windpipe from across a room.

“I want you to hear my work,” Brian said. “Actually listen. Give me feedback. And show me something I can learn.”

“You want training.”

“Vader trained you. I'm not asking you to recreate every aspect of that relationship. Obviously some of the management was—”

“Don't.”

The word stopped him cleanly.

Brian swallowed.

“One lesson. A genuine one. Not wave your hand over a rock and tell me to come back spiritually improved.”

Starkiller looked toward the gate. The open track continued north, empty except for old wheel ruts. Then he looked back at the useless machinery.

“One performance. One lesson. You answer my questions afterward.”

“And feedback.”

“I heard you.”

“That's not always the same as listening.”

Starkiller held out his hand for the journal.

Brian hugged it tighter.

“I perform it.”

“How long?”

Brian opened the book to the lyrics.

“The explanations are included.”

That should have been on the fucking gate. Not BEWARE OF DOG. THE EXPLANATIONS ARE INCLUDED.

He set his bag where he could reach it and drank a small mouthful of water. Then he climbed onto a low block beside the shed, tested its stability with his foot, and stood with the journal open.

From there he could see the whole of Starkiller's mask without leaning backward.

“Better projection,” Brian said.

Starkiller remained in the shade.

“Begin.”

Brian tapped four beats against the cover. Came in after the fourth, caught himself, and decided the hesitation belonged to the introduction.

Back on the coast, when Brian had come up in the Sins' conversation, Kaze's assessment had been two words.

“Simp lame.”

Brian had heard none of it. He was already preparing his defense.

Brian held the last pose with one hand extended.

A strip of loose canvas knocked against the shed.

He lowered the hand slightly. Not all the way. There was still time for a reaction to arrive and make the pose justified.

Starkiller's attention had not left him.

“Well?”

“The wordplay was good.”

Brian's face opened.

“Which part?”

“Head. Hip. Waist. You keep changing what the body means.”

“Exactly. Thank you. Because most people get stuck on whether I was literally—”

“You explain that.”

“For accessibility.”

Starkiller looked at the page.

“You don't let them miss it.”

Brian smiled with the wet-eyed relief of a man being thanked for holding the bathroom door open while somebody was still taking a shit.

“That is the first constructive observation anybody has made about that section.”

“It is difficult to misunderstand your intention.”

Listen to him. Carefully lifting each sentence around the truth like it's a sleeping grenade.

Brian stepped down from the block.

“The wrist line?”

“You connect two references.”

“And the rhyme.”

Starkiller waited.

Brian held up a finger, preparing to demonstrate.

“Like this,” Starkiller said. “Five wrists.”

The finger became a delighted point.

“There. You heard it.”

“Yes.”

“People act like I just attached a random body part to a sentence. It's sound. It's also imagery. If you've got five wrists in your neck—”

Starkiller raised his hand.

Brian stopped, watching the fingers.

They remained still.

“I understood the line.”

“Right. Good.”

Brian lowered his own hand. “Didn't want you leaving with the wrong neck.”

He studied the black mask, searching it for an expression it wasn't going to provide.

“But you enjoyed it.”

“The connections were inventive.”

Brian leaned forward.

“And making you Vader. You see what I did there?”

The mask tilted down toward him.

“You ask to become an apprentice. Then tell the teacher who he has to become.”

Brian paused.

“Exactly.”

“You make your request his obligation.”

“That's the deeper layer. People just hear Vade-da and think I bent a name to get to vanilla. Which I did. But it's also that.”

He tapped the page with his pencil, quite pleased to discover he'd written another thing.

Starkiller's hands remained at his sides.

“The obligation ends with our agreement.”

Brian's tapping stopped.

“Right. We're talking about the song.”

“I'm talking about both.”

That landed. Brian looked away first, down at the margin where there was room to write something that made the exchange sound better.

“You can say if something didn't work.”

“You interrupt your own momentum.”

Brian's smile stiffened.

“Deliberately.”

“Then let the listener reach the connection before you explain it.”

“They might not.”

“You asked.”

Brian looked down at the lyrics.

For a second he just read them.

Then he wrote a note in the margin: Allow audience to catch up.

“That's useful,” he said.

It was. He'd still managed to put the problem on the audience, but he'd written the fucking note.

Starkiller pointed to the ground.

“Put the book down.”

Brian's fingers tightened around it.

“For what?”

“Your lesson.”

“Now?”

“You wanted a real one.”

Brian carefully laid the journal on his bag. He checked that the open page wasn't touching sand, shifted the water container away from it, then faced Starkiller with his feet spread.

“I'm ready.”

Starkiller put a plain metal washer on the block.

“Don't reach for it with your hand.”

“I know that part.”

“Then stop doing it.”

Brian lowered his arm.

He stared until his eyes watered. The washer stayed flat. A fly landed beside it and left, apparently satisfied with its own training.

“You're waiting for me to get embarrassed,” Brian said.

Starkiller crouched, bringing the mask closer to his level.

“You're waiting to look powerful. Pay attention to what is here.”

He tapped the block once.

“Feel where you end. Where that begins. Don't invent a movement and call it one.”

Brian's jaw worked. Then he stopped posing with his shoulders.

The washer didn't move.

But his breathing slowed. His gaze loosened. When Starkiller set the bent release handle beside it, Brian reached to steady himself and touched the metal.

The yard dropped away.

A harsh scrape. Four sharp cracks crowded into one sound. Pressure closed around his hand although nobody was touching him.

Not back there.

Brian jerked away from the block.

The canvas snapped against the shed. The wind returned. His own breath sounded horribly close.

“What the fuck was that?”

Starkiller was already standing.

“What did you hear?”

Brian stared at the handle.

“Something breaking. Then you. You said not back there.”

Starkiller's head turned toward the gate Brian had entered.

“Where were you when I opened the engine?”

“Coming through the yard. I saw it coming down.”

“Before that?”

“Outside. I wasn't hiding. I don't need to stalk people for production material.”

“Touch it again.”

Brian reached more cautiously. This time there was only a thin, sick pressure, like the moment before a door locked. No words. He let go.

“It's not playing the same thing.”

“It isn't a recording.”

Starkiller picked up the handle himself. Waited. Put it down.

“Some people can sense what an object carries from its past. An impression. An emotion.”

“You taught me that?”

“No.”

The answer was immediate.

Brian looked up.

“The exercise didn't put it in you.”

“So I already had it.”

“You made contact once. That is what you know.”

Brian glanced at his journal.

“Force sampling.”

“Psychometry.”

“That's the technical name. I'm thinking about application.”

“Think about the difference between what you sensed and what you guessed.”

Starkiller moved the handle to the far end of the bench, beyond Brian's casual reach.

“And ask before touching my equipment.”

Brian watched him do it.

For once, the little bastard didn't rush to fill the silence. He flexed his empty hand. Looked at his fingers as if he'd discovered somebody had quietly installed another door in his house.

Then he reached for the stolen saber.

“What about something practical?”

Starkiller looked at him.

“That was practical.”

“Combat practical. Because the past has already happened. I'm mostly being threatened in the present.”

Starkiller took him out from under the roof, into the clear space between the block and the open gate. He made Brian put the journal and bag behind the block, away from the blades.

Then an unstable black edge tore into being in his hand.

Its ragged light crawled over the dust. Brian's red blade rose to meet it.

“Hold your guard.”

Brian braced with both hands.

The black saber touched his blade, pressed, and slid it outward. Brian's elbows followed. His chest opened.

Starkiller stopped his edge short of Brian's shoulder.

“You followed the weapon.”

“It's a very noticeable weapon.”

“Watch the person holding it.”

Starkiller reset the contact. This time, as Brian leaned into the pressure, the black blade vanished.

Brian's arms lurched into the empty space.

Starkiller stepped around his outside shoulder. The blade ignited beyond Brian's guard, slanting away from his body, and sliced through a discarded upright metal strip behind him.

The upper piece fell with a bright clang.

Brian turned late. His blade was still defending somewhere Starkiller no longer was.

“Trah-kah-tah,” Starkiller said.

Brian looked from the severed strip to the control on his hilt.

“Turn it off.”

“At the right moment. Move. Then turn it on where the guard isn't.”

“That's fucking cheating.”

Starkiller said nothing.

Brian's expression changed.

“I mean, from their perspective.”

He wanted to try it against Starkiller immediately. Starkiller instead laid the fallen strip across two pieces of scrap, with open air beneath its middle.

“Pass under. Bring the blade up on the other side. Keep it away from your legs.”

Brian did. The first attempt was clumsy: he held the dead hilt too far out, had to retrieve his balance, and stopped before reigniting.

On the second, he shortened the movement. Red light flashed beneath the strip, then rose behind it. He turned his wrists and cut through the end.

A hot piece dropped into the sand.

He'd done it.

Brian stared, then let out one sharp laugh.

“Oh, that's nasty.”

“It leaves you unprotected while the blade is off.”

“Unless they don't know I'm going to—”

Starkiller's blade met his.

Brian switched his off.

Before he could bring it back, Starkiller stepped past the hilt and struck his wrist with the heel of his free hand. The stolen saber flew into the sand. Brian stumbled against the block.

The black blade went out.

“They can move too.”

Brian held his wrist. It hurt. Nothing had broken.

He looked at the cut scrap, then at the place where Starkiller had been standing.

“So the timing's the part.”

“And the distance.”

Brian nodded. A small, ordinary nod. Then he retrieved Clifford's weapon, checked the control, and put it away without turning it on.

He gave the severed scrap one more look. The grin came back before he could arrange it for anybody else.

Starkiller returned to the shade.

“The location.”

Brian picked up his journal and found the lyric page. The torn corner of Shake's note showed between the pages behind it.

He pushed it farther into the book.

“Could you sign?”

Starkiller stared at the pencil.

“Under the lyrics. With what you said about the wordplay. Then I'll give you the route. No more additions.”

The pencil looked very small when Starkiller took it.

Brian turned the book toward him, balancing its lower edge on the block. He had to move his thumb twice to keep it out of the writing space.

Starkiller wrote beneath the last line.

To B. Moo.

I loved the wordplay.

Then the signature.

Brian read it without speaking.

His thumb stroked the blank margin beside the words. Carefully. He didn't touch the writing itself.

“Thank you.”

No branding attached. Just those two words.

Starkiller returned the pencil.

Brian turned to a blank page and drew the inlet, the outer berth, and the road beside the ropewalk. His little hand became steady as he worked.

“Northern landing. The crossing brings you back to this coast. Export depot here. Their camp was outside it when I left. They'd been selling cargo.”

“How long ago?”

Brian accounted for the crossing and the road. He didn't call the information current. He gave the time he'd actually been away.

“Arthur said inland afterward. That's intention. I wasn't there when they left, if they left.”

He marked the blue-awning buyer's place.

“This person dealt with them. Can identify the group. Start here if the camp's gone. Don't go looking for a parked fighter.”

“The ball stays with Chris?”

“It's his car. His ship. Same thing. He's not going to hear X-wing and suddenly discover he's renting it to you.”

Starkiller studied the drawing.

“And Billy?”

“You'll recognize them. I wouldn't start by telling either one what he belongs to.”

“I asked how to find them.”

“I'm giving you professional context. Free.”

Starkiller asked about the approach from the landing and the visible landmark at the inlet. Brian answered both, correcting one line in his drawing when the first explanation put the road on the wrong side of the berth.

He tore out the map and handed it over.

Starkiller compared it once more with the route Brian had described, folded it, and put it away.

Brian tucked the journal into his bag, then changed his mind and kept it against his chest. He slung the bag over his shoulder. The water container knocked against his hip.

“Obviously I'll need to be present for the introduction.”

“No.”

“Not as a condition. As your apprentice.”

“You aren't.”

Brian laughed softly, inviting him to admit the bit.

Starkiller didn't.

The little smile remained on Brian's face a moment after the rest of him understood.

“You identified unusual potential.”

“I gave you the lesson.”

“And there's an ongoing application. You don't find something like that and just—”

Starkiller walked toward the gate.

Brian hurried after him, cutting across his path with the signed page already opening.

“Wait. I need to know how you want me describing our relationship. Because if people ask who developed my—”

Starkiller turned.

His hand opened.

The Force struck Brian across the whole front of his body.

His heels left the ground. His breath punched out. The gateposts flashed past on either side as dust ripped off the track beneath him.

The journal snapped open.

Brian caught it against his chest.

He hit the dune shoulder-first, drove a trough through loose sand, and rolled onto the stone buried beneath its slope. The water container burst against it.

A dark splash spread under his hip.

For several seconds he couldn't pull air back in.

He lay there, mouth working, clutching the book. Sand slid down the bank and collected in the fold of his neck.

Across the yard, Starkiller lowered his arm.

He stepped through the gate and took the northern track. The folded directions stayed with him.

Brian's next breath arrived as a thin, ugly squeak.

Then another.

He rolled off the broken container, coughing.

The journal had stayed dry.

He checked the signature first.

Of course he checked the fucking signature first.

I loved the wordplay.

Still there. So was the note about letting the audience catch up. So was the thin corner of Shake's encouragement, trapped farther back where Brian had pushed it.

Brian closed the book.

Water was soaking into the sand beside him. He tried to cup it, lifted the split container, pressed the seam closed. A last dribble ran over his knuckles.

His wrist hurt. His shoulder hurt. The hilt was still secured at his side. He tested his feet, got one knee under him, and stood with sand pouring out of his clothes.

He looked toward the departing black figure.

His mouth opened.

Nothing came out.

The caravan ruts curved around the dune toward the road he'd used that morning. He could follow those back toward the last stop. The shorter path crossed in front of Starkiller.

Brian stood considering it, the empty container hanging from his fingers.

Then he turned toward the longer route, keeping the dune between himself and the gate.

Brian fastened the journal inside his bag, pulled the strap tight, and started walking.
