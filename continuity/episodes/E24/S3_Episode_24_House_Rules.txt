Season 3, Episode 24: House Rules

The music reached the road before the house came into view.

Briar stopped chewing long enough to listen. Somewhere beyond the orchard wall, a fiddle was racing a drum and losing badly.

“What's that?”

Kaze tossed his empty skewer into the roadside ditch.

“Somebody having a better afternoon than us.”

“We had a good afternoon.”

“Then somebody showing off.”

They followed the wall uphill. The river crossing disappeared behind the apple trees, leaving them on a carriage road with clipped hedges and gravel so white I assumed somebody poor had been made to wash it.

A house stood above the bend, broad and pale, with a glass-roofed hall running along its southern side. Carriages crowded the front court. Through the open doors came music, laughter, and the smell of roast meat.

Briar finished the last bite on her skewer.

“I want to go in.”

“Me too.”

“You don't even know what's happening.”

“I like discovering things with my hands.”

She gave him a sideways look.

“I noticed.”

A footman intercepted them at the steps. His eyes moved from Kaze's bare chest to the torn knee of his trousers, then to the great pillory around Briar's neck and wrists.

“Private reception.”

“Looks friendly,” Kaze said.

“Your names?”

“Kaze. Briar.”

The footman checked a sheet he plainly didn't need to check.

“You aren't expected.”

“That's usually when I'm best.”

“Then we must make an exception,” said a woman behind him.

She stood in the doorway with one hand resting against the frame. Dark hair rose beneath a delicate black ornament. Her gown was a deep, nearly black red, and she had the sort of stillness that made everybody nearby adjust themselves first.

The footman stepped aside at once.

She looked at Briar's restraint without pretending not to, then gave Kaze her attention.

“You seem determined to enjoy yourselves.”

“Depends how much trouble you're offering.”

“I haven't offered anything yet.”

“I'm trying to get you into bed. Figured I'd save us the polite misunderstanding.”

Her smile widened slightly.

“And here I was, prepared to offer wine.”

“We can work up from there.”

“Elise,” she said.

I knew the name. A Noxian noblewoman with beautiful manners, useful connections, and appetites that had long outlived an ordinary human span. When she went looking for company, the people she found didn't always get to choose when the visit ended.

Kaze had none of that history. He had the woman in the doorway, the servants watching for her signals, and a tiny black spider crawling undisturbed over her thumb.

He noticed the spider.

Elise noticed him noticing.

“Does that trouble you?”

“You keep pets on you?”

“Occasionally.”

“I charge extra for that.”

Briar leaned toward the little spider. It retreated beneath Elise's cuff.

“I think it likes her better,” Briar said.

“Early days,” Kaze replied.

Elise invited them inside.

The reception occupied the long glass hall and a terrace overlooking the river. Guests clustered around tables of fruit, cold meats, and little pastries too small to defend their own price. At the far end, musicians played beneath climbing vines. Beyond them stood a door into the older stone part of the house.

Elise introduced neither stranger to the room. She simply walked between them, and people made space.

That told me more than any recital of her titles would have.

“Are you travelling together?” she asked.

Briar answered first.

“Yes.”

“For long?”

“Since yesterday.”

Elise glanced at Kaze.

“You move quickly.”

“You should see him jump,” Briar said.

Kaze grinned.

“She's got references.”

“I prefer to form my own opinions.”

“Now we're getting somewhere.”

Elise handed him a cup of wine from a passing tray. She offered another to Briar, who smelled it, frowned, and set it on a table.

“I want that instead.”

She pointed at the roast.

“Of course.”

Elise signalled a servant. A plate appeared. Briar took it without thanking the signal.

Across the room, a heavy man in green velvet watched her with increasing fascination.

“A remarkable piece,” he said, approaching. “Where did you acquire it?”

Briar looked down at the slice of meat.

“There.”

“The restraint, my dear.”

“It came with me.”

He laughed as though she'd performed something for him.

“I collect military curiosities. Old chains, campaign weapons, execution apparatus. That design is extraordinary.”

“Does your house have windows?” Kaze asked.

The man blinked.

“Naturally.”

“Open one sometime.”

Briar laughed around a mouthful of food.

Elise touched the man's sleeve.

“Master Venn has an interest in unusual craftsmanship.”

“Master Venn has an interest in my neck,” Briar said. “He can ask me.”

The collector reddened.

“I meant no offence.”

“Then you'll be pleased you have an opportunity to stop,” Elise said smoothly.

She guided them away before he could improve matters by speaking again.

Kaze leaned toward her.

“You always this good at moving men around?”

“Only when they make it easy.”

“Try me without the wine.”

“You've scarcely touched it.”

He set the cup down.

“There. Now we can blame my decisions on me.”

Elise laughed, low and unhurried. She was good at making a laugh feel selected for one person. I didn't mistake that for affection, and I wasn't yet sure Kaze did either.

She led them to a table overlooking the terrace.

“Several of my guests will be joining me on a voyage,” she said. “I occasionally find people who want something more than the lives they've inherited.”

“Better food?” Briar asked.

“A beginning.”

“Food's a beginning.”

“What about you, Kaze?”

“Depends where we're going.”

“Across the sea.”

“Big place.”

“I like a man who asks questions.”

“You haven't answered one.”

She regarded him for a moment, then folded her hands on the table.

“There are powers in this world that don't trouble themselves with the ambitions of little men. I've stood before one. It changed what was possible for me.”

“And what'd it want?”

“Everything worthwhile has a price.”

Kaze looked around the room.

“These people buying something, or are they the price?”

Elise's expression barely changed. Her thumb stopped moving against the back of her hand.

Briar watched that small stillness. Then she put down her plate.

“I want to dance.”

Kaze turned to her.

“You know how?”

“No. Do you?”

“Enough to lie about it.”

She stood.

“Show me.”

Elise remained seated while Kaze followed Briar toward the musicians.

The dancers had arranged themselves into pairs that seemed determined to avoid enjoying the music. Briar stepped among them, studied their careful little turns, and frowned.

“Why are they all doing the same thing?”

“So they can tell who's wrong.”

“You?”

“Give me a second.”

He took one of her hands where it extended beyond the pillory, put his other hand at her waist, and drew her into a turn. She followed the first two steps and ignored the third. The heavy restraint swung close enough to another dancer's shoulder to make him retreat.

Kaze pulled her in before they collided.

“Trying to kill my competition?”

“Was he competition?”

He glanced at the offended man.

“Nah. My mistake.”

They found a smaller turn that worked with the pillory. Briar began anticipating it, laughing when Kaze changed direction. Her hand tightened around his.

“You said you were lying.”

“About being good. Never said I wasn't enjoying it.”

“You keep looking at her.”

He didn't pretend he hadn't.

“She's looking back.”

“She looks at everyone like they're already doing what she wants.”

Kaze glanced toward the table. Elise was speaking quietly to Master Venn. His earlier embarrassment had vanished; he was leaning toward her, intent on every word.

“You don't like that?” Kaze asked.

“I want to decide first.”

“Then decide something.”

Briar pulled him into a turn of her own. He let her take the lead, and they narrowly missed a servant carrying six glasses.

“That,” she said.

For several minutes, they managed to be the happiest people in a room that had spent a considerable amount of money on happiness.

When the musicians stopped, a servant approached Briar.

“My lady has arranged somewhere more comfortable for you to eat.”

Briar looked at her empty plate, still on the distant table.

“I was comfortable.”

“More privacy.”

Kaze looked past the servant. Elise had risen beside the collector. She lifted a hand, inviting him to join her.

Briar followed his glance.

“You want to go?”

“I want to hear the price.”

“Then I'm hearing it too.”

She walked past the servant and caught up with Elise before Kaze did.

Elise accepted the change with a gracious nod.

The older part of the house smelled of stone, lamp oil, and something sweet left too long in a closed room. Master Venn walked close beside Elise. Kaze and Briar followed them up a shallow stair into a sitting room above the terrace.

Tall shutters stood open at the far end. Outside lay the glass roof of the hall, a narrow balcony, and the orchard descending toward the road. A bell cord hung beside the door.

A man in a plain dark coat waited near the shutters. No tray, no wine. His hands were empty, but he stood where he could reach both the balcony and the room's occupants.

I have met servants and I have met men paid to make a difficult guest quiet. That one had chosen the second profession.

Elise closed the door.

“I value discretion,” she said.

Kaze looked at the man by the shutters.

“Then why he here?”

“Security.”

“Whose?”

Master Venn cleared his throat.

“Lady Elise has offered me an introduction. A privilege, actually. I don't see why these people—”

“Sit,” Elise said.

He sat.

There was something miserable in the speed of it. I couldn't have told you where flattery ended and her stranger influence began. I could see that he hadn't asked another question since she promised him something he wanted.

Elise turned to Kaze.

“You recognized the nature of an exchange downstairs. I thought you might appreciate a more honest discussion.”

“Try me.”

“The Shadow Isles,” she said. “That is our destination.”

“They are cursed,” Elise added. “They are not closed to me.”

Venn lifted his chin, trying to look as though the name had pleased him.

It did not please me. Those islands were steeped in the Black Mist, a curse that bound the dead and made a very poor distinction between visitors and fresh inhabitants. There were reasons people went there. Most were better examined from a safe distance.

Kaze watched Elise's face instead of Venn's performance.

“You take pleasure cruises into a curse?”

“I know where to land. I know which paths to follow.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“There is a being there called Vilemaw. Ancient. Powerful. Capable of giving a person more than a lifetime.”

Venn pressed his palms together.

“Lady Elise has explained—”

“What you want to hear,” Kaze said. “I'm asking what happens.”

The collector's expression tightened.

“You think youth entitles you to sneer at everything?”

“No. I put work in.”

“My father died unable to recognize his own house. I watched him ask a servant to take him home while he was sitting beside his own fire. If there is a way to avoid that, I intend to hear it.”

For the first time that afternoon, he sounded like a man speaking for himself.

Elise placed a hand on his shoulder.

“You deserve more than that ending.”

His fingers unclasped. He leaned back beneath her touch.

I understood why he'd followed her. I wished he hadn't made it so bloody easy.

Briar was watching the collector now.

“You should still ask what happens.”

“I have assurances.”

“From her.”

Elise's smile remained in place.

“You are both suspicious for people who accepted my hospitality.”

“I accepted lunch,” Briar said.

Kaze laughed. Elise let the silence after it grow until the distant music became awkwardly audible through the floor.

Then she removed her hand from Venn's shoulder and approached Kaze.

“I believe you and I could understand each other.”

“You trying to tempt me or recruit me?”

“Must the two be separate?”

“If you want me distracted, you should've stopped talking about work.”

“And what would hold your attention?”

He looked at her mouth, then met her eyes.

“You know one answer already. Give me a reason to like the rest.”

“Access. Influence. People who can find what you want before you know where to look.”

“And they all come with hands out.”

“Naturally.”

“I like your honesty better than your sales pitch.”

Elise moved back beside Venn's chair. This time she stood behind it, leaving him between herself and Kaze.

“Help me bring suitable people to my patron. In return, I can open doors you would otherwise spend a great deal of time breaking.”

“People like him?”

Venn looked up.

Elise didn't.

“People who want more.”

“And you keep getting more.”

“Yes.”

Kaze smiled at her candour. He came closer, close enough that the man by the shutters shifted his weight.

“Now that's an answer.”

“I thought you'd prefer one.”

“I do. Still ain't signing up to bring you groceries.”

Venn stood halfway.

“Groceries?”

“Sit down, Master Venn,” Elise said.

He hesitated this time.

Briar moved nearer the door.

Elise's eyes followed her.

“Wouldn't you like someone to help with that restraint?”

“No.”

“I have access to people who understand unusual bodies. You wouldn't have to live with discomfort.”

“I said no.”

“You haven't heard what I can offer.”

Briar's fingers curled around the edge of the pillory.

“Then offer it to someone who asked.”

She reached for the door. The man by the shutters crossed the room and put his hand against it above hers.

“The conversation isn't finished.”

Kaze's head turned before the man's other hand reached inside his coat.

The danger had become immediate: a short blade coming toward Briar's exposed side. Kaze's right eye caught the shoulder movement, the wrist turning inward. His body was already moving.

Black Marrow's hilt rose from its temporary sheath at his left hip. His right hand closed around it. The living weapon shifted with his intent as he drew across the reaching arm.

Steel met wrist.

The hand and its knife struck the carpet together.

The man made a strangled noise. Briar drove her pillory into his face and shoved him backward so hard his head struck the doorframe. He dropped beside his severed hand and stayed down, breathing through blood.

Briar's breath caught. The fresh blood had her attention at once; her teeth showed, and for a moment her gaze stayed on the open wrist.

She tightened her hands against the pillory and dragged her eyes away. The restraint helped her hold that choice. It hadn't taken away the hunger.

Master Venn screamed.

Elise lifted her arm.

Kaze felt the new threat and turned into its path, keeping Briar behind him. White webbing snapped across the room, catching his sword arm against the wall. Threads stretched from wrist to plaster as he pulled.

He looked at them, then at her.

“That's your answer?”

She had already moved.

The woman in the red-black gown collapsed into a low, many-legged shape. Jointed limbs struck the floor. Her body swelled beneath a hard dark shell, and the face that had smiled over wine vanished behind a spider's terrible mouth.

Venn tried to run. Briar caught the back of his coat and hauled him away from Elise's descending foreleg.

“Wrong door!” she shouted.

Kaze pulled his arm free.

The webs held their knots. The plaster didn't hold the webs. A sheet of wall tore loose, exposing timber behind it, and Black Marrow sliced through the strands still clinging to his wrist.

Elise lunged before the debris settled.

Kaze stepped inside the sweep of her nearest leg, caught the joint above its point with his free left hand, and drove his shoulder beneath her body. The floorboards bowed under his planted feet.

Then he heaved.

Elise hit the open shutters sideways. Wood burst around her. Her weight smashed through the balcony rail, and she fell onto the glass roof below.

The entire hall rang like a struck bell.

Glass folded inward beneath her. Guests screamed and scattered between the tables as a huge black spider landed among the remains of their expensive lunch.

Kaze stepped onto what remained of the balcony.

“Now they know the fucking price.”

Elise scrambled across a table, driving people before her. She threw a line of silk toward the stone arch at the far end of the terrace, climbed along it, and reached the outer wall before Kaze jumped.

He landed on the terrace beside the arch.

Stone cracked beneath him. The nearest pillar split from top to bottom, and one side of the arch sagged into the garden.

Elise sprang sideways onto the orchard wall as it fell. Her thread caught beneath the collapsing stone and snapped. She staggered, recovered on the wall's narrow top, and turned toward him.

Kaze held Black Marrow low at his side.

Behind him, guests were scrambling through the broken hall. Elise had a clear route down the wall into the orchard. He had a clear route after her.

She backed away first.

“You could have benefited,” she said, her voice coming strangely from that monstrous shape.

“I was having a good time till you hired me.”

“I can be very patient, Kaze.”

“You put a knife next to somebody I came with.”

His smile had gone.

“Keep walking.”

Elise went over the far side of the wall. Branches shook as she moved through the orchard. Kaze watched the movement recede and let her go.

Above the shattered hall, Briar appeared in the broken doorway. Master Venn clung to the frame behind her.

Kaze looked up.

“You good?”

“Yes.”

“He good?”

She glanced back at the collector.

“He's very loud.”

“You want to stay?” Kaze asked.

“No. I want somewhere we can finish a conversation without her turning it into something else.”

He glanced once more at the orchard. Then he brought Black Marrow home to its sheath and let the weapon recede.

“Come on, then.”

Venn stared at the wrecked glass roof between himself and the ground.

“How do I get down?”

“Stairs,” Briar said.

“Where?”

“Same ones we used.”

She came down by those stairs. Venn followed, carefully stepping around the wounded man at the sitting-room door. Two servants went up after them with cloths and a basin. Whether that man would keep his life after losing his hand, I couldn't tell you.

Briar found Kaze in the garden. He was watching a musician rescue his fiddle from beneath a fallen chair.

“She was pretty,” Briar said.

“Yeah.”

“You still wanted to sleep with her.”

“Before the knife.”

“And the spider?”

He considered it.

Briar stared at him.

“Kaze.”

“You asked a question. Let me respect it.”

She laughed so hard she had to lean against him.

Master Venn emerged behind them, pale and trembling. His expensive coat was torn where Briar had caught it.

“You saved me.”

“You were blocking the way,” she said.

“Nonetheless, I owe you—”

“You can stop calling me a piece.”

He looked down at the pillory, caught himself, and met her eyes.

“Yes. Of course.”

She seemed satisfied with that. She turned away before he could turn gratitude into another speech.

They left by the front gate as servants began leading carriages out of the court. Nobody asked for an invitation this time.

By late afternoon, the house and its broken roof were behind them. They took a side path beside the river, following it until the carriage traffic faded and the water spread into a quiet bend beneath the trees.

An old fishing shelter stood above the bank, roof sound, walls weathered, its open side facing the water. There was a cold hearth, a wide wooden bench, and nobody inside.

Briar walked through it once, checking behind the bench.

“No large spiders.”

“Don't promise shit you can't deliver,” Kaze said.

She sat and stretched her legs.

Kaze sat beside her. For a while they watched the river, listening to the last carriage wheels disappear along the higher road.

“You didn't answer her for me,” Briar said.

“You were answering fine.”

“Until he came over.”

“Then he had something else to worry about.”

She rubbed a dried spot of blood from the edge of her restraint.

“I was still hungry.”

“I saw.”

“I could hear him breathing.”

Kaze watched her thumb move over the rim.

“You left.”

“I wanted to leave.”

She looked out at the river. He didn't tell her what she ought to have wanted instead.

After a moment, Briar nudged his knee with hers.

“You were enjoying her questions.”

“Some of them.”

“So was I. Until every answer meant I should do what she said.”

“You got questions?”

“Yes.”

“We got time.”

“Were you really going to cross the sea with her?”

“Didn't get that far.”

“Would you tell me if you were?”

He turned toward her.

“Yeah.”

“Before you jumped onto a boat?”

“Before.”

She nodded, accepting the answer without making it larger than it was.

“I liked the dancing.”

“I know. You almost killed a waiter.”

“He moved.”

“Good waiter.”

She rested her forehead against his shoulder, careful of the pillory. His hand settled at her waist.

“We could try it again,” she said.

“The dancing?”

She lifted her head and smiled at him.

“You ask more questions when you already know the answer.”

“Like hearing you say it.”

“I want another night with you.”

He looked around the shelter.

“Standards getting worse already.”

“The house had too many people.”

“And one significant fucking spider.”

Briar laughed, then drew him into a kiss.

They slept together again at the river shelter, with the evening and the choice entirely their own. I left its private business private.

Their voices still carried now and then across the water.

“You do enjoy being told you're impressive,” Briar said.

“You got a complaint?”

“I haven't told you yet.”

“That's the complaint.”

Her laughter startled a bird from the reeds.

Later, when the light had almost gone, she sat beside him again with her shoulder against his. Kaze's trousers remained torn. Briar's pillory remained hers to open or keep closed. Neither had become easier to manage.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

He glanced at her.

“What about it?”

“You said you would remember yesterday.”

“I remember.”

“Tomorrow, you can remember this too.”

Kaze smiled and put his arm around her.

Upstream, the first lamps appeared along the road. I imagined Elise's guests would have plenty to say when they reached home. Whether anybody would believe the first version was another matter.

Kaze and Briar watched the river until they couldn't see the far bank anymore.
