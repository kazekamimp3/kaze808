Season 3, Episode 48: “A Little Off the Top”

Brian had written excellent presence beneath Initial Contact.

Now he was trying to decide whether the words looked worse crossed out.

Dr. Evil stopped beside the crate.

“Finished?”

Brian covered the lower half of the page.

“Mostly.”

“An encouraging measurement.”

“It's an account of the meeting. Not a fucking weather report. There's interpretation involved.”

The doctor looked toward the harbor.

“The weather has shown considerable restraint about introducing Busta Rhymes.”

Brian moved his hand.

Dr. Evil took the sheet. Read the heading, the short account of the weapons, the proposed inquiry about the driver.

Then the little line at the bottom.

He paused.

Brian looked out at the water with the strained innocence of a man whose diary had developed witnesses.

“Whose presence?” the doctor asked.

“The overall meeting.”

“The meeting had excellent presence.”

“As an event.”

“I see.”

He folded the paper with the last line facing inward.

Brian watched it disappear.

“I was speaking professionally.”

“Then we have both had a very professional afternoon.”

Dr. Evil went aboard.

Brian remained beside the crate, furious that he'd neither received a compliment nor been given a clean reason to object.

Farther along the quay, Chris was looking at a notice nailed beside a warehouse door.

“Arthur.”

Arthur turned.

Chris pointed.

The paper had come from the district inland. A description of the ruined Golden Heron. A request for information about an armed group. Two names, acquired by different men on different roads and now occupying the same unpleasant square of paper.

Kaze.

Christopher Graham.

The description gave clothes, weapons and the places witnesses had seen them. Nothing to help a tallyman decide whether the men reading it were about to become his problem.

Chris read his name again.

“They got the spelling.”

Arthur came beside him.

“You signed it.”

“I know. Still. Standards.”

Billy's voice came from the banjo.

“A rare occasion on which your paperwork has traveled farther than your tip.”

Chris turned his head slightly, giving Billy the whole notice through the headlamp.

“They didn't serve the meal.”

“They are certainly serving something now.”

Kaze stopped behind them.

He read the account of the tollhouse. The broken barrier. The name he'd given the runner. His apparent claim about the land rupture was described as a claim, rather than a fact some district captain could prove by writing harder.

Kaze tapped that part.

“Apparent.”

Arthur looked at him.

“Leave it.”

“I told the motherfucker.”

“He wrote what he could establish.”

Kaze looked toward the warehouse door.

An adult tallyman stood just inside, watching them notice the notice.

His gaze traveled from the name on the paper to the name across Kaze's stomach.

The little book in his hands stopped moving.

Kaze smiled at him.

“See? Yours works.”

The tallyman said nothing.

Chris looked over his shoulder.

“You want a signature too?”

“Christopher,” Billy said.

“What?”

“You have demonstrated the difficulty with those.”

Daynuh came up with Mahnuh. The dog sniffed the lower edge of the warehouse door, where old fish water had soaked into the timber. Daynuh drew him back with a hand against his chest.

“That about us?”

“Some of us,” Arthur said.

Daynuh read the paper.

“Daynuh ain't even on it.”

“You can survive the disappointment,” Chris said.

“Was planning to.”

Kaze stepped closer to the tallyman.

The man backed up once, then stopped when the table touched his legs.

“Who put that up?”

“Post runner.”

“Where'd he go?”

“Harbor watch.”

Kaze looked down the quay.

Arthur moved into his sightline.

“We just came here to ask a question.”

“Asked it.”

“And now you want to make the answer somebody else's afternoon.”

Kaze's eyes returned to his face.

“Maybe I wanna hear what the watch has to say.”

“You heard it. It's nailed to a door.”

For a moment, the harbor continued around them without joining in.

Kaze looked past Arthur again.

The tallyman held his little book against his chest. He hadn't reached for a weapon. He hadn't shouted for help. He was trying to remain a man who had been counting rope before his work acquired a catastrophe.

Kaze looked back at the paper.

“You got a pencil?”

Arthur shut his eyes.

The tallyman offered one.

Kaze took it and drew one line beneath his own name.

Then another beneath apparent.

He gave the pencil back.

“Tell him I said it clearly.”

He walked away before the man could decide whether that counted as a message or a threat.

Chris watched him go.

“He's correcting his fucking wanted notice.”

“Request for information,” Arthur said.

“He's giving it notes.”

Billy plucked one low note through the banjo.

“The artist remains available for comment.”

At the Immortal Bastion, the report had arrived by a surviving military route with dust inside its folds.

The courier stood in a room that had lost one window and acquired three arguments about the cost of replacing it. He had changed mounts where he could, walked where he couldn't, and carried the district captain's packet around more than one place where a road had become an opinion.

Swain let him finish describing the route before asking about the contents.

“Who spoke to the man?”

“The runner, General. The tollkeeper heard him at the post.”

Swain read the name.

Kaze.

Darius stood at the other side of the table, his enormous axe resting within reach against the wall.

“He claims the rupture.”

“He does,” Swain said.

“Could be boasting.”

“It would be an ambitious choice of audience.”

The red fingers of Swain's left hand held the paper against a draft. His silver hair moved slightly as air came through the broken window.

The restaurant account lay beside it. Christopher Graham's signature had been copied from the surviving book. The constable's description distinguished dead staff and diners from the marksman beyond the canal. Two names acquired separately. Whatever had joined the men on the road, the paper gave Swain no account of it.

Swain put a finger beside the signature.

“The original?”

“Held at the district station.”

“Keep it there. Guard it. I want the man who found it available to answer questions.”

Darius read the account of the tollhouse occupation.

“The captain kept his men at the crossings.”

“Sensibly.”

“Some will call it retreat.”

Swain looked up.

“They may volunteer their own crossing to prove the distinction.”

The courier kept his face still.

Darius moved to the route map. Several roads carried new marks over old ink. A line drawn through a broken district did not become usable because a commander disliked the interruption.

“They've left the post,” he said.

“By the time this was written.”

“And by now they could be somewhere else entirely.”

Swain nodded.

“Then we shall avoid an impressive assault on an empty building.”

He called for an officer from the passage. Gave him the two names and the descriptions actually supported by the accounts.

“Question witnesses separately. Ask where they saw them, when, and doing what. No promises of pardon to improve a memory. No patrol sent into a contest it cannot report back from.”

The officer listened.

“Arrest on sight?”

“Observe first. If you can contain them without losing the district, contain them. If you cannot, tell me what would be required.”

Darius looked toward the courier.

“The first man who turns this into a chance to win a personal reputation will answer for the men he spends.”

The officer nodded and went to make the order travel.

It would take time. Horses, boots, surviving bridges. The empire had learned two names. It had not acquired the ability to stand beside them by saying them aloud.

Orra appeared in the doorway carrying a dirty length of survey cord.

“Your lower passage has a new problem.”

Swain folded the report.

“New since the rupture?”

“New since yesterday.”

That brought Darius away from the map.

They followed her below the repaired stair. Workers had shored the approach with timber. Lamps showed the old arch, the braced door and the iron fastening she'd ordered everyone to leave alone.

A soldier stood beside the entrance. He kept his hands where Orra could see them.

She showed Swain the end of the cord.

“I tied it across there.”

“It broke?”

“It was cut.”

The fibers ended cleanly. Not crushed beneath falling stone. Not pulled until the knot gave.

Darius turned toward the soldier.

“Who entered?”

“No one while I was here.”

“You weren't always here.”

The man swallowed.

“Relief was late. I went up for a lamp. Mine was failing.”

Orra lifted the old lamp from a stone shelf. Its reservoir was dry.

“That part's true.”

Darius held out his hand for the lamp.

Orra passed it to him.

“Who was your relief?”

The man gave a name.

“You'll tell the watch commander. He'll send for him. Both of you will give the times separately.”

“Yes, General.”

Darius put the empty lamp down.

“And the next man posted here gets a full one before he takes the stair.”

Orra looked at the soldier.

“Two,” she said. “Unless you want this conversation twice.”

Darius nodded once.

Swain looked past the broken cord.

“What moved?”

“The little plate below the fastening. A finger's width. There's fresh scraping beneath it.”

She crouched without touching the iron.

The door still stood where they'd braced it. The visible damage had not obligingly opened the entire secret. But somebody had used the gap in the watch, and had been more careful about leaving than entering.

Darius called two men from the stair.

One stayed with the soldier. The other took a lamp and stood where he could watch Orra's hands and the passage beyond her.

Swain lowered himself enough to examine the scraped stone.

Beyond the lamp's reach, the narrow gap disappeared into blackness. He kept his hands outside it.

“Can you remove the plate without shifting the support?”

Orra considered it.

“If your men stop trying to improve the answer with their fingers.”

“They will.”

She wedged the surrounding masonry, checked the load, and asked Darius to hold the outer bracket while she eased the loose plate away.

He took the weight with both hands. The metal moved only when she told him it could.

Behind the plate, a narrow recess ran into the newer ironwork.

Orra brought the lamp close.

An oily thumbprint marked the inside edge. Below it lay a short sliver of newly cut cord.

Someone had pushed the evidence out of sight, but had not pushed it far enough.

Swain watched her recover it with the tip of a small iron tool.

“The passage is being used,” Darius said.

“Part of it,” Swain answered.

He looked up toward the braced door.

“Leave the plate as it was.”

Orra turned.

“You want them to come back.”

“I want them to believe the same mistake remains available.”

Darius looked toward the soldier waiting at the stair.

“It won't.”

On the coast, Brian had found a mirror that could be tilted toward the edge of a crewman's desk.

He'd borrowed scissors. Asked for a razor. Been asked why.

“Presentation.”

The crewman had looked at his hair.

Then at the scissors.

“Do you want help?”

“I know what a haircut is.”

The man had left the razor in its case and gone back to work.

Brian now had the first lock between his fingers.

He stared at it.

He could still put the scissors down. There was no contract. Nobody had demanded a transformation. Dr. Evil had not asked for a smaller copy of himself.

That last fact irritated Brian enough to move his hand.

The scissors closed.

Hair fell onto the cloth he'd spread across his knees.

He looked in the mirror.

One side of his head had become an argument against stopping.

He cut the other.

Then the top.

He changed the mirror's angle, lifted his chin and found three different lengths competing to represent him.

“It's transitional,” he told the empty room.

The crewman returned for a spool of line.

Stopped at the desk.

Brian looked up.

The man looked back.

“Do you want help now?”

“I was getting to the finish.”

“I can see where you stopped.”

Brian put down the scissors.

The crewman fetched a second cloth and asked before moving the mirror. He used the scissors to shorten what Brian couldn't see properly, then worked with the razor while Brian held still.

It took longer than Brian wanted. The man's tools were ordinary human tools. The head beneath them was considerably smaller than the one he usually shaved.

“Don't make it uneven,” Brian said.

The crewman looked at the cloth full of Brian's first attempt.

“Doing my best.”

When it was finished, Brian leaned toward the mirror.

His face was still his face.

That was a relief he resented needing.

Above it, the bare scalp changed the whole outline. He turned left. Then right. Pulled his collar up, considered it, and left it where it had been before.

One change was enough to have to explain.

“Good,” he said.

The crewman began gathering his tools.

“Thank you,” Brian added, as though supplying a correction to the room.

“You're welcome.”

“Busta Rhymes would understand the commitment.”

The crewman looked at the scissors he was cleaning.

“Is that another barber?”

Brian stared at him.

“No.”

“Right.”

“That's such a small room to put him in.”

The man looked around the actual small room.

“I only work here.”

Brian considered explaining the full significance. The crewman had already begun folding the cloth, carefully trapping the cut hair inside it.

“Never mind.”

It felt less like restraint than having an important cultural conversation interrupted by somebody disposing of most of his former head.

Brian climbed down from the seat.

The seam beneath his shirt pulled once as he stretched. He put a hand over it until it settled, then straightened his jacket.

Nothing under the cloth had asked to admire Dr. Evil. Nothing there had promised to stop being hungry because Brian had changed his head.

He left the borrowed tools where the man wanted them and went to find his employer.

Dr. Evil sat at the briefing table with Dutch and Clifford. They were discussing which stores could be moved without interfering with the crew's work.

Brian stopped in the doorway.

Dutch saw him first.

His sentence lost its ending.

Clifford turned.

Then Dr. Evil looked up.

The room went quiet.

Brian put his notebook against his chest.

“What?”

Dr. Evil continued looking.

“Have we had an accident?”

“It's a haircut.”

The doctor looked toward Dutch.

“Was any of it recovered?”

Brian climbed onto his box beside the table.

“I decided to change my presentation.”

“To mine.”

“You don't own bald.”

Dr. Evil touched his own scalp with the mild interest of a landlord hearing somebody question the lease.

“No. But I have occupied it for some time.”

Clifford looked down at his hands.

Brian saw.

“You can say something.”

“Looks colder,” Clifford said.

Dutch shifted a paper over the corner of the ledger.

“A change can be a useful declaration of purpose.”

Brian pointed at him.

“Exactly.”

“Unfortunately,” Dr. Evil said, “we shall still have to hear the purpose.”

Brian's hand dropped.

The doctor went back to the stores list.

That was worse than the staring.

Brian opened his notebook.

“The public-facing impression matters. If people immediately understand you're serious, you don't spend the first ten minutes explaining the position.”

Dr. Evil glanced at the page.

“How many minutes have you budgeted for this?”

“Busta Rhymes understands reinvention.”

The doctor set the paper down.

“Did he take the rest of your hair?”

“I'm talking about committing to a direction.”

“You have certainly committed. We could hardly ask you to put it back before supper.”

Dutch's mouth tightened at one corner.

Brian turned toward him.

“You were just agreeing with me.”

“I agreed that a change could mean something.”

“It does.”

Dr. Evil folded his hands.

“Then I look forward to discovering what.”

Brian's little finger rose toward his mouth.

He saw the doctor's eyes follow it.

The finger stopped.

He lowered the hand onto the notebook instead.

For one long second, neither spoke.

Then Dr. Evil turned to Dutch.

“Continue.”

Dutch got through the next item before a crewman appeared carrying the recorder used for their earlier research.

Brian watched him cross the doorway.

“Actually. We could use that.”

Dr. Evil shut his eyes briefly.

“Could we.”

“A reference recording. Company presentation. We keep it here. See what works before anybody has to see it in person.”

“We've just had several people see it in person.”

“And now we can improve.”

The doctor opened his eyes.

“Whose presentation?”

“The company.”

“With me.”

Brian paused.

“Yes.”

“Well. There may be something to it.”

The crewman looked at Dutch. Dutch looked at the small patch of table still available between the papers.

“Nothing leaves this room,” Dutch said.

“It hasn't even entered the fucking recorder,” Brian said.

The crewman set the device down and checked its remaining space. Then he asked where they wanted him to stand.

Dr. Evil settled back in his chair.

Brian stood on his box.

The crewman lowered the recorder until both heads were in view.

Dr. Evil examined the angle.

“Why are you interviewing my chin?”

The man raised it.

Brian vanished below the frame.

“I'm still here,” Brian said.

“We had all hoped otherwise,” Dr. Evil said.

Brian pointed at the table.

“I could stand there.”

“Food goes there.”

“Not now.”

“I prefer to preserve the possibility.”

Dutch moved Brian's box farther from the chair. The crewman stepped back. Both men now fit without placing Brian among the stores lists or giving Dr. Evil the looming perspective of a disappointed dentist.

“There,” Dutch said.

Brian inspected the distance.

“We look separate.”

Dr. Evil smiled.

“Technology is extraordinary.”

The crewman started recording.

Dr. Evil put one hand on the chair arm.

“Tampon Pandemic Productions represents an opportunity—”

“Founded by Brian.”

The doctor stopped.

The crewman stopped recording.

Dr. Evil turned his head very slowly.

“Were you planning to interrupt with the entire birth certificate?”

“I founded it.”

“Then you have had your moment. Some of us are attempting to provide it with a future.”

Brian folded his arms.

Dutch set down his pencil.

“Let him finish the opening. Then introduce yourself.”

“As founder.”

“Yes, Brian.”

Clifford shifted his chair out of the background without being asked.

They started again.

The doctor delivered his opening. Brian waited through it, watching him hold the little room's attention without raising his voice. The pause at the end was so complete Brian nearly missed it.

Then he straightened.

“I'm Brian. Founder. I handle the creative direction and music.”

His mouth remained open for one perilous extra second.

Somewhere behind his teeth, Busta Rhymes was approaching an intersection at speed.

Brian closed his mouth.

The crewman stopped the recording.

“That's usable,” Dutch said.

Brian looked at him.

“Yes. It is.”

They gathered around the recorder's small viewing screen. The crewman played back what they'd just made.

On it, Dr. Evil sat motionless and bald. Brian stood beside him, also bald, looking up at the doctor before remembering to look toward the lens.

Nobody spoke.

Brian pointed.

“Take out that bit.”

“Which bit?” the crewman asked.

“Before I speak.”

Dr. Evil leaned closer.

“Oh, I think we should keep the respect.”

“I'm checking the cue.”

“A wonderfully attentive check.”

Clifford looked over Dutch's shoulder.

“Good picture.”

Brian turned.

“For what?”

“Evidence.”

The doctor laughed once. Brian looked back at the tiny image of himself looking up.

“It's a height issue.”

“Several,” Dr. Evil said.

The crewman stopped playback and put the recorder away. Brian watched the screen go dark. He wanted to see it again, preferably when fewer people were standing behind him, and couldn't think of a way to ask that wouldn't give the doctor the rest of his afternoon.

Brian sat down on the box.

He hated the man.

He had also spent the afternoon making it more difficult to explain how much.

On the submarine, the stores discussion had moved on without Brian's permission.
