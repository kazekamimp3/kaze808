Episode 74: Carry-On

By morning, somebody had painted an arrow around the hole in the quay.

Not filled it. Not fenced it. Painted an arrow.

Civilization was recovering beautifully.

Arthur followed the arrow with his eyes, then looked at the broken roof beyond it. A dockworker was levering loose tiles into a cart. Another stood on the intact corner, arguing that the damaged half still counted as covered storage.

Kaze was beside the Subaru, picking a loose curl of metal off its torn front wing. He turned it between his fingers, looked at the missing mirror, then looked up.

A broad, flat ship came down over the water.

Its engines rolled dust along the quay before the landing gear touched. Two forward prongs passed over the depot wall. A cockpit stuck out along one side, as though somebody had remembered the driver after finishing the rest of the vehicle.

Kaze watched it settle on the stone freight apron.

“I want that.”

Arthur glanced at him.

“You could ask.”

“I could.”

Neither moved for a moment.

Then Arthur started toward the ship.

Billy followed, banjo against his belly. Artoo rolled away from the Subaru to inspect the newcomer, chirping to himself. Through Billy’s instrument, Chris said, “Hold on. Let me see the back of it.”

Billy turned his head.

“The ship, Billy.”

“I am looking at the ship.”

“You’re looking at a woman carrying onions.”

“She is between me and the ship.”

“Move.”

Billy moved. The woman kept her onions.

Inside, Chris leaned toward the exterior monitor. His forearm cut had closed during the night’s recovery, leaving a tender stripe. The bruising had faded enough that he no longer guarded his ribs when he moved. He set down the cloth he had been checking and studied the ship instead.

Behind him, Daynah was asleep on the couch. Mahnah occupied the part of it Daynah’s feet had failed to defend.

The ramp lowered with a hydraulic groan.

A man in a dark vest came down first, blaster riding low at his hip. He looked over the damaged harbor, the patched buildings and the fresh arrow pointing around the hole.

“This is the repair depot?”

Arthur looked at the ship.

“Depends how bad it is.”

The man’s mouth tightened.

“It’s fine. We need a cooling-line coupling.”

Something clanged inside. A deep, irritated roar followed it.

“And the part he broke getting to the coupling.”

A huge, brown-furred figure ducked down the ramp carrying a scorched metal housing. Grease marked the knees of his dark maintenance coveralls. His bandolier lay across them, and a bowcaster hung within reach against his chest.

He held up the housing.

The man pointed at it.

“That was already cracked.”

The Wookiee snapped the remaining attached strip off with his fingers.

“All right. Now it’s broken.”

Kaze walked around the end of the ramp, looking up beneath the hull.

“What’s it called?”

“The Millennium Falcon.”

“And you?”

“Han Solo. That’s Chewie. You run this place?”

“No.”

“Then try not to stand under anything we’re removing.”

Chewie pushed a low freight trolley beneath the open maintenance hatch. He set the broken housing on it, climbed onto the platform and reached up into the ship.

Han watched Kaze circle back.

“You looking for passage?”

“Maybe. How much for the whole thing?”

“More than you’ve got.”

Kaze smiled. “You don’t know how much I got.”

Han looked at the missing mirror on the Subaru.

“I’ve got a pretty good idea.”

“That ain’t his,” Arthur said. “He’s poorer.”

Chris’s voice came out of the banjo.

“My car has been shot. That is not a financial assessment.”

Han looked at the instrument.

Billy rested a broad hand over its strings.

“My associate is particular about his automobile.”

“Your associate in there?”

“He is available for comment.”

Chewie stopped working long enough to look over his shoulder. Billy had wandered between the trolley and the ramp, trying to see what he was fixing.

Han’s hand drifted nearer his blaster. His expression stayed easy.

“Well, tell him the ship’s not for sale.”

“I heard you,” Chris said.

Kaze stepped onto the bottom of the ramp.

Han moved across his path.

“Passengers pay first.”

“I’m just looking.”

“Look from there.”

Kaze glanced past him into the corridor, then back at the ship’s exterior.

“Does it have a bathroom?”

“Yes.”

“See, Chris? Already an upgrade.”

“We have a bathroom.”

“We have an argument about where the bathroom is.”

Billy’s eyes narrowed.

“There is a designated area.”

Han raised his voice a fraction.

“Chewie.”

The Wookiee lowered the tool in his hand. He took his bowcaster off its sling without pointing it yet.

Arthur saw the movement and stopped beside a stack of empty freight pallets, where the corner of the stack covered his right side. His hands remained clear of his guns.

“You boys expecting trouble?” he asked.

“I’m looking at it,” Han said.

Kaze rocked once on his heels.

“You were doing pretty good till that.”

“Till what?”

“I was gonna look around. Now you got everybody doing the thing. Hand by the gun, your boy’s got the crossbow. You could’ve just—”

He stopped and gave a small laugh.

“Nah. That’s bullshit. I was gonna steal it anyway.”

Han drew.

Kaze was already moving off the line of the muzzle. The first bolt struck the pallet stack beside Arthur and blew burning wood across the stone.

Kaze caught Han’s wrist before he could track him. He drove the hand upward, turned the blaster out of it and threw the weapon under the hull.

Han hit the ramp on one knee. His free hand slapped its side control.

The ramp began to rise.

Kaze put his foot against the lower edge and held it there. The mechanism whined. Han used the pull on Kaze’s arm to get his feet under him, drove a shoulder into his chest and tried to turn through the gap.

Kaze barely shifted.

“Oh, you actually—”

Chewie fired.

Billy swung the banjo into the shot’s path.

The plasma-wrapped quarrel vanished against its face. The strings gave a sharp metallic snarl. Billy plucked them, sending the captured attack back across the apron.

Chewie was already dropping off the trolley. The returned shot tore through the housing he had left on it and blasted the platform sideways.

Hot fragments skipped beneath the Falcon.

“That,” Han shouted, “was a repairable housing!”

Chewie roared at him.

“I know what I said!”

Billy advanced while Chewie came up behind the tipped trolley. A second shot scorched the stone at Billy’s foot as he changed direction. The banjo had stopped the first one because he had put it there. The rest of him did not come with a complimentary force field.

Chewie shoved the trolley at his knees.

Billy stepped over its edge and caught the bowcaster as Chewie tried to bring it up again. The Wookiee drove his shoulder into Billy’s middle, pushing with both legs.

Billy slid half a step.

Then the catfish lowered his weight.

The movement stopped.

“Now, son,” Billy said, “I appreciate a gentleman who arrives dressed for the work.”

Chewie struck him in the mouth with the bowcaster’s stock.

Green blood ran over Billy’s lower lip.

Billy smiled wider.

“And he has brought his own tools.”

Han twisted against Kaze’s grip, saw the fight under the hull and stopped trying to win a wrestling match with the shirtless man who had just become a bollard.

He kicked the ramp control again.

The pressure reversed. Kaze’s braced foot dropped with the ramp, and Han used that change to wrench his arm toward the ship.

Kaze let him go.

Han sprinted inside.

Arthur drew his left UNSHAKEN revolver and covered the ramp without firing.

“You meant to do that?”

“He ain’t running away from his ship.”

Kaze followed him up.

Inside, Han tore open an already-loose service panel. He pulled a heavy power coupling free and smashed its connector collar against the frame. The collar split. He struck it again, breaking the mount, then backed along the corridor with the damaged part in his hand.

The engine hum died.

“You want it?” Han said. “Fine. Fly it.”

Kaze looked at the darkened strip above the doorway.

“What’d you do?”

“Something you’re not fixing before I’m gone.”

“You’re still here.”

Han hurled the coupling at his face and lunged for the ramp.

Kaze caught the metal piece. His other hand caught Han by the vest and slammed him against the corridor wall.

The impact knocked the breath out of him. Kaze held him there long enough to look at the part, then set it on the floor.

“You threw the thing you wanted me not to have.”

“Try plugging it in.”

Kaze glanced at the broken collar. Han had not just handed over a key. He had snapped it in the lock first.

Han drove his knee up.

Kaze turned it aside and pushed him down onto the deck, face first.

“Stop. You’re making this a whole fucken activity.”

Outside, Chewie had abandoned the bowcaster rather than let Billy control both his arms through it.

He caught the edge of the maintenance hatch and hauled himself up. Billy reached after him. Chewie swung both feet into the catfish’s face, knocking his hat crooked, then dropped beside the landing gear.

Billy’s next step brought him around the strut.

Chewie seized his wrist, turned under the arm and drove an elbow into the joint. Billy’s arm bent. The Wookiee kept turning, trying to put the landing strut between them.

Billy’s tail hooked behind his leg.

Chewie fell onto one knee and immediately rolled clear of the descending banjo. Its rim struck stone hard enough to punch chips into his coveralls.

He came up with a length of broken trolley rail.

Billy wiped his mouth with his thumb.

“I once fought a fellow so hairy the mosquitoes hired a guide.”

Chewie swung for his head.

Billy caught the rail under his arm.

“Claimed he could pull a stump without a rope. I said, ‘Then pull it.’ Well, he changed his mind about which stump. Suddenly we had to walk half a mile to a special stump he knew personally—”

Chewie released the rail and punched him square in the eye.

Billy spat green blood onto the stone.

“You are considerably less evasive.”

From the banjo, Chris snapped, “Billy, keep your head up. I can’t see shit.”

“I am being struck in it, Christopher.”

Billy drove his shoulder into Chewie’s chest.

The Wookiee hit the landing leg back first. His breath burst out. Before Billy could close his arms around him, Chewie ducked sideways and rammed a heavy fist into the catfish’s bleeding mouth.

Billy bit down on empty air as the hand withdrew.

Chewie snatched his bowcaster from where it had fallen and pulled back toward the ramp.

He saw Han on the floor inside.

The weapon came up toward Kaze.

Billy struck its barrel sideways with the banjo neck. The shot blew a black scar into the apron. He crowded Chewie against the ramp support, forcing the bowcaster across the Wookiee’s own chest.

Chewie let one hand go and clawed for Billy’s face.

Billy shoved the weapon down, stepped around the outside of Chewie’s planted leg and used his free forearm to turn his shoulders.

For one instant, Chewie’s back was exposed.

Billy folded his ring and little fingers, pressed the extended index and middle fingers together, and raised his thumb. He licked the two-finger point.

“Kancho.”

The thrust landed against the center seam of the coveralls.

Chewie’s whole body jolted.

The bowcaster fell. His legs folded beneath him without catching his weight. His face struck the stone, and the sound that came out of him broke halfway through.

Billy stepped back.

Chewie tried to push himself up. His arms shook. Nothing below his waist moved.

Han heard him.

“Chewie?”

He stopped struggling beneath Kaze’s hand.

“Chewie!”

Kaze looked down the ramp. Han shoved again, but this time toward his friend rather than the ship’s controls.

Kaze released him.

Han stumbled down the slope and dropped beside the Wookiee. He rolled him enough to clear his face, supporting his head. Blood threaded the fur around Chewie’s mouth. His breaths came shallow and uneven.

“Hey. Stay with me.”

Chewie’s fingers closed around Han’s sleeve.

His legs remained where they had fallen.

The blow had left him paralyzed below the waist. He was alive, barely, and being alive did not make the damage temporary.

Han looked up at Billy.

For once, he had nothing glib ready.

Billy’s swollen eye watched him over the blood on his own lip. The banjo hung motionless in his hand.

“Get away from him,” Han said.

Billy looked toward Kaze, then moved aside.

Han tried to lift Chewie. The dead weight of the legs dragged them both back down. He caught his friend before his head hit the stone again and shouted at the depot workers behind the pallet stacks.

“I need a cart! A flat one!”

One of them hesitated.

Arthur holstered his revolver and pulled the tile cart toward them. He tipped its load onto the quay, sending roof fragments rattling across the painted arrow.

“This’ll do.”

Han glared at him, then grabbed the cart’s edge.

“Hold it.”

Arthur held it.

Together they shifted Chewie onto the flat bed. Han kept talking to him, low and fast, while a dockworker shoved folded sacks beneath his head.

Kaze came down carrying the power coupling.

Han saw it.

“You’ll never get it off the ground.”

Kaze turned the part over.

“You keep saying that like it’s the same thing as taking it.”

He looked at Billy.

“We taking it?”

Billy adjusted his hat.

“I do enjoy a furnished property.”

“Wait,” Chris said through the banjo. “Let me get a look inside first.”

Han’s eyes flicked from one to the other.

“You can’t fly it.”

“Nobody’s arguing with you,” Kaze said.

The dockworker at the cart handles said the harbor surgeon was two streets inland.

Han looked back at the Falcon.

Then Chewie’s grip weakened on his sleeve.

“Go,” Han told the worker. “Keep it level.”

He walked beside the cart, one hand on his friend, refusing to let it pick up speed over the broken paving.

At the corner he looked back again.

Billy had put both hands on the Falcon’s lower hull.

His jaw opened.

It kept opening.

Han stopped walking.

The ship’s forward edge slid into a mouth that had no right to surround it. The landing gear lifted from the stone. The hull followed, whole, vanishing into the immense wet passage while Billy’s hat remained the same ridiculous size on top of his head.

Seconds later, the apron held scorch marks, a broken trolley and a catfish wiping his chin.

Han stared at the empty space.

The cart moved another foot. Chewie pulled weakly at his sleeve.

Han turned and went with him.

There are moments when a man discovers that every precaution he took was perfectly reasonable and entirely useless because the burglar ate his house.

This was one of those.

Kaze stood where the ramp had been.

“Chris.”

The banjo crackled with his voice.

“I said let me look first.”

“You can look now.”

“It’s parked by my tools.”

Billy beamed.

“Convenient.”

Inside BELLY, the Millennium Falcon rested intact in the oversized storage area, safely on its own landing gear. Its ramp remained down. The old tools and stored belongings were still there around it; Billy had not needed to smash the living room to acquire a garage ornament.

Chris walked beneath the hull, ducked around the ramp support and climbed aboard.

The ship smelled of hot machinery, stale upholstery and repairs performed by people who had been intending to sleep afterward.

He found the open service panel. The disconnected power line was plain enough. Its missing coupling was not something he could replace by admiring it.

Outside, Billy swallowed Kaze carrying the part, then Arthur, depositing them on the dry interior walkway. Artoo wheeled up, inspected Billy’s face, and backed away from the dripping lower lip.

Billy waited.

Artoo gave a long, disapproving whistle.

Then he rolled forward and accepted the same entrance.

Chris heard him coming up the Falcon’s ramp.

“Oh, good. Somebody else can tell me I’m wrong in a language I don’t understand.”

Kaze handed over the coupling.

Chris compared its contacts with the open connection. He followed the adjacent cable to the mounting point, then held up the split collar.

“Well, that little shit knew where to hit it.”

“Can you fix it?”

“The mount. The part inside looks intact. Keep your hands off the panel.”

He carried the coupling to his workbench. Kaze followed, watching him separate the damaged housing from the unbroken connection it had held.

Chris selected metal from his existing salvage, measured against the original and made a replacement support. The work was familiar; the device it supported was not. He kept the original electrical parts together and checked their seating before he returned to the ship.

Kaze picked up the ruined collar.

“He really did fuck this up.”

“On purpose. That's the part of his workmanship I respect.”

Chris fitted the repaired assembly, secured it and checked that the connection stayed seated when the cable moved. The corridor lights returned without flickering. He watched them, then checked the mount again.

“There. Doesn't make me qualified to rebuild the whole ship.”

“I didn't ask you to rebuild the whole ship.”

“You haven't owned it twenty minutes.”

Under the hull, the open maintenance bay still showed why Han had landed. The cooling-line fitting was scorched and its support housing had been destroyed in the fight. Chris inspected the exposed connection, then left that system off. He could repair an understandable mount; he was not going to pretend he had already identified the correct replacement for an unfamiliar flight component.

He climbed back aboard with the damaged power collar in his pocket. He wanted to keep the piece Han had broken.

“Why?” Kaze asked.

“So I can show people what I fixed.”

Kaze looked at the entire stolen spaceship around them.

“You gonna need a smaller story.”

Artoo chirped and extended his probe toward an accessible socket.

Chris stopped watching the light and watched the droid instead.

“You know what you’re doing?”

Artoo turned his dome toward him.

“Fair.”

The little droid connected. A nearby display filled with changing symbols. Chris could see the machine exchanging information; he could not suddenly read all of it.

He left the engines off.

In the cockpit, Kaze dropped into the pilot’s seat and put both hands on the controls.

Chris reached over his shoulder and lifted them off.

“We are inside Billy.”

“I know where we are.”

“Then don’t start the spaceship in the house.”

“I wasn’t starting it.”

“What were you doing?”

Kaze looked at the panel.

“Seeing where everything is.”

“You don’t know what anything is.”

“That’s why I’m looking, nigga.”

Arthur ducked into the cramped passage behind them. His shoulder brushed the padded wall. He turned sideways to let Chris back out, then found Kaze’s leg blocking the other direction.

“One of you gonna move, or did we steal a closet?”

Kaze drew his knee in. Arthur squeezed past, tested the rear seat and shifted his gunbelt clear of the armrest.

Chris looked from the available seats to the narrow doorway.

“Billy is not coming in here.”

“He’s already around it,” Arthur said.

“You know what I mean.”

“I’m trying not to.”

Kaze leaned back, smiling at the controls.

“It’s ours, though.”

Chris took the copilot’s seat. He adjusted it, found the travel limited by a toolbox wedged behind the rail and pulled the box free. The seat slid properly after that. He set the box in the corridor instead of pretending it had ceased to exist.

“We can sit in it,” he said. “That’s where I’m at.”

“You fly the other one.”

“The other one is my car.”

“It becomes a fucking spaceship.”

“That doesn’t make every spaceship my car. You think a helicopter’s just a truck that got nervous?”

Arthur looked through the cockpit glass at the living walls beyond it.

“I ain’t ruling anything out today.”

Chris studied the panel without touching the unfamiliar controls. His expression had changed. The pleasure was there now, beneath the caution: a real ship, real machinery, an entire set of problems he wanted to understand.

“We’ll put it somewhere open,” he said. “Go through it properly. We are not launching this bastard blind.”

Kaze started to answer, then looked at him.

“All right.”

Chris glanced over, almost suspicious of how easily that had gone.

Kaze stood.

“I still want the chair.”

“It comes with the ship.”

“Good. Don’t move it.”

They left the cockpit single file. Arthur had to back out first to make room, carrying the toolbox Chris had left in everybody’s way.

Near the lounge, Daynah had woken to find a spacecraft beyond the storage walkway.

He sat up slowly.

“How long was I asleep?”

Chris came down the ramp.

“We got a ship.”

Daynah looked past him at the hull, then down at Mahnah, who had stolen the warm place behind his back.

“You buy it?”

Arthur shook his head.

“Saved a fortune.”

Daynah rubbed his face.

“Course you did.”

He stood, took a few careful steps as his recovering back loosened, and went to look. At the ramp he stopped beneath the low overhead structure and tilted his head to judge the passage beyond it.

“That’s gonna be a pain in the arse.”

“We’ll work it out,” Chris said.

Daynah pointed toward the couch.

“That already fits.”

He went back to it.

Outside, Billy waited while the depot’s workers decided how far away they wanted to stand.

The answer continued increasing.

He recovered Chewie’s fallen bowcaster and handed it to the dockworker who had stayed behind.

“For the gentleman.”

The worker took it with both hands and no reply.

Han’s blaster still lay beneath the spot where the hull had been. Billy left that too.

He had taken the larger item.

Chris asked to come out. Billy yielded through the shared body’s opening, folding inward as Chris became the sole exterior figure on the apron.

The worker looked from the catfish’s vanished face to the much smaller man standing in its place.

Chris pulled his cowl straight.

“He’s done shopping.”

Billy’s voice came from the speaker in Chris’s mask assembly.

“For the moment.”

Chris returned to the Subaru and recalled it into its Pokéball, damage and all. Artoo remained inside with the Falcon by choice. Kaze and Arthur stayed in BELLY too, arguing over whether the curved lounge seat was comfortable or merely difficult to get out of.

Chris put the ball away and walked inland.

No engines. No getaway driver. Just a man carrying his car while his roommate carried a freighter.

Inside, Billy approached the Falcon’s ramp and tried to follow the others aboard.

His hat struck first.

He took it off.

His shoulders stopped him next.

Billy stood there, holding the hat against his chest, considering the doorway with deep personal disappointment.

“Christopher.”

“What?”

“This home has been built for cowards.”

Chris kept walking.

Behind him, far down the quay, the painted arrow still pointed toward the empty landing apron.

For the first time all morning, it was giving excellent advice.
