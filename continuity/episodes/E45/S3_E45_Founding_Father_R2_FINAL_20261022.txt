Season 3, Episode 45: “Founding Father”

“I fucking hate him.”

Clifford kept looking down the road.

Beside him, Brian sat against an empty poultry container, his little boots planted wide to stop the cart's movement throwing him sideways.

“He wasn't even there,” Brian continued. “Was he there when I named the organization?”

“No.”

“Exactly. Then he turns up in a submarine and everybody starts acting like he invented leadership.”

Clifford adjusted the covered roast between the containers.

Brian watched him.

“You're taking better care of his chicken than you are of me.”

“Chicken didn't open itself.”

Brian turned away.

The wheels carried them toward the coast through the dark. Behind them lay the poultry shop, the closed gate, and several witnesses to Brian's attempt to feed Dr. Evil's complimentary dinner to Mahnuh.

He'd had the whole return journey to improve his explanation.

So far he'd improved it into six explanations that couldn't survive being said in the same room.

By morning, he'd selected sabotage assessment.

By the time he saw Dr. Evil, he'd decided to wait and hear what Clifford said first.

Dutch met the cart at the berth. He received the five silver from the successful delivery, keeping them separate from the older company purse. Then Clifford handed him Arthur's folded reply.

Dutch read it.

For a moment, the coast and its problems had to wait outside his face.

He folded the paper carefully.

“He's well?” Clifford asked.

“He says he is.”

“Good.”

Brian looked between them.

“Can we go inside? I've been sleeping against a cage.”

Dutch put Arthur's letter away.

“How did the delivery go?”

“Stock arrived,” Clifford said. “Client paid. Asked about doing it again.”

Dutch nodded.

“And the chicken?”

Clifford looked down at Brian.

Brian smiled without pleasure.

“There's a technical component.”

“Of course there is,” Dutch said.

Dr. Evil was waiting in the submarine's briefing room when they brought the covered dish aboard.

He let the roast be set before him. Lifted the cover. Breathed in.

Then he looked toward the doorway.

“Excellent. Proof of a satisfied client.”

Brian stepped forward.

Dr. Evil glanced down.

“And Brian.”

Dutch pulled out a chair. Clifford brought his own into position. Brian climbed onto a low storage box beside the table, getting his eyes above its edge.

Dr. Evil watched the final little scramble.

“We have chairs.”

“I can't see over the table from the chairs.”

“Neither can I see the floor when you speak from it. Yet I have adapted.”

Brian dragged his notebook onto the tabletop.

The doctor cut a piece of chicken.

“Report.”

Clifford gave it to him. The actual transfer. The rusted chain. The clear space before he used the lightsaber. The gate. The payment.

Dr. Evil nodded appreciatively.

“Very good, Clifford.”

Brian leaned forward.

“The receiving precautions were my—”

“I'm aware you attended.”

“I designed the precautions.”

“Then you have also done very well.”

Brian stopped.

The doctor ate another piece.

“At a smaller portion of the task.”

There it was.

Brian's smile went away.

Clifford finished.

“Then he opened your dish and called the dog.”

Dr. Evil's knife stopped moving.

He looked at the roast.

Then at Brian.

“This dish.”

“A vulnerability assessment.”

“My dish.”

“The cover was unsecured. I demonstrated that an interested animal could compromise—”

“My chicken.”

Brian took a breath.

“You're focusing on the object.”

Dr. Evil set down his knife.

“Yes, Brian. Because you attempted to feed the object to a dog. I find the object central to the discussion.”

Dutch sat back, letting Brian's chosen explanation reach the place it had been heading all night.

“Did the dog eat any?” the doctor asked.

“No,” Clifford said. “Chris shut the gate.”

“Chris.”

“Yes.”

Dr. Evil turned to Dutch.

“Please make a note. The man who isn't employed here has protected my lunch from the man who is.”

Brian pushed his notebook forward.

“The same man who created your security arrangement.”

Dr. Evil looked at the notebook.

“Did you include a section about you?”

“No.”

“An oversight.”

Brian began talking faster.

“I identified an access weakness. It was a live scenario. If you send somebody into a club to test the sound, you don't scream because he touches the fucking mixer. You let him demonstrate where the feedback is.”

Dr. Evil waited.

Brian pointed at the roast.

“That's the mixer.”

The doctor looked at it.

“I don't want to hear your chicken album.”

Clifford lowered his head.

Brian caught the movement.

“You can laugh. It's fine.”

“Thank you,” Dr. Evil said.

He laughed once, deliberately, directly at Brian.

Then resumed cutting.

Brian's fingers tightened around his pencil until the wood creaked.

“Nobody listened when I said the transfer worked because of what I told them.”

Dutch spoke at last.

“It was useful advice. I'll record it.”

“Oh, you'll record it.” Brian looked toward the doctor. “He gets a meal. I get a fucking footnote.”

Dr. Evil inspected a piece of skin on his fork.

“Would you like some?”

Brian hesitated.

“Yes.”

The doctor ate it.

“I'm sorry. I meant the footnote.”

Dutch closed his eyes briefly.

He'd ridden with murderers who needed less supervision at breakfast.

Dr. Evil reached for a small plate and put a piece of chicken on it. He added gravy, considered the amount, and removed a little with the spoon.

Brian watched every movement.

The plate slid across the table.

“There. A portion proportionate to your involvement.”

“I didn't ask for a children's meal.”

“Neither did the dog. Apparently you make these decisions for everyone.”

Brian shoved the plate back.

Dr. Evil caught it before the gravy spilled.

His expression changed.

“Careful.”

It was quiet. Completely without the little performance.

Brian took his hands off the plate.

The doctor kept looking at him for another second.

Then smiled again.

“Now. You won't be attending the next delivery.”

“What?”

“You've done so much to test our precautions. I should hate to exhaust you.”

“You're punishing me for finding a weakness.”

“I'm separating the weakness from the poultry.”

Brian stood on the box.

“I founded this organization.”

Dr. Evil looked him up and down.

“And now you're standing on it.”

Brian looked at the box.

He stepped off it immediately.

Which put his eyes below the table.

Dr. Evil waited.

Brian climbed back on.

“You are such a fucking asshole.”

“Yes. And I have a submarine. Imagine if I'd also been pleasant. You wouldn't have a single criticism.”

Dutch leaned forward before Brian could throw his entire body into the gravy.

“He can review the next arrangement here. Put his recommendations in writing. His name attached.”

Dr. Evil considered it.

“Very well.”

Brian looked at Dutch.

The doctor pointed his fork at him.

“But no food handling.”

“Fine.”

“No animal testing.”

“I wasn't—”

“No experimental chicken music.”

“That's not a fucking thing.”

“Then this should be an easy restriction to observe.”

Brian took his pencil again.

Dr. Evil moved the plate back within his reach.

“You may eat while you work.”

Brian glared at the chicken.

Then ate it as though the bird had personally sold him out.

The crew came in after the meal had been cleared. The two men who'd accompanied Clifford brought their transfer notes. Dutch moved the money aside and laid out the paper.

Brian had written three recommendations in the time Dr. Evil had taken to enjoy telling him he couldn't be trusted near a duck.

Keep the receiving gate closed until the cart was secured. Keep covered dishes separate from empty returns. Establish the actual load before promising a repeat price.

At the top he'd written his name.

Underlined it.

Then put a second line beneath the first in case the first failed to convey the full scope of Brian.

Dr. Evil held up the sheet.

“Who prepared this?”

Brian stared at him.

The doctor turned the paper over, checking the back.

Dutch took it gently from his hand.

“Brian's recommendations. We'll use them when we negotiate another run.”

One of the crewmen nodded.

Brian settled slightly on his box.

Actual listeners. Actual useful information. For a moment he had a surface on which to stand that wasn't packing material.

He explained the gate issue clearly.

Then the covered dishes.

Then he began explaining why this was fundamentally the same problem as managing a crowded dance floor with an inadequately isolated sound booth.

Dutch's pen slowed.

“The load,” he prompted.

“I'm connecting the principles.”

Dr. Evil put one hand up.

Brian stopped.

The doctor looked at the crewmen.

“You see? It can be done.”

Brian's jaw shifted.

“I was going to say that live birds require different handling from feed.”

“Then we have all been spared the nightclub.”

One crewman coughed into his hand.

Brian pointed at the three lines.

“Those are good recommendations.”

“Yes,” Dr. Evil said. “We are using them.”

That took some of the force out of Brian's next breath.

The doctor leaned back.

“You see how little speaking was required to establish that?”

There it went again.

Dutch turned the page before Brian could retrieve the argument.

The next delivery remained a possibility. No new price had been agreed. The crew needed to know what the merchant actually wanted moved before offering another run.

One man asked whether the empty cart should return by the same road.

Dutch marked the known route.

“Unless you learn a reason to change it.”

Dr. Evil put a finger beside the mark.

“And nobody offers a lower price without consulting me.”

Brian looked up.

“You haven't set a price.”

“Then they should find lowering it particularly difficult.”

The meeting moved on.

And Brian, who had been preparing to explain why Dr. Evil was an incompetent parasite on somebody else's enterprise, watched the room obey him.

No ray gun. No shouted threat.

A finger on a map and the assumption that people would wait for his decision.

It irritated Brian more than the chicken.

He knew how to make a room continue hearing him. He could fill every silence until somebody got tired enough to surrender.

The bald bastard could make one, then leave everybody else responsible for it.

Brian hated that too.

When the crewmen left, he remained beside the table.

Dr. Evil noticed.

“Was there something else?”

Brian pulled his notebook closer.

“The name.”

The doctor looked at Dutch.

“Oh, good. We had nearly completed something.”

Dutch stayed where he was.

Brian had seen the edge of the trademark drawing beneath a stack of briefing papers. He reached toward it.

Dr. Evil placed his hand on the stack.

Brian stopped with his little arm extended.

“You said we'd discuss it.”

“We are.”

“You keep saying we're discussing it while you're putting things on top of it.”

The doctor lifted his hand.

Brian pulled out the drawing.

Tampon Pandemic Productions sat inside Dr. Evil's proposed design, looking far more expensive than the circumstances under which Brian had originated it.

He hated that the lettering was good.

He hated the room for containing enough light to see that.

“I came up with this.”

“Yes.”

“All of it.”

“The name.”

“The concept.”

Dr. Evil looked around the submarine.

“Which part of the concept was watertight?”

Brian slapped the drawing.

“You know exactly what I mean.”

“I'm trying to establish whether I owe you for the torpedo tubes.”

“This isn't about your fucking submarine.”

“Then why are we having it in my fucking submarine?”

Dutch watched Brian absorb that.

The doctor had used almost the same tone as before. A little less ceremony. A little more ownership.

Brian looked toward the door and the men who had gone through it.

Then back at the drawing.

“I want the founding credit. And I want to be consulted before you put my name idea on anything.”

Dr. Evil steepled his fingers.

Then one hand came away, the little finger resting beside his mouth while he considered how much longer he wanted Brian to stand there.

Brian watched the hand.

It was an absurd thing to do in a discussion about somebody else's founding contribution. Yet somehow the doctor made it look as though the discussion had been brought here to entertain that particular finger.

Brian looked back at the drawing before the doctor caught him looking.

“Anything.”

“Anything representing the organization.”

The doctor drew the paper nearer.

“A letter?”

“If it's official.”

“A container?”

“Yes.”

“Your box?”

Brian looked down.

Dr. Evil was already smiling.

“We could put your founding credit on the side. Wherever you go, there it is. You could carry it to meetings.”

“I'm not carrying a fucking plaque that I have to stand on.”

“Then we agree it should remain here.”

Dutch set his pen down.

“We can acknowledge the contribution without settling the whole question today.”

Dr. Evil turned toward him.

“I have acknowledged it.”

“You offered to write it on a box.”

“In a prominent location.”

Brian crossed his arms.

“Why can't you just say I did something good without making me regret hearing it?”

The doctor regarded him.

For a second the argument had no stationery in it.

Then Dr. Evil looked at Dutch.

“Is this still the trademark discussion?”

Brian reached for his notebook.

“Forget it.”

Dr. Evil put one finger on the drawing before Brian could take that too.

“You may bring proposed terms. I will consider them.”

“That's what you said before.”

“And you have arrived with feelings.”

“I arrived with the company.”

“On a cart. With my chicken. Which had a more difficult journey than anticipated.”

Brian stared up at him.

The doctor leaned slightly over the table.

“Write. Them. Down.”

Brian picked up his notebook so hard that the pencil rolled off.

It struck the deck.

He looked at it.

Dr. Evil looked at it.

Nobody moved.

Eventually Brian climbed down and retrieved it himself.

Behind him, the doctor asked Dutch whether they had a smaller pencil.

“This one's fine,” Brian snapped.

“I was asking Dutch.”

Brian went out.

I would love to tell you the door slammed. It was a heavy submarine door, and he couldn't reach enough of it to make the statement he wanted.

He dragged it partway closed.

It stopped.

Dr. Evil waited until Brian had gone a few steps down the passage.

“Open or closed, Brian.”

The footsteps stopped.

Came back.

The door finally closed.

Dutch looked at the doctor.

“You work rather hard at that.”

Dr. Evil picked up the drawing.

“He's very responsive.”

Brian took his notebook to the small work space he'd been given. He set it down, climbed onto the seat and began writing terms.

Founding recognition.

Consultation over use of the name.

Authority over creative direction.

He stared at the third line.

Then added, in larger writing, independent authority.

That looked better.

The seam beneath his shirt moved once against the cloth.

His hand stopped.

He held still until the little pull passed, then pressed the fabric flat and returned to the page.

He didn't write about Tracy.

He wrote about credit.

He was still on the first page when two crewmen brought the empty returns down the passage outside.

Brian heard a container scrape the doorway.

“Not there,” he called.

The scraping stopped.

He climbed down and went out.

The men had stacked the empty containers beside an open store compartment. One of the folding frames leaned over the lower shelf, making the whole arrangement look closer to finished than it was.

Brian pointed toward it.

“You need to secure that first.”

“We're moving it,” one man said.

“I'm telling you how.”

The man looked at his partner.

Brian heard the silence and recognized it as the wrong kind.

“I've just reviewed the entire transfer procedure.”

“These are empty.”

“An empty thing can still fall on somebody. That's not an advanced concept. I don't know why we're all pretending weight becomes imaginary after the chicken gets out.”

The nearer man took hold of the upper frame.

Brian stepped closer to demonstrate where it should go.

The man's boot came backward toward him.

Brian darted sideways.

“Watch where you're fucking standing.”

The crewman turned with the frame still in his hands.

Its lower edge knocked the leaning one loose.

Brian saw it coming and moved. His new legs caught the deck awkwardly, one knee folding beneath him as he got clear.

The frame struck metal beside his foot.

Both men stopped.

So did the conversation at the far end of the passage.

Dr. Evil stood in the briefing-room doorway.

“Put it down.”

The man holding the upper frame began to lower it.

“Both of you.”

The other man took the far side. Together they set it flat against the bulkhead.

The doctor waited until their hands were free.

“One carries. One watches the route. If the route contains Brian, move Brian.”

Brian pushed himself upright.

“I'm standing right here.”

“We've located you. Thank you.”

The nearer crewman bent toward him.

Brian slapped the approaching hand away.

“Don't pick me up.”

The doctor raised a finger.

The man straightened.

“Very well. Brian will move Brian.”

“I was trying to tell them the same fucking thing.”

“Then apparently the route contains two problems.”

Brian looked up at him.

“They nearly dropped that on me.”

Dr. Evil's gaze moved to the fallen frame.

Then to the crewmen.

Neither man tried explaining that it was empty.

“You will secure it properly,” the doctor said. “I don't want to come out here again and find somebody flattened beneath our returns. It makes the whole operation look reversible.”

The men took the frame together.

Brian stepped out of their path under his own power. The leg held. He didn't thank anybody.

Dr. Evil watched him flex the knee once.

“Hurt?”

“No.”

“Good. Then we'll retain the box at its present height.”

Brian's head came up.

The doctor went back into the briefing room.

The crewmen waited until Brian was clear before moving the next container.

He watched them take it through the doorway. One carrying. One watching.

Exactly what he'd wanted done.

He returned to his notebook and sat down harder than necessary.

For a moment he could still hear the way Dr. Evil had said put it down.

No preamble about experience. No demonstration of superior knowledge. He hadn't even needed to tell them why he was entitled to be angry.

Brian picked up the pencil.

Then put it down again.

He didn't want to learn anything from that prick.

By the time Clifford came past, the first page contained enough underlining to suggest an emergency beneath every sentence.

“How's the work?” Clifford asked.

“He's deliberately misunderstanding me.”

“Probably.”

Brian looked up.

“You notice it too.”

“Hard to miss.”

“And nobody says anything.”

Clifford considered that.

“You do.”

Brian waited for the rest.

There wasn't any.

He looked back at the notebook.

“He likes you.”

Clifford's hand rested near the chair's wheel. The gifted lightsaber stayed secured to its frame.

“Gave me something useful.”

“Exactly.”

“Doesn't mean he knows me.”

Brian rubbed a thumb across the pencil's end.

From farther down the passage came Dr. Evil's voice, asking a crewman why a document described him as a passenger.

The crewman explained that it was a berth record.

Dr. Evil asked whether the berth had noticed whose submarine it was.

Brian listened.

Then made a small sound through his nose.

Clifford caught it.

Brian looked up sharply.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was stupid.”

“All right.”

Brian turned a page.

“It was a stupid thing to say to somebody doing a berth record.”

Clifford nodded.

“You listened close.”

“I could hear him.”

“So could I.”

Clifford began to move on.

Brian stopped him.

“How does he do that?”

The chair stopped.

“What?”

Brian waved toward the passage.

“People tell me to shut up. He says something fucking ridiculous and they change the document.”

Clifford looked toward the briefing room.

“Owns the boat.”

“It's not just the boat.”

The admission annoyed Brian as soon as he heard it.

He put his pencil down.

“I mean, obviously the boat's a large part of it.”

Clifford looked back at him.

“Careful. You'll start looking up to him.”

Brian's head came up.

Clifford watched it happen.

“Already got the angle.”

“Fuck off.”

The chair continued down the passage.

Brian stayed over the notebook.

For a while he wrote nothing.

Then he crossed out the paragraph in which he'd explained the entire history of choosing Tampon Pandemic Productions.

Dr. Evil already knew who had named it.

That wasn't where the argument was.

Brian pulled the paper close and began again.

When he returned to the briefing room, he carried one sheet.

Dutch remained at the table, sorting the transfer notes. Dr. Evil had the trademark drawing turned toward himself.

Brian climbed onto his box without discussing it.

Set down the sheet.

“Founding credit. Consultation on the name. Actual authority over creative work.”

The doctor glanced at the paper.

“Much shorter.”

Brian kept his mouth closed.

Dr. Evil waited for the extra explanation.

It didn't come.

He looked at Brian.

Brian looked back.

Dutch took the page.

“These we can discuss.”

The doctor extended a hand for it. Dutch passed it over.

Dr. Evil read the three requests.

“Define creative work.”

Brian's little finger lifted toward the corner of his mouth.

He caught it halfway.

Closed the hand around his pencil instead.

“Presentation. The name. How people hear about us. Things I'm already contributing to.”

“Chicken acoustics.”

“Keep making that joke. It was nearly funny the first three times.”

Dutch looked down at the transfer notes.

Dr. Evil's eyes narrowed.

Brian held his gaze.

There he was again. Same mouth. Same offended little bastard who'd spent a night trying to rename sabotage until it sounded employable.

The doctor laid the paper beside the drawing.

“I will consider the first two. The third requires considerably more definition.”

“Then consider them.”

Brian stepped off the box.

Dr. Evil watched him collect his notebook.

“Brian.”

He stopped.

The doctor waited until he looked up.

“Your recommendations on the transfer were useful.”

Brian's face changed before he could prevent it.

Dr. Evil saw.

Let him have half a second.

“Try to submit the next set without a confession attached.”

Brian's face changed back.

“Go fuck yourself.”

He went out.

This time he left the door open.

Dr. Evil looked toward it.

Then at Dutch.

Dutch kept sorting papers.

“I'm not closing it for you.”

The doctor sat very still.

Out in the passage, Brian stopped beside a polished metal panel. He could see himself in it if he stood close enough.

He adjusted his collar.

Lifted his chin.

Considered the result.

Then pulled the collar back down and went to finish his work.
