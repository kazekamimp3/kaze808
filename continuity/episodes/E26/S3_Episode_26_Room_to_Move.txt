Season 3, Episode 26 — Room to Move

The landlord took the cup away before Kaze could throw again.

“You've won,” he said.

Samira looked at the empty place on the bench. “He hasn't.”

“He's won the opportunity to stop.”

I thought it a sensible prize. The Copper Cup was a roadside inn in the Noxian hills, and possessed remarkably few cups for a business willing to put one on its sign.

Briar picked up the last unbroken target and handed it over. Then she caught Kaze's wrist and pulled him toward the stairs. One of the two rooms Kaze had paid for upstairs was hers. Having her own key had pleased her almost as much as the supper.

She stopped outside her door.

“I'm keeping this room.”

“Keep it.”

“You can visit.”

Kaze smiled. “Was hoping you'd say that.”

She let him kiss her, then pressed a finger against his chest.

“Knock.”

The door shut in his face.

He knocked immediately.

From inside came a delighted laugh.

She opened the door. They talked until the laughter gave way to yawns, then fell asleep beside each other with the window open to the cool air.

By first light, the yard smelled of damp leather and frying onions. Kaze came downstairs behind Briar and returned her key. Samira's was already on the counter. Outside, she stood beside Orren's wagon, testing the step with her boot before climbing aboard. Her left wrist remained braced. A night's sleep had done nothing miraculous for a broken bone.

Orren held the rail steady for her. He was a broad, weathered carrier with metalwork packed beneath his wagon cover and an excellent understanding of what he had agreed to carry.

“One passenger,” he told Kaze.

“I'm admiring your wagon.”

“Admire it from the ground.”

Samira settled beside the driver's bench. She had traded him one ordinary pistol for that seat and the delivery of a letter to the makers of her stolen weapons. The letter rested in his leather packet beneath his coat. The pistol was locked in a box at the rear. Neither transaction had restored the beautiful guns Arthur had taken from her.

She still wore her remaining pistol and knife.

“Lask is the next market town,” Orren said. “Western gate. If you follow the road, you cannot miss it.”

“People say that before giving terrible directions,” Briar said.

“Then follow the wagon.”

Kaze leaned against the wheel. “We might get there first.”

Samira glanced at the torn knee of his trousers. “Try arriving with something left to wear. I have seen the current selection.”

“You had a good look.”

“I am an observant woman.”

She smiled, and there was warmth in it. Her grievance against Arthur hadn't vanished with that smile. Kaze's loyalty to him hadn't vanished either. They had discovered other things to enjoy while that ugly fact sat between them.

Orren gathered the reins.

Another cart rattled into the yard before he could leave. Its driver pulled up so abruptly that an empty basket bounced out of the back.

“You.”

The man pointing at Kaze wore a leather apron whitened by stone dust.

Kaze looked him over. “Morning.”

I remembered him. He'd been the foreman at the quarry where Kaze had fought Baldur, another dangerous stranger in a country already managing a generous supply. The foreman had dragged his workers clear before Kaze dropped half an excavation on the man he'd been fighting.

Apparently, he'd found time since then to become very fucking angry.

“Do you know what you've done to my quarry?”

“Made it deeper?”

The foreman's face changed color.

A second man came through the yard entrance on foot.

For a moment, I forgot the foreman entirely.

Kratos.

Ash-pale skin. The red marking across his head. The beard, the scars, the great axe behind his shoulder. I knew that man. I'd known his silence in grief and his silence before violence, and I had been grateful for his company more often than I'd ever successfully told him.

Seeing him walk into this yard hurt in a place surprise alone doesn't reach.

He carried no familiar companion at his belt. There was no boy beside him. Only his weapons, worn armor, and the expression of someone who had followed unwelcome news to an increasingly unwelcome destination.

The foreman turned toward him.

“That's the bastard. The one I told you about on the road.”

He flung an arm toward the landlord. “I came to hire wagons, and here he is. Eating breakfast while my men pick their tools out of a hillside.”

Kratos looked at Kaze, then at Briar's red pillory, then back to the foreman.

The foreman jabbed a thumb toward Kaze. “Ask him where your man is.”

Kaze straightened away from the wheel.

Before either stranger spoke, something in him had already paid attention. His danger sense didn't provide a name or a history. It registered what stood close enough to hurt him. The quiet man in the yard had substance behind his quiet.

Kaze's smile changed.

“Who you looking for?”

“A tattooed man. Thin. Bare-chested. He called himself Baldur.”

“Yeah. Met him.”

“Where?”

“Under the quarry when we left,” Briar said. “He was coming out again.”

“Coming out?” the foreman snapped. “He pulled through a retaining wall. We couldn't get near him. Then he walked away while I was still counting my men.”

That was news to Kaze. His eyebrows rose.

“Which way?” Kratos asked.

“Up through the scrub. I didn't follow.”

“You saw him leave?”

“With these eyes.”

Kratos took the answer seriously. He asked which side of the quarry, which ridge, whether Baldur had spoken. The foreman gave him landmarks and said the stranger had demanded to know where the man with the black sword had gone.

Briar looked at Kaze.

“He noticed.”

“Hard not to.”

Orren cleared his throat. “I'm moving the wagon.”

Kaze stepped clear of the wheel. That was all Orren needed. He clicked to the horses and took his paid passenger through the gate.

Samira twisted in her seat.

“Western gate,” she reminded Kaze. Then, with a glance at Kratos: “You look occupied.”

“For a minute.”

“Of course.”

The wagon turned onto the road. Her laughter went with it.

Kratos started after the foreman's pointing hand, toward the opposite road.

Kaze moved into his path.

“You didn't tell me your name.”

“Kratos.”

“Kaze.”

Kratos waited.

“You know Baldur?”

“Yes.”

“You fight like him?”

“Move.”

Now, I had heard that particular word from him in enough circumstances to know it rarely benefited from a second request.

Kaze heard an invitation nobody had given him.

“I want to find out.”

Kratos stepped around him.

Kaze caught his shoulder.

The response came without a drawn weapon. Kratos turned inside the reaching arm, caught Kaze's wrist and drove his other palm against the elbow, folding the arm into a lock as he stepped behind him.

Kaze's body had begun shifting before the hold closed. His right eye tracked the turn; his shoulder rolled with it. He let the pressure come far enough to feel its strength, then planted his feet.

The yard stones cracked beneath them.

Kratos stopped applying pressure.

“Enough,” he said.

Kaze looked back at him.

“Nah. Just started.”

He drove his free elbow backward. Kratos released the wrist and checked the strike with his forearm. The impact jolted both men apart and broke the nearby water trough off its stand.

Water slapped across the foreman's boots.

The landlord shouted something about his fucking trough.

Kratos looked beyond Kaze, toward the doorway full of people.

Then he walked out of the yard.

Kaze followed.

Briar caught his wrist.

“You just slept.”

“I'm awake now.”

“I noticed.”

She let go. Her expression wasn't permission or alarm. It was the look she gave an interesting animal shortly before discovering which end bit.

Across the road, an abandoned vineyard climbed the slope in three broad terraces. Dead vines wound around leaning posts. Waist-high stone walls held the levels apart. At the bottom sat a roofless winepress, its circular stone basin half full of leaves.

Kratos crossed the roadside ditch and stopped on the lowest terrace. Behind him, nobody stood between the walls.

Kaze stepped onto the grass opposite him.

“Had to find somewhere private?”

“The people at that inn have done nothing to you.”

“Neither have you.”

“Then leave.”

Kaze grinned. Gold caught the morning light across his upper teeth.

“Nah. I like you.”

He punched Kratos in the chest.

This time there was no tentative contact. Kaze drove from the ground through his hip and shoulder, and the blow carried the larger man backward into the first terrace wall. Stone burst behind him. He went through it standing, boots cutting twin furrows in the earth beyond.

Kaze was already coming through the opening.

Kratos met him with a shield.

It unfolded from his left forearm just before impact. Kaze's next punch struck its face. Kratos angled the blow past his shoulder, stepped inside the extended arm and rammed the shield's rim beneath Kaze's jaw.

Kaze's head snapped back.

Kratos caught him behind the neck and drove him face first into the broken wall.

The remaining stones folded.

Kaze came through the falling rubble laughing, black blood gathering at his lower lip. His left hand closed on Kratos's harness. He pulled the man close and drove a knee into his ribs.

Kratos grunted. Kaze felt it through the grip.

“There you are.”

He tried another knee. Kratos caught the leg, lifted and turned, throwing him across the lower terrace toward the winepress.

Kaze hit its outer rim feet first.

Stone cracked beneath his shoes. His knees folded, took the force, then released it. He launched straight back along the line he'd come, leaving half the basin's rim falling into the leaves.

Kratos had time to brace.

Kaze hit him shoulder first anyway.

They tore through the second wall together and ploughed into the slope beyond. Earth climbed around their bodies. A row of vine posts toppled toward the hollow they'd made.

For a breath, all I could see was dust.

Then Kaze rose out of it with Kratos's fist already in his stomach.

The next blow caught his cheek. The third he caught on the black living armor along his right arm. He shoved the punch aside and struck Kratos across the mouth.

Red blood marked the beard.

Kaze's split lip was already drawing shut.

Kratos saw both things. His eyes moved from the healed mouth to the black blood still stuck beneath it.

“You recover.”

“Fast.”

“That does not make this wise.”

“Wasn't trying to be wise.”

Kratos's hand went over his shoulder.

The axe came free.

Leviathan. I'd seen that blade split armor, frozen timber, and the plans of men who had mistaken a quiet traveler for an easy target. Frost gathered along its edge as Kratos brought it down.

Kaze's warning instinct tightened before the swing arrived.

Black fibers gathered at his left hip. His right hand crossed his body and drew a long, narrow sword from the sheath they formed. Black Marrow's irregular body glowed faintly crimson between its ribs. The moment it cleared, the sheath sank back into him.

Sword met axe above his head.

The impact drove his feet deeper into the slope.

Kaze tilted the living blade, letting the axe slide away from his face. He struck with his empty hand. Kratos turned enough that the punch grazed his jaw, then shoved the axe haft across Kaze's chest and forced him back down the incline.

Briar had crossed to the far end of the lowest terrace. She climbed onto an old gatepost, well clear of their line, and tucked her feet beneath her.

“I liked that turn,” she called.

Kaze glanced her way.

“Which one?”

Kratos threw the axe.

Kaze ducked. It struck the upper terrace wall behind him, burying itself almost to the haft.

“His,” Briar said.

Kratos closed while Kaze was still low. A shield blow drove his sword arm outward. Another punch folded him at the waist.

Then the warning at his back changed.

The axe had left the wall.

Black Marrow shifted before Kaze consciously ordered the parry. Its living grip pulled against his palm; the blade turned behind his shoulder and struck Leviathan's returning edge.

The axe skipped sideways. Kratos caught it farther out than he'd intended.

Kaze stepped into that widened opening and drove his forehead into the bridge of Kratos's nose.

Black Marrow was as much part of him as the hand holding it. Its response had opened the way for that headbutt, and Kaze was already following through.

Kratos staggered one step. Kaze hooked his ankle and shoved.

The god hit the ground hard enough to send loose stones jumping.

Kaze raised Black Marrow.

Kratos rolled toward him, inside the descending cut. The blade struck earth beside his shoulder. He kicked Kaze's knee, stood into him and drove the axe's haft beneath his chin.

Kaze's head tilted back. Kratos pushed him uphill.

The ground gave way under their feet before either man did.

A broad section of terrace slumped downward, carrying dead vines, fence posts and the remains of the second wall. Briar jumped from her gatepost to the stable ground beyond the vineyard. She landed, turned, and watched the post disappear beneath sliding rubble.

Kaze saw where she'd gone. Then he looked back at Kratos and smiled through the pressure against his throat.

He could have reached for crueller answers. Fire that clung. The cutting power he could send through the sword. He had used that power to bring down the quarry. Here, he wanted the resistance in the hands holding him, wanted to make this particular man stop choosing restraint.

He let Black Marrow flow back through his right hand.

Then he grabbed the axe haft with both hands and pulled Kratos close enough to bite his forehead.

Kratos jerked back before the teeth closed. Kaze's laugh became a grunt as a knee struck his ribs.

“You nasty bastard,” I said, with more familiarity than condemnation. I'd seen gods employ considerably less honest methods.

Kaze twisted sideways under the haft, caught Kratos around the waist and launched.

The terrace burst beneath them.

They rose above the dead vineyard, bodies locked together, the road and inn dropping away below. Kaze had driven them uphill, toward a bare shoulder of rock beyond the top wall.

Kratos drove an elbow into his back.

Kaze held on.

They hit the rock shoulder together.

The sound rolled down the valley. A crack opened across the pale slab, and a sheet of loose earth slid off its edge into the vines below.

Kaze landed over Kratos, one knee planted beside his chest. His fist struck the stone where the god's head had been.

Kratos caught the next punch on his shield and shoved upward.

Kaze rose with it, deliberately, bringing both feet beneath himself. He kicked the shield and used its hard face as a launch point. Kratos slid backward across the broken slab; Kaze flew uphill, struck an exposed boulder with his feet and rebounded.

He came back lower and faster.

Kratos dropped beneath the flying kick.

Kaze's heel split a second boulder farther downslope instead.

He landed in the rolling fragments. Something hot snapped across the space where his chest had been.

A hooked blade bit into the rock beside him.

Kratos had put away the axe. A burning chain ran from his hand to the blade embedded in the shoulder of stone.

He pulled himself across the gap.

Kaze saw the route and moved to meet him. Their fists struck almost together. Kratos's blow turned Kaze's head; Kaze's drove into his breastplate and buckled him sideways.

The chain went slack. Kratos planted a foot and swung its blade low.

Kaze jumped the sweep.

The second chained blade came from above.

His danger sense had warned him. He chose the narrow space inside the cut instead of retreating, turning his right shoulder into it as he struck.

The edge opened that shoulder. Heat seared the wound. His punch still landed beneath Kratos's jaw.

Both men stumbled apart.

Thick black blood oozed down Kaze's arm. Beneath it, the torn structure was already drawing together, charred tissue loosening as living material closed the gap. It wasn't painless. His grin had a harder edge now.

Kratos watched the wound recover.

Kaze watched Kratos wipe blood from his mouth.

“You feel that?” Kaze asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He rushed him again.

Kratos put the blades away and met him bare-handed.

The fury came through the body before the face. He caught Kaze's striking arm, pulled him off balance and hit him once beneath the ribs.

Kaze left the ground.

Kratos caught his ankle before he could land and swung him against the rock.

The slab broke.

Kaze's body struck again, lower, punching through the fractured surface into the packed earth beneath. On the third swing he folded at the waist, seized Kratos's forearm and forced the grip open with both hands.

He dropped beside him, rolled once and came up driving forward.

Kratos struck him head-on.

Kaze blocked with crossed forearms. The impact sent him backward through the broken top wall and down the slope, heels carving two long trenches through the vineyard. He hit a buried foundation stone and stopped with the earth heaped behind his calves.

For a moment, neither man moved.

The violence in Kratos's breathing eased. He stood above the wreckage with blood in his beard and a hand pressed briefly to his side. I'd seen that rage sustain him through terrible things. He let its fierce grip ease now, drawing a slower breath.

Kaze lowered his arms.

A fracture beneath the living armor settled. His right shoulder had closed. Dust clung to the blood on his chest, and another strip of cloth hung from his already ruined knee.

He looked up the slope.

“There he is.”

Kratos looked back without pleasure.

Then he turned and started around the broken terrace toward the road.

Kaze crouched.

Briar watched him choose a launch point. She didn't shout. He hadn't missed where Kratos was going.

He sprang across the ruined ground and landed ahead of the god, cracking the last intact stretch of low wall beneath his feet.

Kratos stopped.

“You got somewhere better to be?” Kaze asked.

“Yes.”

“Baldur.”

“Among other things.”

Kaze studied the blood beneath Kratos's nose. Kratos studied the face that had already repaired itself.

The next blow could have started a different fight. They were close enough. Neither had run out of ways to hurt the other.

“He wanted you to make him feel something,” Kratos said.

Kaze's smile thinned.

“He tell you that?”

“I knew him.”

“You keep saying that.”

Kratos looked toward the road where the foreman stood by his cart.

“I killed him.”

The words changed Kaze's attention more effectively than the last punch had.

“No, you didn't.”

“I did.”

“Then who walked out of the quarry?”

“That is what I intend to learn.”

Kaze glanced down at his own right hand, then back at the stranger.

“How?”

Kratos waited.

“How'd you kill him?”

“His protection was broken.”

“Break it again.”

“You do not know what you are asking.”

“I'm asking what it takes.”

Kratos met his eyes.

“You will not learn it by striking me.”

I could have supplied a history to fill the silence. It would have contained a mother, a son, an accident, and more grief than either man standing in that vineyard had asked to hear. Kaze had heard only what Kratos chose to give him.

He looked back over the ruined terraces.

Then he stepped out of the way.

Kratos passed him slowly, still watching his hands.

“Ask the foreman about the upper track,” Kaze said. “There was an unbroken ridge behind the old cut. That's where we went.”

Kratos stopped at the ditch.

“Do not follow me.”

Kaze bared his teeth.

“Don't make it sound fun.”

Kratos crossed the road.

Kaze stayed where he was.

Briar came down the grass beside the ruined vineyard. She looked at his shoulder, then pressed a thumb against the place the hot blade had opened it. The skin held beneath her touch.

“You got what you wanted?”

“Some of it.”

“He didn't look happy.”

“He wasn't supposed to.”

She considered that, then glanced at the flattened walls.

“I liked the bit where he threw you.”

“Which fucking bit?”

“There were choices.”

He laughed. It came out rough at first, then easier.

Across the road, the foreman was speaking to Kratos again. The landlord had come out to inspect his broken trough. He looked from it to the vineyard and made no attempt to cross toward either fighter.

Kaze reached into his pocket.

He walked back to the yard and put two silver coins on the wet stand.

“For the water thing.”

The landlord stared at them.

“And that?” He pointed across the road.

“Yours?”

“Belongs to a family in Lask.”

“Then those aren't their coins.”

Kaze turned away with six silver left. The landlord swept the two coins into his palm before anybody reconsidered.

I won't pretend the gesture settled a damned thing. There was still a vineyard laid open across the slope, a quarry without a working floor, and a foreman who had now heard Kaze give his name. But the trough had acquired a repair fund. Even generosity arrived in peculiar shapes with this lad.

Kratos left on foot while the foreman stayed by his cart. Neither offered the other company. At the bend, the god looked back once, saw Kaze standing with Briar, and continued toward the hills.

Briar watched until he disappeared.

“Are we going after him?”

Kaze rolled his healed shoulder.

“You still want that market?”

“Yes.”

“Then we're going to the market.”

She took his hand and walked him away from the inn. A little beyond the road fork, a bare shelf rose above the ditch. From there, the road wound across open fields toward a low ridge. Orren's wagon was a moving brown shape well ahead of them.

Briar stepped onto the shelf first.

“Can we go over him?”

“Probably scare the horses.”

She looked at the fields instead.

“Beside him, then.”

Kaze crouched and lifted her, one arm beneath her knees and the other supporting her back. She settled the pillory clear of his jaw, then leaned close enough to kiss the bloodless corner of his mouth.

“That part's better,” she said.

He looked down at her.

“I got options too.”

“Show me one that goes forward.”

He bent his knees.

The shelf cracked under the launch. They rose over the ditch and out across the morning fields, keeping wide of the road. Briar's laugh broke loose as the first hill dropped beneath them.

Kaze chose a bare patch of firm ground for the landing, beyond the wagon's route. He came down with bent knees, ran three steps through the force, and brought her upright without letting go.

Behind them, Orren's horses kept walking.

Samira stood carefully on the wagon step, one hand on the rail, watching the pair ahead.

“A minute?” she called.

Kaze turned, still holding Briar.

“Told you we'd get here first.”

Samira looked from his torn trousers to the dark stains on his chest.

“You should have asked him for clothes.”

Briar laughed so hard that Kaze had to set her down.

The road to Lask lay open in front of them. Behind, above the Copper Cup, dust was still rising from the vineyard.
