Season 3, Episode 51: “Fetch”

Chris had lost six silver, four hinges and the right to describe the afternoon as profitable.

He left the drinking house carrying the little cloth that had held Mahnah's food. The dog followed it through the doorway, still prepared to believe in a second meal despite overwhelming evidence from the first economy.

“I farted,” Chris said.

“Noted.”

“Thanks.”

“Yep.”

Arthur stopped beside the yard gate.

He'd heard precisely half that conversation.

“Who're you thanking?”

“Somebody acknowledged it.”

“Well, I was aiming to let it pass.”

Kaze laughed, then looked past the gate toward absolutely nobody.

“You got a fucking opinion on everything else. That's all he gets?”

What would you prefer? A brass plaque? Christopher Graham Released Air Here. Please Keep This Historic Site Ventilated.

“See,” Chris said. “Now you're doing too much.”

Billy's voice emerged from the projector in Chris's retracted mask assembly, warm and pleased. Billy was full-sized inside BELLY, watching through the headlamp feed, his banjo resting against his knee.

“Christopher has always wanted his work recognized.”

Arthur looked between them.

“I can hear the fish. The other fellow seems to be keeping to himself.”

He couldn't hear me. Neither could Daynah. Kaze, Chris and Billy could answer the commentary; Mahnah had his own silent relationship with the direction an audience ought to be. That did not make this a group telephone call. Arthur was watching three men argue with a radio he hadn't been issued, and the dog had better reception than he did.

“You want the answer?” Kaze asked him.

“To which part?”

Kaze's grin thinned.

He turned that same attention toward me.

“Who killed Daniel?”

The yard stayed a yard. A worker rolled an empty barrel past the barn wall. The mason who'd won their money came through the door, saw the group blocking the gate and went around them.

“Tai Lung,” I said.

Chris stopped folding the cloth.

Kaze didn't move.

“Say it again.”

“Tai Lung killed Daniel.”

Arthur watched Chris's hands go still. Then Kaze's face.

“What?”

“Tai Lung,” Kaze said aloud. “Killed him.”

Daynah's hand settled against Mahnah's shoulder.

The dog stopped trying to reach the cloth.

“The leopard?” Arthur asked.

Kaze nodded once.

“How'd you come by that?”

“He told me.”

Arthur looked at the empty air Kaze had been addressing.

It was an entirely reasonable look. You cannot expect a man to accept murder testimony from the patch of sky above a wheelbarrow just because three of his friends have become very serious about it.

“Rematch,” I said. “The loading yard. Daniel went in. They fought. Tai Lung killed him.”

Kaze listened. Repeated it for the two who couldn't hear.

Billy said nothing from the headgear.

“Where is he now?” Kaze asked.

“I don't know.”

“But you know that.”

“Yes.”

Kaze's jaw shifted.

“Could've said something.”

“I could have.”

No jaunty little excuse came after it.

Chris finished folding the cloth. Too carefully. Corner against corner, then again, until it was a small square that had never needed that much attention.

Arthur stepped out of the gate so the barrel man could pass back through.

“We got a name,” he said. “We ain't got a direction.”

“I know.”

“You were looking at the hills like you might ask one of 'em to move.”

Kaze looked toward him.

“Might.”

Arthur didn't smile.

“Wouldn't tell you which hill.”

That landed. Kaze looked away first.

Daynah rubbed the loose skin beneath Mahnah's collar, the same unhurried movement he'd used when the dog was small enough to fit against him without reorganizing a room.

“Tell me what you're doing before you do it,” he said.

“I haven't done anything.”

“I'm getting my request in early.”

Kaze breathed through his nose. Looked at the road beyond the works.

“Still want Swain.”

“All right,” Arthur said.

“That ain't me forgetting.”

“Didn't say it was.”

Chris put the cloth away.

“If we get a place on the cat, we get a place. We ain't gonna find him by walking angrily in four directions.”

Kaze watched him.

“You picked one?”

“For now. The road you just fucking asked for.”

They went to find the foreman.

He gave Arthur the surviving route beyond the terraces: keep above the old watercourse until the road divided, then take the branch marked by the square stone post. It would carry them toward the capital road, not directly to the Grand General's fucking lap. There were more broken crossings beyond the part he knew. He said so. Arthur asked which ones he had actually seen.

Kaze listened to the useful part.

Then they left the settlement.

For a while, nobody tried to improve the silence.

The afternoon road passed above abandoned furnace channels and descended into a pocket of occupied ground: gardens behind low walls, a little smithy, a chapel with both doors open to the heat. The congregation belonged to a small foreign faith. Noxus had plenty of room for strange beliefs, provided the believers could survive the neighbors and produce something taxable.

This particular faith kept a wooden man nailed to a wooden cross above its altar.

You may already see a problem developing.

“Don't fucking help it,” Kaze said to me.

I haven't touched anything.

“You sound excited.”

That's because I have eyes and your dog has left the road.

Daynah turned immediately.

Mahnah had slipped behind a passing pair of people carrying an empty handcart. He was crossing the chapel's shaded forecourt, nose low. The open double doors were wide enough. This was the full extent of his invitation.

“Mahnah.”

The dog's ears moved.

He went inside.

Daynah started after him.

Chris was closer to the doorway. He stepped aside to see past the nearest pew.

“Oh.”

From the headgear, Billy asked, “What has he found?”

Chris's headlamp feed gave him the answer.

“Oh,” Billy said, with considerably more pleasure.

The service had reached a moment of shared quiet. A man near the front was speaking with his hands folded; everyone else had turned toward the altar. They heard the claws before they saw what the claws belonged to.

A woman looked back.

Mahnah looked at her.

Her hand closed on the shoulder beside her.

The man beside her followed her gaze, then moved so quickly off the bench that his knees took it with him.

The first crash brought the rest of the congregation around.

Mahnah stood taller at the shoulder than several of them. His mouth hung open in the warm room. His tongue showed between teeth built for an entirely different relationship with a sheep than the one currently being discussed at the front.

Somebody said, “Hellhound.”

Somebody else climbed onto a bench.

The priest unfolded his hands.

Mahnah had noticed the cross.

It hung low behind the altar, an enormous dark timber structure taller than Daynah. The lower upright extended beneath the carved man's feet. Old rope and iron brackets held it against the wall.

Mahnah crossed the aisle.

“No,” the priest said.

The dog sniffed the bottom of the upright.

“No.”

I respect a man who tries the same negotiation twice before abandoning theology.

Mahnah opened his jaws around the timber below the figure's feet.

Chris put one hand over his own mouth.

Daynah reached the threshold behind him.

“Move.”

Chris moved.

The first pull stretched the upper rope. The second tore a bracket from its rotten fixing. The cross lurched forward, caught on the remaining restraint, then came free with a bang that sent plaster down behind the altar.

Mahnah stepped backward with it.

The carved man swung toward the aisle.

Three people dropped flat.

The priest caught the altar cloth before the timber could drag it into the candles. He shoved a candlestick away with his forearm and pulled the others clear. No divine intervention. Just a man recognizing that the immediate problem had become fire.

Mahnah adjusted his bite.

One end of the crossbeam struck a pew.

The dog stopped. Turned his head.

The other end struck the pew opposite.

Now he had a puzzle.

Daynah reached the first row of benches as Mahnah backed out of the narrow aisle into the wider entrance space.

“Hold.”

The dog looked toward him, turning the cross at the same time. The far end clipped the doorframe. Mahnah backed another step, raised his chin and tried the opening at an angle.

One end passed through.

Then the rest followed.

He emerged into the forecourt carrying the crucifix like an exceptionally satisfying stick.

Daynah came through behind him.

For one magnificent second, a fourteen-hundred-pound pit bull stood before a terrified congregation with their savior sideways in his mouth, proudly showing the man who'd raised him what he'd found indoors.

Kaze had stopped beside the handcart.

His face changed.

He pressed his lips together.

Daynah looked at him.

“Don't.”

Kaze looked at the wooden man.

At Mahnah.

At Daynah arriving to collect a dog and discovering he'd have to collect a religion with it.

“Nigga.”

That was all he got out before he bent over laughing.

The priest came through the doors behind them.

“Put him down.”

Chris lost the fight with his own face.

“Which one?”

Daynah turned toward him.

Daynah took hold of one end of the crossbeam.

“You wanna carry the other end?”

Chris stopped laughing just long enough to take the weight Daynah indicated. Kaze laughed harder at the immediate promotion.

Mahnah's tail began to move.

“Don't encourage him,” Daynah said.

“I'm holding Jesus. He's slippery as a buttered pool cue. Get the other—yeah, there. We are not dropping the son of God on my fucking shoes.”

“Then hold him still.”

The priest stared at Chris's headgear when Billy spoke from it.

“Well. I haven't seen that face in an age.”

“You know the likeness?”

“I knew the gentleman.”

Chris closed his eyes.

“Billy.”

“What?”

“Read the room.”

“We are outside.”

The priest took a step closer.

“You knew our Lord?”

Billy's voice acquired the leisurely warmth of a host whose guests had finally asked the right question.

“We had a misunderstanding.”

Kaze straightened a little.

“Oh, tell him.”

“Don't tell him,” Chris said.

The priest looked between their faces.

“Who is speaking?”

Chris touched the side of his headgear.

“Friend of mine.”

“Where?”

“Complicated.”

“Not particularly,” Billy said. “You are holding one man and speaking for another. You do enjoy making work for yourself.”

Mahnah shifted his paws.

Daynah placed a hand over the dog's muzzle, keeping fingers clear of the bite, and supported the upright with his other arm.

“Give.”

The jaws relaxed.

The timber dropped its last inch into Daynah's grip.

Mahnah sat.

He looked pleased with the outing.

Chris and Daynah lowered the crucifix carefully against the chapel wall. A deep wet dent marked the upright. The carved man's chin had scraped the floor. One wooden foot had lost its toes.

The priest crouched beside it.

“He brought him back,” Kaze said.

The priest looked up.

Kaze pointed at the dog.

“Technically.”

“Stop helping,” Daynah said.

Billy cleared his throat through the projector.

“I should clarify that when I say I knew him, I mean personally. This isn't one of Christopher's hobbies where he mistakes watching a man for having been invited into his house.”

Chris turned his head toward empty air.

“You hear this?”

Constantly. I'm a narrator trapped between a chef's ass announcement and a fish preparing to explain the Crucifixion from the perspective of the catering department. My career counselor is hanging from a fucking ceiling fan somewhere.

“No,” Billy agreed. “Our business concluded rather differently.”

The priest's mouth opened.

Closed.

He looked as though he'd swallowed a communion wafer and felt it deploy little fucking landing legs.

He settled on Chris.

“What did he say?”

“Nothing you wanna put in a song.”

Kaze crouched beside the damaged cross.

“Ask what happened.”

The priest didn't.

Billy continued anyway.

“I swallowed him.”

The little gathering behind the priest went still.

Chris looked at the sky.

“There it is.”

“The whole man,” Billy added. “A very brief acquaintance. His followers have apparently been more persistent.”

“Blasphemy,” said the woman who'd first spotted Mahnah.

“That was not the difficulty at the time.”

“The man. I left them ample room for interpretation.”

“Our Lord lives.”

Billy's pause was almost courteous.

“Sir.”

“Don't.”

“I am trying to be delicate.”

Kaze made a sound that sent him back against the handcart, laughing into his forearm.

The priest stared at Chris.

“I didn't do it,” Chris said.

“Then stop speaking for him.”

That shut him up more effectively than the accusation.

Billy, who had spent a substantial portion of his life resenting interruption, sounded delighted.

“Now there's a sensible request.”

Daynah had his dog by the collar.

“We're done.”

“I had barely begun,” Billy said.

“Yeah. That's what I'm protecting us from.”

The priest looked at the damage above the altar through his open doors. Then the teeth marks in the cross. His hands were still shaking, but he had stopped trying to hide them.

“Take the animal away.”

Daynah nodded.

“I will.”

“And them.”

“They got their own legs.”

He walked Mahnah toward the road.

Chris followed. Kaze stayed long enough to watch two congregants lift the cross off the ground. They didn't ask him to help. He didn't offer.

The woman who'd said blasphemy was staring at the name across his stomach.

Then she looked past him at Chris.

Recognition came slowly enough to watch it happen.

“The Heron,” she said.

Chris turned.

The priest looked at her.

“The notice at the smithy. Those are the names.”

She backed toward the doors. Not toward the cross. Toward the people inside.

Kaze's amusement subsided into attention.

Across the road, the smith had come out beneath his awning. A notice lay open in one hand. He had been watching the commotion; now he looked from the paper to the tattooed man and the armed man beside him.

He called his apprentice indoors.

An adult at the handcart took its handles and moved away without asking Kaze to let go first. Kaze released the rail.

The smith went inside after the apprentice. They lifted the door bar together.

Chris heard it fall into its brackets.

“Well,” he said.

The chapel bell began to ring.

Not the measured call that had brought people to the service. Fast, uneven strokes. An alarm meant for the few houses within hearing.

The priest hadn't run. He stood beside his damaged cross while somebody inside pulled the rope.

“Go,” he said.

Kaze studied him.

“You asking, or—”

“I'm telling my people to stay away from you.”

The bell kept sounding.

Chris's hand rested near his right hip.

“I'm walking.”

Kaze watched him go.

The priest stayed where he was. No secret weapon emerged from his robe. No god reached through the bell tower to rescue him. He was frightened, and he had made the choice anyway.

Kaze could have killed him.
