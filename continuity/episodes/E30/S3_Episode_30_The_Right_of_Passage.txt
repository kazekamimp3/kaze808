Season 3, Episode 30: The Right of Passage

Morning found Kaze abandoning yesterday's argument.

He left the stones bracing the ballista's wheels, stepped off its drawbar, and looked at the road beyond the harbor shelf. The bound weapon remained where it stood. There was no loading it onto Billy, no offer to solve its ancient problems, and no second negotiation.

Billy had released Daniel from BELLY, and the simian came down on his cloud beside Kaze.

“Your ex is taking this better than expected.”

“That's a fucking siege engine.”

“Exactly. Terrible at communicating. Takes forever to go anywhere. Kind of your type.”

Kaze swung at his shoulder. Daniel ducked and laughed, nearly spilling the amber bottle in his ice hand.

Billy, who had spent the night as the only exterior body, watched them from the edge of the shelf. Below, workmen were still chipping the collapsed harbor gate into pieces small enough to haul away. Kaze had helped create that inconvenience days earlier and felt no sudden urge to assist with the restoration.

“Are we quite finished with the maritime estate sale?” Billy asked.

“Been finished,” Kaze said.

Inside BELLY, Chris heard him through the live exterior feed. He stood at the workbench with a pencil between his teeth, glaring at the carp ornament sitting on the wooden stand he'd made with Kaze.

“He better not bring home a fucking bridge today.”

Arthur, seated beside the stored silver, looked toward the screen. “That's an oddly specific request.”

“Every time I don't specify, I get punished.”

Meatwad tilted the camera toward the carp. Charles moved into the shot before it settled.

“We have discovered the recurring motif of the household,” Charles announced. “Men struggling to respect what they're given.”

Chris took the pencil out of his mouth.

“I have given you absolutely nothing to announce.”

Billy let Arthur out onto the shelf, then yielded the exterior to Chris at that same spot. 
Arthur brushed his hat and surveyed the road. Daniel was already arguing with Kaze about whether they should take the river road marked on Harl's sketch or follow the ridge toward the interior.

“Which way's got people?” Kaze asked.

“Both,” Arthur said. “Which is what roads generally do.”

“I mean people worth talking to.”

“Your standards have been giving us trouble.”

Daniel opened the sketch. “River road's shorter. Has a crossing. I love a crossing. Humanity builds a whole bridge so I can avoid getting my feet wet.”

“You're a fucking monkey with a cloud,” Chris said.

“That's why I can recognize unnecessary infrastructure.”

They chose the river road. There was no grand departure speech. Kaze went ahead on foot, Daniel kept pace by hopping between the rocks above, and Arthur walked alongside Chris until Chris needed the vehicle for the longer stretch. They were headed into Noxus, an empire that liked to measure people by what they could take, hold, build, or survive. Nobody had offered these particular visitors citizenship. 
By midday, the road began to speak before the people did.

An iron hammer rang somewhere beyond the next ridge. Another answered it. From a lower hollow came the shriek of frightened horses and the sharper report of a military whistle.

Arthur slowed. “That ain't ordinary construction.”

On the hill beside him, Daniel flattened his ears and listened. His pale eyes followed the distant road through gaps in the scrub. “There's an entire argument happening down there. Forty people standing still, a bunch trying to move, and one guy floating in the middle like he's allergic to dirt.”

Kaze smiled. “Floating?”

“Yeah. You wanna complain to the manager?”

“Fuck no. I wanna meet him.”

They crested the ridge.

A ravine split the valley below. An old stone road approached from their side and resumed on the far lip, with a deep, fast river running between. The original bridge had lost its central span long before the Sins arrived; weathered stone abutments still pointed at each other across open air.

Something extraordinary occupied that gap now.

Iron plates, rails, braces, and the ribs of several dismantled wagons had been joined into a broad temporary crossing. Not welded. They held one another at angles that should have made a normal engineer spit blood. As Kaze watched, an iron rail rose from the bank and settled into the structure without a hand touching it.

Above the middle of the bridge hovered an older white-haired man in dark, travel-worn clothing. Both arms were raised only slightly. His attention moved between the iron and the people crossing it.

There were families on the bridge, not soldiers: a woman guiding a child around an uneven plate; two men carrying a wounded elder between them; carts piled with bedding and tools. On the near bank stood Noxian infantry in red and iron, a pair of field guns, and a captain beside a wagon loaded with chains. The soldiers weren't attacking yet. They were trying to close a passage while people were still on it.

Chris squinted. “I know that dude.”

Arthur studied the floating stranger. “From where?”

“Not personally. From something that's gonna be real fucking hard to explain.”

Kaze didn't need the explanation. The weight of the man's attention was interesting all by itself.

Below, the captain raised her voice.

“I have given you until the last cart. Then that iron returns to imperial service.”


“You gave them two hours. Your men blocked the road for the first one.”

“Those are imperial materials.”

“Some are abandoned wagons. Others belong to the people crossing.”

“All of it can be requisitioned.”

“Then you should have brought someone capable of taking it.”

The captain's jaw set. Her name, they would learn soon enough, was Sorell. She commanded the nearby road detachment, and her orders concerned a spring military movement that required iron wherever the army could find it. She regarded the crossing as stolen military capacity. The man holding it together regarded it as the only practical path out of the valley.

Neither believed this was a philosophical discussion.

Kaze started down the slope.

“Hold up,” Daniel said. “You saw the two cannons, right?”

“Yep.”

“Floating man's moving metal.”

“Yep.”

“Then you see how fucking stupid the cannons are.”

Kaze glanced over his shoulder. “I'm going to talk to him, nigga.”

Daniel's grin widened. “Sure. And I'm an emotional-support orangutan.”

“Thought you were drunk.”

“Multitasking.”

Arthur stopped beside Chris at the lip of the slope. “You still got the car out?”

Chris glanced at the recalled vehicle's container. “Not deploying it into a war zone unless somebody's paying for the bodywork.”

“Didn't mean the car. Meant a way out if this gets stupid.”

Chris looked at the bridge, the guns and Kaze already walking toward them.

“We brought our own stupid.”

They descended without disguising themselves. The first infantryman spotted Kaze's exposed tattoos and unusual right arm, then noticed the barefoot simian with ice for hands. His spear came down crosswise across the road. His companions shifted their shields forward.

“Civilian approach closed!”

Kaze looked from the spear to the bridge. “I'm not stopping here.”

“Captain's order.”

“Tell her I don't work here.”


Arthur stepped up behind Kaze. “Might help if you stopped pointing that thing at his throat.”

The soldier looked down. “He came straight toward it.”

“And yet there he is, still interested in getting closer.”

Before the soldier could answer, a rail lifted itself out of the mud behind him and passed overhead toward the floating stranger. The infantryman ducked. Kaze followed the rail with his eyes and laughed.

“That's cool as fuck.”

The stranger turned.

For a moment Magneto studied them from above the crossing. He recognized no one. He saw a group with unusual weapons, no insignia, and the unmistakable ease of men who expected to survive violence. 
“You are not with her,” he said.

“Fuck no,” Kaze answered.

“Then stay off the crossing until the families are through.”

Kaze stopped smiling quite so broadly.

“Who're you supposed to be?”

“Erik Lehnsherr. Some call me Magneto.”

Chris took one step closer to Arthur and spoke quietly. “Yep. That's the dude.”

“Magneto,” Kaze repeated. He glanced at the suspended structure. “That name comes with the whole thing, huh?”

“The whole thing?” Magneto asked.

“The metal.”

“It comes with quite a bit more.”

Kaze appreciated the answer. He didn't move onto the bridge.

Captain Sorell looked from the group to Magneto. A runner had already carried word to the other field gun that the stranger's bridge was to be disabled if the captain gave the signal. Her orders gave her authority to reclaim the iron; they hadn't prepared her for a foreigner who could assemble it in the air.

“I need that bridge,” she called to Magneto. “You may keep the road open for your people once my engineers secure it.”

“Your engineers tried to pull the supports while children were standing on them.”

“I stopped them.”

“After I stopped the rail.”

Sorell's expression didn't change. “I won't negotiate in front of armed strangers.”

Kaze turned. “Then quit negotiating.”

She took in his face, then Arthur's gun belt, then Daniel's ice hands. “You have one opportunity to leave my position.”

“I'm trying,” Kaze said, pointing at the bridge. “You're the one doing all this.”

Chris peered at the nearer field gun. It was an ordinary iron-barreled piece on a reinforced carriage, with a firing crew standing beside the wheels. Not magical. Not particularly sophisticated. But it did have a compact sighting assembly he hadn't seen before.

“Look at that adjustment mount,” he muttered.

Arthur saw his expression. “Don't.”

“What?”

“Whatever you're thinking of unbolting.”

“I wasn't gonna unbolt it yet.”

“Comforting.”

Daniel had gone still. His bottle rested against his hip, and the hard ice of his fingers tightened around the neck. Across the road, a team of horses backed away from the wagon carrying chains. Not because of the floating man. A soldier had dragged a terrified young laborer out from behind the wagon and was wrenching him toward the captain.

The boy wasn't in uniform. He was old enough to work the road and young enough to look hopelessly out of place among armed men. He'd cut the binding around two confiscated handcarts, trying to give their owners a way across.

The soldier shouted, “Sabotage!”

Magneto's eyes turned sharply toward the sound. 
Sorell glanced at the prisoner. “Hold him. Do not strike him.”

The soldier obeyed the first part. He yanked the laborer so violently that the boy stumbled against the wagon wheel.

Daniel sighed. “There goes the customer-service award.”

He stepped sideways, his bare foot finding purchase on a rock. A slender seam of ice crawled beneath the soldier's boot and locked it to the ground. Before the man could wrench free, Daniel swung around the wheel, caught the boy by the collar with his tail, and pulled him behind the cart.

“Safety inspection,” Daniel said. “Your floor failed.”

The soldier reached for his sword.

The captain shouted, “Stand down!”

Too late for the man. Daniel's spear haft caught his wrist, knocked the blade sideways, and delivered a compact blow to the chest that threw him back against the wagon. His helmet rang off the iron. He lay breathing, unable to rise immediately.

There was a small and dreadful pause. Everyone saw the result.

Kaze turned toward Daniel. “You started it.”

Daniel swigged his bottle. “Actually I prevented workplace injury. Very different department.”

Sorell drew her sword. “Seize the armed men.”

Arthur's voice went flat. “That would've been better left unsaid.”

Two soldiers advanced, then checked their steps as Arthur's right poncho shifted and the crimson glow beneath his hat caught the light. He had a revolver in each hand now. No dramatic charge, no inexplicable hesitation. He had clear sight of their knees and the dirt beyond.

“Find yourself somewhere else,” he said.

One obeyed. The other lifted his shield and lunged.

Arthur shot him through the exposed sword arm. The round passed out into the slope, tearing earth where no one stood. The soldier dropped the sword and crumpled, alive and screaming. Arthur stepped past him without firing again.

Kaze closed on Sorell. She saw his speed and threw herself behind the chain wagon rather than trading blows, shouting the field-gun signal.

The nearer gun crew had been ready to fire at the supports. Now it swung toward the invaders.

The first round boomed.

Magneto caught the iron shot in the air before it reached the roadside families. The ball hung above the bridge for a heartbeat, spinning, then accelerated backward. It smashed the gun's muzzle and broke the front of its carriage apart. Two crewmen were hurled onto the bank by splintering timber. One did not rise.

The remaining gun turned its barrel toward Magneto.

“Oh, for fuck's sake,” Chris said. “They are absolutely not learning.”

His headlamp's compact indicators tracked the crew, but they didn't fire for him. He reached for THE CRITIC, the large earned rifle carried with his gear, and looked for a clear path past the fleeing families. There wasn't one yet.

Then the second crew stopped trying to aim at Magneto.

They aimed at the bridge's far abutment, where the refugees would have to leave the ironwork. If they could destroy the landing, the floating man would have to choose between holding the span and evacuating the last carts.

Magneto recognized the aim. He could bend the barrel; he could lift the gun; he could spread a shield. But the crossing already bore moving weight, and frightened people were beginning to bunch together. He looked once toward the far bank and made his decision.

“Keep them moving!” he called.

Kaze saw the same line of fire. He didn't wait for a proposal.

Black Marrow formed from the flesh of his right side into his hand, dark and alive, the sword's edge taking shape as he accelerated down the bank. He crossed the distance before the crew completed its firing preparation. The barrel wasn't his target. The men were.

A frightened soldier struck Kaze across the forearm as he passed, opening a shallow cut that oozed thick black blood. Kaze did not stop. His first sword stroke split the gunner near the vent; his second tore through the crew chief before he reached the lanyard. Already, the cut in Kaze's flesh was closing. The sword pulled back into his hand's line, ready, but there was no third living attacker still committed to the gun.

Kaze glanced at the artillery. “Who's next?”

No one answered. One of the surviving soldiers dropped his loading tool and ran.

The shot never fired.

Aye. No sacred sporting interval obliged Kaze to let a man finish preparing his murder. Today patience had missed its carriage.

On the bridge, people began moving again. Magneto lowered the intercepted shot onto the emptied near bank and shifted both arms. An entire section of iron plating rose, allowing the last handcart to clear a buckle in the surface. His expression remained grim. He had not summoned the Sins, and their violence had made his already fragile evacuation harder to control.

Sorell burst from behind the chain wagon. She had abandoned the idea of defending the artillery and was headed uphill for a mounted messenger. Her detachment was breaking apart; if she could reach the relay road, she could bring a stronger force down the valley before the strangers moved inland.

Arthur saw her going but didn't fire. The woman had made a competent retreat, and he had other things to think about. A wounded man was crawling into the remaining horses' path. Arthur caught the reins and hauled the animals sideways, more interested in preventing them from trampling him than in chasing a captain who had already lost her guns.

Kaze saw Sorell too.

“Let her run,” Arthur called.

Kaze looked toward the messenger horse, then toward Magneto floating above the bridge.

“Yeah. He's more interesting.”

The captain made it to the ridge with two survivors. Her escape was not mercy from all of them, merely a choice none of them took back. She had witnessed the strangers' weapons and the floating man's power. Her report would not need embellishment to be dangerous.

Chris had reached the wrecked first gun.

“Holy shit, look at that elevation assembly.”

Arthur turned. “Chris.”

“Just a minute.”

“It's missing half its barrel.”

“I wasn't here for the barrel.”

Chris knelt by the broken carriage. He tested the sight mount's surviving screw and worked the part loose with an ordinary tool from his belt. The first attempt twisted the damaged bracket rather than freeing the screw. He changed the angle, braced it and tried again.

“Come on, sweetheart. I have ruined nicer things than you.”

The compact assembly came free, bent but intact enough to study. He turned it over and smiled.

Arthur watched the motion with a baffled affection. “You got a chest full of money in Billy and you're salvaging a gun that just tried to kill us.”

“Money doesn't tell me how this adjustment works.”

“Neither does a broken adjustment.”

“That's why you take it apart.”

Kaze walked back up from the other gun, Black Marrow already reintegrated into his body. The narrow slash in his trousers and fresh grime remained visible. He stopped beside Chris.

“What'd you get?”

Chris held the bent mount up.

“A little fucking education.”

“Worth the trip?”

“More than your talking crossbow.”

Kaze laughed. “Fair.”

On the far side, the last of the families reached solid ground. Magneto set down the final brace with a metallic groan. Then he floated across the ravine and landed near the three Sins without asking permission.

He looked first at the gun wreckage. Then at the bodies. Then at Kaze.

“You stopped the shot.”

“They were gonna shoot,” Kaze said. “I had a sword.”

“Your intervention also turned a threatened seizure into a battle.”

Daniel landed lightly beside them. “To be fair, that was mostly me. The soldier was being a dick.”

Magneto faced him. “You struck first.”

“Technically, gravity struck first. He fell down. I was just helping the paperwork.”

“Enough,” Magneto said.

Daniel laughed under his breath. He was listening, though.

The old man turned back to Kaze. “You could have kept walking.”

“Could've. Didn't.”

“And now the captain will return with a larger force.”

“Good.”

There it was. Not a misunderstanding. Kaze knew exactly what kind of answer he had given.

Magneto looked at him for a long moment. “Do you find every escalation amusing?”

“Not every one.”

“Which ones?”

“The interesting ones.”

“Then I hope you learn the difference before someone else must pay for your education.”

Kaze's smile narrowed. His right eye studied the man's posture, the nearby iron, the space separating them. He wasn't reading Magneto's thoughts or stealing his physics by looking. He was interested in where the next choice might lead.

“Could've been you paying, old man.”

Magneto stayed still. Discarded swords lifted from the mud around the wrecked wagon, points angled down.

“You are welcome to discover how expensive I can be.”

Arthur shifted his weight. He could feel the warning in the suddenly orderly metal. His cybernetic body did not surrender itself to the other man's will, but neither did he pretend that a field of directed iron was an ordinary saloon threat. He kept his hands clear of his holsters.

“Both of you made your point,” he said. “The folks crossed. We got our road. Any further argument costs us a perfectly good afternoon.”

Kaze looked at Daniel. Daniel raised an eyebrow and tipped his bottle toward the far bank.

“The man's got a point,” Daniel said. “A very pointy point. Several, actually.”

Kaze could have challenged Magneto. The possibility pleased him. But the old man was not retreating, and the answer to the more useful question was already in front of him: powerful enough to hold a bridge and a conversation at once, and stubborn enough to risk both over strangers. Kaze wanted to know what made that worth doing.

“Those your people?” he asked.

Magneto's eyes flickered toward the caravan.

“No.”

“Then why're you sweating for them?”

“Because I have spent too much of my life learning what happens when the strong decide some lives are cheaper than a piece of machinery.”

Kaze studied him. “Sounds personal.”

“It is.”

That answer did not invite further explanation. Magneto had no obligation to give one, and Kaze, for once, did not demand it.

Daniel looked across the ravine. “They've still got another cart halfway up the slope.”

Magneto turned. One of the handcarts had lost a wheel on the far bank. People were trying to drag it by hand, delaying the last stragglers.

He looked back at the Sins. “The crossing stays here until they are clear.”

Kaze stepped aside. “Then do your shit.”

Magneto went back to the ironwork. He lifted the damaged cart's iron axle, easing it over the stones while the workers guided its load. 
Arthur went to retrieve Chris. The chef had already tucked the damaged sight assembly into his kit. One of Sorell's surviving soldiers approached, holding a bloody rag to his wounded arm. He was unarmed now.

“Your friend,” he said to Arthur, pointing at Kaze. “What is he?”

Arthur looked at the man, then at Kaze sitting on an artillery wheel while Daniel tried to convince him that he had single-handedly won a labor dispute.

“Trouble,” Arthur said. “But I imagine you worked that out.”

The soldier took a nervous step backward.

“Are you going to kill us?”

Arthur rested his thumb on his belt. “Not presently. Might be wise not to test how long that lasts.”

The soldier nodded and helped another wounded man toward the ridge. Arthur did not chase them. Their identities, injuries and testimony would travel to the Noxian command by actual horses, not by a miracle in the air.

Chris straightened and looked at the bridge. “We're gonna cross on that thing?”

“Everybody else did,” Arthur said.

“Everybody else wasn't carrying a living apartment full of murder souvenirs.”

Billy's voice emerged from the permitted banjo channel, warm and wounded. “I do wish you'd stop calling our collection that.”

“Your collection includes an entire fucking carp with no plumbing.”

“A gentleman is entitled to incomplete dreams.”

Chris considered the metal span and the hovering man adjusting it. “Could just fly.”

“Could,” Arthur said. “But you were complaining about fuel yesterday.”

“I complain about lots of stuff. That's not an argument.”

“It's one of the few that keeps changing anything.”

Daniel floated over the bridge on his cloud to scout the far path. He checked the gravel bank, the winding lane beyond, and the people gathering their possessions for another hour of travel. He returned to report the road clear enough for ordinary passage.

Kaze and Arthur crossed on foot with Chris behind them. The metal creaked under their weight but held because Magneto continued directing its load. The Sins kept their hands off the families' supplies. Not out of sainthood: there was nothing there that interested them enough to argue over with the man keeping the bridge upright.

Halfway across, Kaze looked up at Magneto.

“You coming to Noxus?”

“I'm already in Noxus.”

“The capital, smartass.”

“Not with you.”

“I wasn't asking you to join us.”

“Then you have your answer.”

Kaze laughed. “I like you.”

Magneto watched the swordless man continue over the bridge. “I am not sure that is reassuring.”

“Wasn't supposed to be.”

Far below, the river broke into white water against fallen rock. Above, the bridge shifted once more as Magneto raised two braces to support the next movement of families. The Sins reached the opposite shore with no toll paid and no iron stolen. That was a real win for them, though not one any banker would know how to record.

The larger victory belonged to a different set of people.

An hour later, the refugees had gone beyond the far rise with their tools, bedding and injured companions. Magneto dismantled enough of the temporary bridge to deny an easy pursuit from the military side. He did not scatter the iron uselessly. The braces moved across the ravine to the protected bank, ready to serve the people's next crossing if they could find a safe destination.

He watched the last cart vanish between the hills. They had not asked for a lecture about a better world. They'd needed an intact road and the time to use it.

Far behind them, Captain Sorell was already riding toward the signal station with three surviving soldiers. One gun was smashed beyond immediate service, the other had lost its crew. She would report her own decision to fire, the prisoners she had failed to hold, and the dangerous strangers who had broken her command. The report would be hers. Whether anyone believed all of it was another matter.

In Zaun, Heisenberg had finished hearing his own witnesses and was busy with the more immediate indignity of making Serrat account for unpaid wages. He had not learned about a bridge in distant Noxus. He had no reason yet to connect it to the thieves who'd emptied his collection house.

Inside BELLY, Charles filmed Meatwad questioning Chris about the salvaged sight assembly. Chris turned it over in his hands and explained how the damaged worm screw changed the angle of the mount. Charles tried to interrupt and tell the camera that he had supervised the salvage. Meatwad asked whether the gun had ever been inside him.

“No,” Chris said.

“Then how'd you get its insides?”

Chris held up the loose screw. “Patience. Tools. Knowing where to put your hands.”

Charles leaned into the frame. “That's what I've been telling everybody.”

Chris didn't even look at him. “And not touching a damn thing till I know why it's there.”

Charles withdrew his hand from the workbench.

Arthur watched from a chair nearby. “Well, there's a lesson for the director.”

The camera remained on Charles long enough for his smile to turn brittle.

Outside, Kaze walked the inland road with Daniel and Arthur. Chris kept the exterior for now, enjoying actual daylight instead of monitor light. The harbor was behind them. Ahead, the road climbed through Noxian hill country toward larger settlements and harder opportunities.

Daniel handed Kaze the bottle. “So. Floating metal man. You think he could pick Arthur up by his shiny bits?”

Arthur looked over. “You try explaining that question to him.”

“I will. Just as soon as he gives me a magnet-sized appointment.”

Kaze drank, handed back the amber bottle and grinned at the road.

“He wasn't scared of us.”

“He had people to get across,” Arthur said.

“Yeah.” Kaze rubbed his thumb over the small healed cut on his forearm. “That's what made him interesting.”

Daniel took a sip. “And here I thought it was all the flying cutlery.”

“That too.”

The laughter that followed wasn't particularly reassuring. But it was theirs, and the road was open.
