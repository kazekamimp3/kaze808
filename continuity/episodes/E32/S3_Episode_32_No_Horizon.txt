Season 3, Episode 32: No Horizon

The first man to see the World Serpent thought the horizon had come loose.

I don't blame him. A horizon ought to stay where the gods put it. It is one of the few things sailors and politicians ordinarily agree upon.

Master Pilot Rell stood on a Noxian harbor's outer breakwater. Three vessels were trying to enter. One sailed sideways, one drifted toward the rocks, and the last, a fishing skiff with two brothers aboard, vanished between swells.

Beyond them lay a dark ridge. Rell had watched that ridge for half an hour, waiting for the mist to lift from the supposed island. Then it blinked.

An eye opened above the water, larger than the lighthouse lantern room, and considered the breakwater.

Rell dropped his light.

More of that body rose beyond the harbor. Farther sections curved away with the curvature of the planet. He had coiled himself around Runeterra; stretches of his living body lay against seas Rell would never see. Yormungander had brought that impossible scale with him. He had no obligation to fit the place.

A ship blew her horn. Men abandoned cargo to free the anchor. Another vessel struck her broadside as both crews lost sight of the enormous head.

The skiff came up on its next wave and disappeared into the trough.

Yormungander turned his eye toward it.

For a moment his jaw hung above the two brothers, and every sailor who could still pray made a selection from whatever gods he'd brought. Then the enormous head shifted away. His cheek brushed the sea beside the skiff rather than over it.

The brothers survived that choice. The wave made by his movement smashed the nearer merchantman's rudder against the breakwater. Her stern swung sideways. One sailor went into the water and did not surface where he'd fallen.

It's tempting to call that mercy. It may have been. It's equally tempting to call him a monster for the damage. A creature long enough to girdle a world can mean well and still rearrange a coastline with a glance.

The harbor's warning bell began to ring. Rell picked up his lantern and ran for the signal stairs.

He had a town to warn, and his legs were a great deal shorter than the news.

***

Several hours inland, Kaze was walking after a woman who'd just left him behind.

Katarina Du Couteau had left Odran's house with evidence of a conspiracy. She'd killed the financier before the Sins could ransom him; they'd stolen two chests of coin instead. Those were inside BELLY with Chris. Billy remained outside in the courtyard with Arthur and Daniel.

Kaze caught up with Katarina at the north street's covered arcade.

She heard him coming. Of course she did. She kept walking anyway.

“You always leave a man standing there after he helps you?” he asked.

“Only the ones who mistake being useful for being invited.”

“Damn. And here I thought I had a special place in your heart.”

“You have a very visible place in my evening.”

She looked at the drying black blood beneath his ribs. Her gaze lingered on his exposed tattoos before coming back to his face.

“Your injuries are closing.”

“Yeah. I'm a terrible patient. You could try examining me anyway.”

“That,” she said, “was almost subtle.”

“I don't do subtle when I like what I'm looking at.”

A smile came and went. She stopped beneath an arch, close enough that her shoulder brushed his bare arm as she turned.

“I have a governor to warn. A man still alive because I reached Odran first.”

“Then warn him. I'm not asking you to retire and buy a house.”

“What are you asking?”

Kaze had followed because she'd changed his plans without asking permission and hadn't apologized. Now she'd stopped, and he wanted a straight answer.

“I'm asking if you wanted me to follow.”

Katarina's thumb rested against the hilt at her hip.

“I knew you would.”

“Not what I asked.”

“No.” She leaned nearer. “It's not.”

She kissed him, quickly and with considerably less uncertainty than her words had offered. He caught the back of her waist. For two heartbeats nothing else interested him.

Then she stepped away, caught his reaching fingers and moved them gently off her belt.

“Don't follow me into his residence.”

“I'll try to contain my grief.”

“Try harder. There are people there I need to speak to while they're still breathing.”

She resumed walking. Kaze watched her go, not mistaking the kiss for ownership of her next decision.

He found that disappointing in a way he enjoyed.

***

Back at the house, Arthur was smoking a thick cigar while Billy guarded the place where the two chests had vanished into his impossible stomach.

Daniel sat on the fountain rim with one bare foot in the water. His ice forearms caught the moonlight. He had been trying to teach a dead soldier's abandoned banner to stay upright on its own.

“I'm tellin' you,” Arthur said, “we're not splitting money we haven't counted.”

From Billy's banjo, Chris's voice carried out of BELLY. “Nobody said split it. I said don't let him put it beside the acid.”

Billy looked insulted. “I am perfectly capable of distinguishing supper from treasure.”

“You swallowed an acorn you didn't own.”

“An acorn has no concept of property.”

Daniel tipped backward until his feet held him on the fountain edge. “I've met landlords who disagree.”

Arthur ignored him. “Chris, when you can inspect the chests, check the seals before opening anything.”

“Finally. Somebody appreciates an unopened box.”

“Don't take it apart until I get a look.”

The banjo gave an exasperated twang.

Kaze came through the gate smiling. Arthur noticed; Arthur was unfortunate enough to know him.

“You find your assassin?”

“Yeah.”

“And?”

“She's busy.”

Daniel put a hand to his chest. “A woman with a job. Beautiful. Rare. Nature is healing.”

Kaze caught his ankle and dumped him into the fountain.

Daniel surfaced laughing, swept a hand across his hair and froze the water off his head in a white plume. He found his frost-filmed unbreakable bottle at his belt and drank. He could have avoided the fall. Kaze knew it, and Daniel knew Kaze knew it.

“You're buying me a towel,” Daniel said.

“You're a fucking ice monkey.”

“Drying myself is not the point. I'm making a principled argument about household expenses.”

Arthur tried not to smile. He failed.

They slept a little. The money remained uncounted; Katarina had her own obligations.

By morning the harbor's warning had found the town.

***

The rider came in at a gallop, salt white on his coat. He'd changed horses at one relay station; his second mount staggered beneath him.

Three strokes of the town bell brought people into the square.

A Noxian warmason, one of the empire's military engineers, caught the rider beneath the market arch. She took his sealed report, broke it and began reading before the animal had stopped trembling.

“Harbor mouth obstructed,” she said to the guard beside her. “Merchantman grounded. Outer pier lost. Send the stone crews. No wagons go down the coast road until we know where the water is.”

“An attack?” he asked.

The rider tried to speak. All that came out was a cough. He pointed toward the report.

“Something alive,” the warmason said. “Large enough that the pilot couldn't see where it ended.”

Kaze, who had been looking for breakfast, changed direction at once.

Arthur read his expression and groaned. “Oh, hell.”

“Tell me that's not what I think it is,” Kaze said.

The rider managed, “A snake. The size of the fucking coastline.”

Billy went perfectly still.

He knew that word. He knew the shape that went with it, and the part of himself that hated being reminded how young most monsters were.

Daniel looked from Kaze to the warmason. “You want to see it.”

“I want to see him up close.”

“It's real,” Billy said. “And I decline the invitation.”

Arthur spread his hands. “Good. Two votes for not going back to the harbor we just left.”

“I'm going,” Kaze answered. Not an order. A personal decision, and he took the coast road without asking them to make it theirs.

Daniel stood, taking his spear. “I can't let you make the worst choice on the continent unsupervised.”

Arthur looked at Billy. “Well?”

“I don't care to meet that serpent.”

“Neither do I.”

From inside BELLY, Chris spoke through the banjo. “Does anybody know if it came up near the shipyard? Because if it broke the hoists I could use the chain drives.”

Arthur shut his eyes. “Of course that's what you heard.”

“I also heard giant snake, Arthur. My head ain't full of just fucking winches.”

Billy stared after Kaze, then turned his enormous mouth toward the road. “We are not providing a free ride into its teeth.”

“Agreed,” Arthur said.

They followed for different reasons. Daniel scouted ahead on his cloud. Billy walked rather than swallowing anyone. Hours later, they caught Kaze near the coast.

***

Back at the harbor, T.P.P. had decided that the end of ordinary shipping was a commercial opportunity.

Dutch van der Linde stood on a crate near the quay, addressing frightened citizens. His black TPP tattoo showed above blistered palms. Back at their rented headquarters waited an unfinished painting of Tahiti, four mangoes and an unpaid landlord.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” Dutch declared, “there are moments in history when the common man discovers his natural power. This is such a moment. The government will promise you a ship. I am offering you the possibility of passage.”

Behind him, Busta Rhymes held an empty money bowl. Miniature Brian Griffin, conjoined at Busta's shoulder, wore a look of administrative triumph that belonged on a much larger face.

“We are not selling boats,” Brian announced. “We're selling priority. There is a distinction, and frankly it's an elegant one.”

Busta leaned toward him. “Man, ain't nobody buying your fucking vocabulary.”

“Then why is the bowl filling?”

“Because I'm holding it.”

A woman with a little boy on each side pushed forward and asked what time the evacuation vessel would depart.

Dutch looked toward the harbor mouth. Between two broken masts, something vast and dark lay where the sea had been empty the day before.

“The situation,” he said, “is evolving.”

“That means there isn't a ship,” she said.

Clifford Marston rolled his hand-rim chair closer. Formerly Jack, he wore an unfinished TP tattoo and a cape that caught under one wheel. He yanked the fabric free, furious.

“Give her money back, Dutch. I ain't getting shot because you promised people a boat that don't exist.”

“I promised a chance!” Dutch said.

Brian looked offended. “You cannot refund chance. That's why it's such a tremendous product.”

Clifford turned his chair toward him. “You want to say that to her kid?”

For once, Brian looked at the children before answering. He recovered his nerve too late to look convincing.

The harbor bell rang. Soldiers cleared a landing for the Rook, an iron-hulled rescue launch returning from the outer pier with injured sailors. Dutch hadn't hired it.

Busta looked at the ship. Then at the money bowl. Then at Dutch.

“Now that's a boat.”

Dutch's face brightened with dreadful speed.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “I have a plan.”

Clifford buried his face in one hand. “I swear to God.”

***

Off Bilgewater, a fisherman named Varo cut his net loose rather than be dragged under. An immense scaly body crossed the sea, reaching beyond both sides of his horizon.

He never saw the head. He marked his chart to warn other fishermen, and because the lost net had cost him three months' earnings.

He turned for harbor.

Hours of water separated that decision from the Noxian port. No one heard Varo's story there that morning. The creature linked the two places with its body long before any messenger could link them with words.

***

The Sins reached the Noxian harbor late in the morning.

Yesterday the port cranes stood straight. Now cargo platforms were broken, ships lay sideways, and warmasons inspected a crack running the height of the seawall.

Beyond all that was a line of living darkness.

Billy stopped so abruptly Arthur nearly walked into his back.

Only part of Yormungander was visible. His spine dwarfed offshore islands. The nearer water rose as the living mass beneath it shifted.

I knew the older serpent in another life of this world. This younger one had no reason to remember me. That is an odd sort of grief: to recognize somebody magnificent who has yet to become the person you knew.

Kaze stood still for three whole breaths. Daniel waited. He didn't fill them with jokes.

Then Kaze laughed.

“Nigga. That is ridiculous.”

“An admirably technical assessment,” Arthur said.

“Look at him.”

“We are,” Billy said. “We can stop now.”

Below, a braided woman in a pilot's salt-stiff jacket wrestled the Rook's anchor cable while deckhands braced its capstan. Dutch had boarded and was telling her about his supposedly legitimate evacuation business.

Kaze took in Dutch, the money bowl and the injured sailors, and understood the scam before he heard a word.

“Holy shit,” Chris said through Billy's banjo. “Is that Dutch?”

Arthur grunted. “And the rest of the circus.”

Clifford had wheeled himself onto the gangplank, his pistol across his lap. Brian was instructing Busta to block the returning pilot's view of the ticket money.

The pilot glanced at the conjoined pair and then at the injured sailors still being lifted from her deck.

“Get off my boat,” she said.

Dutch spread his hands. “Madam, no one is taking your boat. We are proposing a mutually beneficial arrangement.”

“Get. Off.”

Kaze stepped up beside her.

“I like a woman who makes her decisions quickly. What's your name?”

She looked him up and down, from the tattoos on his bare chest to his stained samurai pants. Her expression changed, though she didn't release the cable.

“Hessa. Are you going to help, or are you selling me something too?”

“Depends. You know how to get close enough to that snake for him to see me?”

“You've got a death wish.”

“Maybe. You've got beautiful eyes. We can both be observant.”

Hessa laughed once, surprised into it. She held his gaze an extra moment before returning to the capstan.

“I know where the water still has depth,” she said. “I don't know where he'll move next. Help me get this boat clear and I'll decide whether you're worth taking out.”

“That's all I wanted. A little faith.”

“Try lifting something.”

He grinned and went to the cable.

Arthur crossed the gangplank just as Dutch recognized him.

“Arthur. My boy.”

“Don't you start.”

“We could do something extraordinary together. Consider the scale of the opportunity.”

“I am. You're selling seats on a ship with wounded sailors still aboard.”

“Every successful enterprise begins with a commitment.”

Arthur looked at the bowl in Busta's hand. “And ends when somebody checks the books?”

Busta bristled. “Hold up. My money is real.”

“That's the trouble.”

Clifford leveled his pistol. Not at Arthur's head, but close enough to announce himself.

“You got something to say about that?”

Arthur studied the young man's posture, the chair, the repaired frame. His crimson cybernetic eye resolved the pistol's cocked hammer and Clifford's tightening wrist while also tracking the injured sailor crossing behind him.

“Yeah,” he said softly. “I got something to say. Put that away before you decide we all need another funeral.”

Clifford's jaw worked. For a second he looked ready to shoot. Then the old bitterness won over immediate action. He lowered the muzzle but didn't holster it.

Daniel came up beside Busta, eyed miniature Brian and asked, “Is he the captain, or does the captain grow on the other shoulder?”

Brian drew himself up. “I am the strategic partner.”

“That's a no, then.”

Busta barked a laugh he hadn't meant to give.

The warping pier answered with a groan.

Hessa looked down. “Off the wood! Now!”

A fresh swell had caught beneath the broken outer platform. The Rook's anchor cable tightened through the capstan. Its strained fitting began tearing loose from the quay.

Arthur's cyber-eye tracked the strain, the men behind the line, and the cable's likely whipping path. He hauled a wounded sailor clear with his rebuilt hand.

“Clear that line!”

Nobody had time for a debate. Dutch jumped off the boat onto the quay. Busta carried Brian with him. Clifford spun his chair, but a broken plank jammed beneath the left wheel. He struck it twice with his palm, suddenly very young and very frightened.

Daniel planted his spear and slid across the sloping dock on bare gripping feet. A thin line of ice spread under the jammed plank, heaving it upward. He took Clifford's chair by the rear rail and pulled him free.

“Congratulations,” Daniel said. “Your boarding pass includes ground transportation.”

Clifford stared at him. “I didn't ask you to touch my chair.”

“You're welcome. Next time I'll rescue the chair separately.”

Arthur drew HIGH NOON. The single heavy weapon's shotgun core came into his hand, not THE COUNT's long-range artillery configuration. He aimed a concentrated slug into the exposed shore-side iron link, away from the hull and cleared crew. The shot severed the link and drove the broken metal into the seawall.

The freed cable whipped across empty decking. Where three people had stood, it took the railing clean off.

Kaze caught Hessa as the platform fell out from under her. He jumped toward the Rook with her against his side, Black Marrow growing from his right hand. A timber swung down across their path. He cut it once, the living blade passing through the loaded wood, and kicked the falling half clear of the boat.

They hit the deck hard. Hessa rolled onto an elbow, breathless, still holding her pilot's whistle.

“You always this fucking dramatic?” she asked.

“Only for women who like me.”

“Unfortunate for the rest of us.” Her grin contradicted her complaint. “Get the stern line free.”

At the quay's edge, Billy had an excellent view of the enormous movement beyond the harbor wall. He wanted no part of it. He also saw the launch drifting with injured sailors still aboard.

“I am not swimming toward that thing,” he said.

Chris's answer came through the banjo. “Then let me out. I can work the engine while they free it.”

Billy looked toward the outer sea, then made a decision that cost him something. He opened the living seam beneath his jaw. Chris emerged through the continuous inside-out inversion, full-sized and carrying his own gear; Billy folded inward to occupy BELLY in Chris's place. They remained one exterior body.

Chris landed on the quay exactly where Billy had been and ran for the launch.

The Rook's engine coughed. Chris followed the exhaust, found a jammed linkage beside the boiler and reseated its seized valve pin with wrist tools and sound metal from the damaged bracket.

“Come on, baby. I've seen worse guts on a Sunday special.”

The engine caught. Hessa aimed for a sheltered berth, but the current hauled the Rook away from the ruined pier and toward the harbor mouth. Injured sailors were still aboard, along with opportunists who had not been asked to stay.

On the quay, TPP watched their supposed evacuation business depart without them.

Brian looked at Dutch. “We have made an administrative error.”

“Don't tell me about errors,” Dutch snapped. “Tell me how much we took.”

Busta examined the bowl. “Enough for the rent and a little left, if nobody makes us pay the people back.”

Across the quay, the woman with two children was demanding exactly that. A harbor guard had begun taking names.

Clifford watched the departing Rook with murder in his eyes. Arthur had been aboard for all of thirty seconds. It had been long enough to make an enemy feel humiliated again.

Dutch watched the harbor clerks abandon their office for the damaged piers. Its door stood open.

He didn't chase the Rook. He walked toward the office instead.

“Change of plan,” he said.

This time, Brian was delighted.

***

Hessa tried to turn back toward the inner harbor. The current carried the Rook seaward anyway.

Living scales blocked the deeper channel. Conflicting currents squeezed the remaining water between that ridge and the rocks.

Arthur told Hessa to turn back. She told him she was already trying. He didn't like the answer, which made it no less true.

At the bow, Kaze saw the immense eye beyond the outer wall. Two stranded ships vanished behind a curling ridge of flesh.

“Hey!” Kaze shouted into the noise. “Yormungander!”

The creature did not answer in any language the sailors knew. A deep sound passed through the hull, as much vibration as voice. Chris gripped the rail while Hessa corrected their drift.

I'd have liked to translate it. I've made a career of knowing more than is strictly good for me. But this was the younger serpent, born of Giant magic into a life I knew only from the other end, and I hadn't heard enough to pretend I could speak for him.

Daniel came down on his cloud beside the bow and stayed above the dangerous water. “I think he heard you.”

“Good.”

“Probably hearing is one of his better qualities.”

Kaze leaned across the bow and caught Daniel's sleeve as the cloud drew near. A request to stay close. Daniel looked back. Neither made another joke.

Beyond the ridge a Noxian patrol cutter came around an exposed reef. Its commander stood by a deck gun, staring at the gigantic head. His sailors were still hauling the vessel's damaged sail clear of the rigging.

“Do not fire,” Hessa whispered.

The gun spoke anyway. The shot struck a wet scale and vanished in a spray of water. The serpent turned. The commander's next order died in his throat. His gunners ran for the stern while their ship drifted broadside toward the living ridge.

Yormungander lowered his head. He could have closed his jaws around the entire cutter. Instead he pressed one immense cheek against the water beside its bow. The wave shoved it hard onto the reef. Timbers burst, and men scrambled into the shallows. The guns went quiet.

Chris watched the ship settle against the rocks.

“He could've split that thing open.”

“Still might,” Arthur said.

Then the creature lifted his head. The wave traveled toward the Rook with the implacable patience of a falling building.

Arthur braced his rebuilt legs and grabbed Hessa's shoulder to keep her at the tiller. Chris ducked behind the engine casing. Daniel drove a sheet of ice against the launch's port side, giving the next rise of water something solid to break against. The ice shattered, but it bought Hessa enough time to turn the bow into the swell.

Kaze stayed standing, his hand sunk into the rail as black living tissue gripped the wood. The first wall of water lifted them. The next slammed over his head. His feet tore free; he struck the mast support and came back up bleeding at the shoulder, laughing because he was still there to laugh.

“THAT'S WHAT I'M TALKING ABOUT!”

Arthur spat seawater. “You're a goddamn idiot!”

“Yeah!”

“He could've killed every one of those men,” Chris shouted.

And there lay the maddening part. His restraint had still wrecked a ship. Yet it was restraint: a decision, not an empty movement of water.

Yormungander turned the immense eye back toward them.

Kaze could have tried to strike. His living sword was still part of him, and he'd never been particularly fond of the distance between wanting a fight and starting one. For once, he held still because someone else's decision had become more interesting than his own.

He raised one hand, palm outward.

The serpent watched. Then he looked beyond the harbor, toward an inland ridge where thunder had started rumbling despite the clear morning.

The head moved away. Not as a servant obeying a command, nor as a beast chased off. He had something of his own to attend to.

The Rook, with its battered passengers and stolen companions, had an opening to reach shore again.

Hessa took it.

***

At a rocky overlook farther along the coast, a huge red-haired man stood beneath a sky beginning to spark.

Thor carried Mulenear in his left hand. He had followed the sound of a sea he recognized across this strange coast. He looked down at the serpent's retreating head. Lightning crawled over the hammer as his grip tightened.

He had been torn out of his own battle with that creature. He knew what it meant to be told to fight, and what it might cost him to choose the fight again. Beyond that rage lay the thought of the family he still intended to reach.

He did not throw.

He turned from the overlook and walked toward the inland road, the hammer heavy at his side.

The serpent's gaze followed him as far as the curve of the coast permitted.

***

The Rook made harbor in early afternoon. Workers cleared broken moorings and argued over the surviving berths. The port would not recover quickly.

A guard made Busta refund the woman with two children. Meanwhile Dutch, Brian and Clifford slipped into the emptied harbormaster's office and stole a ledger of redirected cargo and inland destinations. Dutch held it like a map to the future.

“Gentlemen,” he told the others, “now we have something worth selling.”

Clifford looked down the quay toward the Rook, where Arthur was helping Chris off the deck.

“First I want to make that son of a bitch regret his day.”

“That,” Brian said from Busta's shoulder, “can be incorporated into the business model.”

On the Rook, Arthur had his cigar back between his teeth, unlit now. He took one look at the settling harbor and turned to Kaze.

“You got what you came for?”

Kaze looked out to where the giant body still occupied a line no mapmaker had put in the world.

“No,” he said. “Got something better.”

Daniel stood beside him, squeezing seawater from the hem of his layered clothing. “A healthy respect for the natural world?”

“Nah. I want to know what he wants.”

Arthur didn't joke. Chris stared at the horizon. Inside BELLY, Billy was quiet.

The silence couldn't last. It was never going to.

But the sea had changed the terms of their arguments. That was what frightened me. It had changed the terms for everyone else as well.

And somewhere beyond the harbor, a creature that had wrapped a world had decided which way to turn his head.
