Season 3, Episode 50: “All In”

Dr. Evil had nearly finished speaking when Brian began exercising in his sleep.

At first, the doctor assumed it was dissent.

Brian sat on a folded cloth near the end of the submarine's bench. His little feet hung clear of the floor. Without the cloth, the table edge would have cut across his eyes; with it, he had a commanding view of everybody's wrists. His notebook rested against his stomach, and his bare head had tipped backward just enough to expose the expression of a man who believed unconsciousness was somebody else's administrative problem.

His knees opened.

Closed.

Opened again.

Dr. Evil stopped beside the table.

Brian's hips lifted off the cloth.

He made a strained, determined grunt.

Dutch lowered the stores list.

“Brian?”

Another thrust. Another breath forced through clenched teeth.

His knees kept working, out and in, with the defeated determination of a squid trying to escape an extremely small pair of trousers.

Clifford looked up.

“What's he lifting?”

“My opinion of leaving him ashore,” Dr. Evil said.

Brian's hands tightened around the notebook. His little heels rose another inch.

He sounded as though he had reached the final repetition of something a doctor would advise against on a form with no pictures.

Dutch leaned closer.

“He's asleep.”

Dr. Evil watched the knees close.

“Then his contribution has improved considerably.”

Brian grunted harder.

The doctor waited.

“Briefly.”

Clifford moved his cup farther from the bench.

“Does he do that?”

Dutch considered the question with the weary seriousness it deserved.

“He appears to.”

Brian's notebook slid sideways. Dr. Evil caught it before it fell, holding it between two fingers.

Brian's hands continued gripping the place where it had been.

“Remarkable,” the doctor said. “We have removed the work and retained the effort.”

Brian's knees opened again.

Clifford looked at Dutch.

Dutch looked back.

They both looked at Dr. Evil, who had just caught himself watching the entire movement like it might eventually produce a report.

“Wake him.”

Dutch touched Brian's shoulder.

The little man stopped. Opened his eyes. Looked at the three faces above him.

“What?”

Dr. Evil held out the notebook.

“Your training session.”

Brian took it.

“I was listening.”

“With your pelvis?”

Brian looked down at himself.

His legs were still apart.

He brought them together.

“I adjusted my position.”

Clifford nodded.

“Several times.”

Brian tucked the notebook under his arm.

“Busta Rhymes would understand active recovery.”

Dr. Evil looked beneath the bench.

“Was he spotting you?”

Brian slid off the bench. His feet found the rung beneath it; he lowered himself onto that, then down to the floor, where the table immediately erased everybody except their shoes.

“If the meeting is over, I'll retire.”

Dr. Evil looked down toward the voice.

“Take the gymnasium with you.”

Brian walked out beneath the table's projecting corner without needing to duck. It spoiled the dignity of an otherwise perfectly serviceable exit.

That was the night after the harbor interview. While TPP discovered that Brian could lose an argument without being conscious, the Sins camped inland on the road back toward the works.

They spent the following day walking the surviving route. Arthur's map saved them one useless climb and sent them around a washout that hadn't improved since the outward journey. They slept again before reaching the settlement below the furnace terraces on the third day.

The driver was there.

The foreman found him sitting outside a wheelwright's shed, his injured leg stretched onto a low crate. He'd lost a cart, kept the mule and acquired a limp he had no intention of discussing with the man who'd broken the upper road.

Kaze stopped in front of him.

“The one with the horns.”

The driver looked toward the foreman.

“Maul,” Arthur said. “Asked for your map.”

“Took it.”

Arthur held up the torn survey.

The man's expression changed.

“That's mine.”

Arthur let him examine it. Kept the wrapped weapon in his satchel, away from the conversation.

“Where'd he come from?” Kaze asked.

“Across the gap.”

“Before that.”

“I didn't see.”

Kaze waited.

The driver shifted his bad leg.

“My cart was going over. I was still in it. He was on the other side when it stopped falling.”

“He say why he wanted me?”

“Didn't know your name till I gave it.”

The wind moved through the broken wheel hanging beside the shed.

Kaze looked at him more carefully.

“You gave it.”

“He asked who caused the broken ground. I told him what people said.”

“Then?”

“Your tree friend spoke to him.”

The foreman looked down.

Nobody supplied a joke.

Kaze had followed a weapon toward the coast, then a driver's knowledge back inland. At the end of the route sat a man who had been frightened of a falling cart. He hadn't seen a doorway to another world. He hadn't received instructions from whoever Kaze had imagined might be waiting behind Maul.

He'd passed on a name.

Kaze's own name.

“That's it?”

The driver nodded.

“That's it.”

Kaze looked toward the terraces where K.K. had gone to see something interesting.

Arthur folded the torn survey carefully.

“Mind if I keep this till we're clear of the broken roads?”

The driver considered the paper, then Kaze.

“Keep it.”

Arthur nodded his thanks.

Kaze stayed beside the shed long enough for the driver to become nervous about the silence.

Then he stepped away.

“Didn't come looking for me,” he said to Arthur.

“Not till he heard of you.”

Kaze looked back down the road they'd spent two days following.

“Motherfucker.”

It wasn't directed at the driver.

They found a place to stop below the works, where a former cart barn had become a drinking house. The broad doors and high rafters accommodated workers, freight hands, Daynuh and a pit bull whose shoulders stood higher than some of the customers' heads.

The keeper asked them to keep the dog away from the cooking door.

Daynuh looked at Mahnuh.

“Hear that?”

The dog's nose remained pointed at the cooking door.

“Course you did.”

They settled in the yard beside the barn. Arthur went to speak with the foreman about the open road beyond the works. Daynuh lay back against their packs with Mahnuh beside him, tired from the walk and already wondering which of his friends was going to describe the next bad idea as a short trip.

Kaze looked through the open barn doors.

People were playing cards at a long table.

He watched a pile of silver move from one side to the other.

Then looked at Billy's great, impassive face.

“Tell Chris I got something better than looking for dead men's directions.”

Billy smiled.

“I am almost afraid to interrupt him.”

Inside, Chris had opened a battered box of mixed ironmongery in his stored salvage. Four good brass hinges lay beneath bent brackets and a handful of rusted fittings.

He lifted one. Worked it. Checked the pin.

“What?” he asked through the outward channel.

“Kaze has found recreation.”

Chris looked at the hinges.

Then at the exterior monitor, where the tavern's pantry door sagged visibly from its upper fastening.

“Bring these out first.”

Billy returned the small wrapped bundle to Arthur's waiting hands when Arthur came back. Then he yielded. Chris was outside now, Billy full-sized inside; Arthur gave the same four hinges to Chris.

Chris unwrapped the hinges with the solemnity of an outlaw about to liquidate part of his fucking personality.

The keeper examined the hinges.

“Four silver.”

“Six. And a beer for the big one when he comes in.”

“They're used.”

“So's your door. Yours is trying to become a window.”

The man worked the hinge twice.

“Six. One beer.”

Chris accepted the coins. Left the hinges. The keeper put the promised beer aside for Daynuh and began examining his newly acquired fittings.

Kaze watched the silver go into Chris's hand.

“There.”

Chris looked at him.

“I sold something.”

“You can get over it.”

“Give me a fucking minute.”

The night before, back aboard the submarine, Brian had left his sleeping place and entered the crew galley with his eyes barely open.

Dutch saw him from the passage.

“Brian?”

Brian didn't answer.

He picked up a folded cloth. Put it on a stool. Climbed onto the stool using the lower brace, hauled one knee over the edge and stood on the cloth so he could reach the preparation surface.

Dutch followed.

Brian took two pieces of bread from the covered board. His small body obscured the butter dish while he fussed over it, then he laid the bread precisely beside one another.

Then picked up a spoon.

Held it upright.

Put it down on the bread.

Dutch watched him consider the arrangement with closed eyes.

Dr. Evil appeared in the passage.

“What is he doing?”

“I believe he's asleep.”

“That narrows very little.”

Brian moved the spoon to the other piece of bread.

Clifford arrived behind the doctor, stopping where he could see without crowding Dutch against the stool.

“Making something?”

“A case,” Dr. Evil said.

Brian reached toward a cup beside the old coffee pot. It was a normal crewman's cup, meaning that against Brian it had the proportions of a modest village well.

He put both hands around it.

Lifted.

Dutch moved closer in case he fell.

Brian got the rim up to his face and leaned backward to compensate for the weight. A small amount of coffee came over the edge onto his shirt.

“Hot,” Brian said.

Dr. Evil looked at the pot.

Then at Dutch.

The coffee had been left since earlier that evening. No steam. The pot sat cold beside the unused heat.

Dutch touched the side of the cup as Brian lowered it.

“It isn't.”

Brian made an offended little sound, as though temperature were a management decision nobody had consulted him about.

He put the cup down.

Stood motionless.

Dr. Evil leaned slightly toward him.

“You have burned yourself with room temperature.”

Brian placed the second piece of bread over the spoon.

Pressed once.

Clifford considered the sandwich.

“Crunchy.”

Dutch took the spoon out before Brian could pick the food up.

Brian's hands continued with the task. He lifted the two pieces of bread together, held them against his chest and began turning toward the stool's edge.

Dutch put a hand beside his shoulder.

“Brian. Stay there.”

Brian opened his eyes fully.

Looked at the bread.

At the wet patch on his shirt.

At the three men watching him.

“What?”

Dr. Evil folded his hands behind his back.

“We were waiting for the menu.”

Brian set the bread down.

“I got up for something.”

“You appear to have found most of it.”

He looked at the coffee.

“Spilled.”

“Yes.”

“It was hot.”

Dutch held the cold cup where Brian could touch it.

Brian did.

A pause.

“Must've cooled.”

Dr. Evil turned toward Clifford.

“I want that kind of confidence in our pricing.”

Brian began climbing down. One foot found the brace. The other searched beneath the stool, briefly extending into the empty air like a tiny surveyor disputing a cliff.

Dutch kept his hand nearby until both feet reached the floor.

Brian straightened his damp shirt.

“I don't require supervision.”

Dr. Evil looked at the two pieces of bread.

“The cutlery disagrees.”

Brian left with as much dignity as a man could recover while walking beneath an audience's elbows.

Nobody applauded.

Clifford picked up the spoon.

“He buttered the handle.”

By the time Daynuh woke in the barn yard the following afternoon, Kaze had introduced Chris to the card table.

Chris sat opposite him. Three local players had made room: a cordmaker, a broad woman with a mason's scarred hands, and a freight clerk who'd been dealing.

The clerk finished explaining their five-card draw game. No opening ante on this table. A player could fold without paying; money went in when somebody actually bet.
