Season 3, Episode 49: “Public Facing”

“Don't step on that.”

Kaze looked down at the plank across the roadside drain.

Then at Daynuh.

“It'll hold me.”

“I know. It'll also snap, tip that stone into the ditch and leave a hole for the next wagon. You get to be right and everybody else gets a fucked axle. Wonderful arrangement.”

Kaze put one foot on the stone beside it instead.

“You always got something to say about a board.”

“Boards generally listen.”

Chris stopped behind them, the banjo hanging at his belt. Billy was inside, watching through the headlamp.

“Could take it,” Chris said.

Daynuh bent, examined the split end and turned the plank over.

“Could. Good piece above the crack.”

Kaze looked at him.

“You just said leave it.”

“Said don't step on it. Different conversation.”

Chris nodded gravely.

“Important distinction.”

“You ain't taking the fucking bridge.”

“Now it's a bridge,” Chris said. “He thought it was a board till we showed interest.”

Arthur stopped at the low wall with the map still folded. The harbor lay below them, close enough to see men moving between warehouses. The inland road climbed away beyond the drain. They had barely begun the return toward the upper works.

Daynuh settled the plank back on its supports, sound side uppermost.

“There. Still a bad idea. Now it's a bad idea somebody can cross.”

Chris inspected the pale, protected underside.

“Could make a shelf.”

Daynuh looked toward Kaze without turning his head.

“Two shelves.”

Kaze smiled slowly.

“You enjoy this shit.”

“Yeah. Took you long enough.”

“You gonna give him shit when he's got half a house in there?” Kaze asked.

Daynuh looked at Chris.

“Depends. Good half?”

“Useful things,” Chris said.

“That's what people say before they show you seven broken wheels and a door that goes nowhere. But if he's got good hinges, I'm interested.”

“See?” Chris said.

Daynuh pointed at him.

“Didn't finish. If you bring me a whole fucking church because you like the hinges, we're having another conversation.”

Billy spoke through the banjo.

“Architecture deserves to be appreciated in context.”

Daynuh slowly turned back toward Kaze.

“There's your real problem. One of them collects. The other one can eat the neighborhood.”

“And you encourage both.”

“I like hinges.”

Mahnuh stepped across on the stones. Daynuh scratched his shoulder when he reached the other side.

The man following them from the harbor stopped below the culvert.

Arthur had seen him coming. So had Kaze. Neither had mistaken his repeated interest in the same patch of road for expert concealment.

“You lost?” Arthur asked.

The man wore the harbor watch's short coat. His eyes moved to Kaze's stomach and stayed there long enough to establish that his assignment had become unpleasantly easy.

“My officer wants to speak with you.”

Kaze stepped toward him.

“Which you?”

The watchman swallowed.

“Kaze.”

“Here I am.”

“At the station.”

“Here I am,” Kaze repeated.

The watchman looked past him at Daynuh, then at Arthur's hands, then at Chris.

“I'll tell him.”

He turned back downhill at a walk that became a jog once he had something between himself and the tattooed name.

Daynuh watched him go.

“We waiting?”

“He wants to ask me something.”

“Everybody wants to ask you something. Usually it's why the fuck their house is over there now.”

Kaze leaned against the wall.

“Could be interesting.”

“Could be a man doing a job.”

“That too.”

Daynuh considered the space beside him, then sat on a sound stretch of stone.

“All right. But if this turns into you arguing with a uniform till sunset, I'm eating before you finish.”

Down at the quay, Brian was attempting to accompany a crewman's stores inquiry without looking like something the man had brought to compare against a bolt.

The warehouse keeper leaned over his counter.

“That one yours?”

“I represent the company,” Brian said.

“I asked him.”

The crewman inspected the folded canvas he'd come to price.

“He's with us.”

“Founder,” Brian added.

The keeper looked at his bare scalp.

“Was it a hard winter?”

Brian put his notebook on the lowest accessible corner of the counter.

“It's deliberate.”

“Must be. Nothing grazed it that evenly.”

The crewman turned his face into the canvas.

Brian heard the breath escape.

“We are evaluating suppliers. A professional impression would help.”

“Then let him evaluate,” the keeper said. “He knows which end of the canvas is canvas.”

Outside, the watchman passed the open door on his way to fetch his officer. Brian recognized the name he gave the man waiting by the warehouse.

Kaze.

Brian went still.

Then looked up the road.

From the quay he could see the enormous seated shape beside the culvert, the standing men and the small dog.

He picked up his notebook.

“I'll be back.”

The crewman lowered the canvas.

“We're only getting a price.”

“I'm developing an opportunity.”

“Don't develop one in my name.”

Brian went uphill under his own power, already arranging the encounter into something he could report as initiative.

Inside BELLY, Charles Manson had come to his own communication station because the exterior feed showed uniforms approaching a conversation.

He liked conversations where somebody believed the wrong person was in charge.

Master Shake stood beside him, yellow hands planted against his white cup.

“We should be outside for this.”

Meatwad rolled closer.

“I wanna go outside anyway.”

“Exactly,” Shake said. “Public representation. I have been saying this.”

“I wanna see if the sky still looks like that.”

“That is not what I have been saying.”

Charles leaned toward the station's outward channel.

“Billy. You got people out there saying who people are. I'd like to stand there when they say it.”

His voice came through the banjo at Chris's belt.

Chris looked down.

“You can already talk.”

“Talking ain't standing. You're standing. You tell me they're the same.”

Shake crowded him.

“I require an appearance.”

Daynuh looked toward Chris.

“That another one?”

Chris stared at the banjo.

“That's the milkshake.”

“Still got the milkshake.”

“Yeah.”

“And the meat.”

“Right here,” Meatwad said through Charles's station.

Daynuh rubbed his chin.

“You got a whole fucking lunch arguing in there.”

Billy's voice followed, pleasantly deliberate.

“I have heard the requests.”

Chris tilted his head.

“You want them out?”

“I would enjoy seeing what they do with the privilege.”

“That's a terrible reason.”

“You keep bringing home things for less.”

Chris looked at the plank.

Daynuh looked at him looking.

“Don't put that on the board,” Chris said.

They changed places. Billy became the sole exterior body beside the wall; Chris arrived full-sized inside his home, where Frylock was waiting near the lounge.

“You're letting Shake speak to officials?” Frylock asked.

“Billy is.”

“That distinction won't help.”

“I know. I enjoy having it.”

Outside, Billy moved to a level patch off the road and opened his enormous mouth. His release choice brought Charles through first. Boots found the stone. Charles straightened, looked around and breathed deeply.

Meatwad came next, rolling clear of the teeth onto the dry verge.

Shake followed with both hands raised.

“Do not wipe me on anything. I am not the wiping thing.”

Billy shut his mouth.

Three residents stood outside. No prisoner had followed them. Inside, Chris watched the same road through the live monitor, with Frylock beside him.

There we go. The Seven Sins' public image now included a milkshake arriving through a giant fish's mouth while disputing his classification as a wet wipe. I have worn a red suit through some medically indefensible afternoons, and even I know when the branding department should crawl into the sea.

Meatwad looked up.

“Yeah. That's the sky.”

He sounded pleased.

Brian reached the culvert just as Shake turned toward Kaze.

“Before anyone speaks, I will handle the public.”

Kaze stared at him.

“Who the fuck elected you?”

“Master Shake requires no election.”

“Master of what?”

“That is the complete name.”

Daynuh leaned forward.

“If the second half's a warning, I appreciate it.”

Brian stopped beside the wall.

Chris's laugh came out of the banjo.

Then stopped.

“Oh, Brian. Buddy. What happened?”

Kaze looked down.

His smile arrived in pieces.

“No fucking way.”

Brian held the notebook against his side.

“I changed my appearance.”

“To the doctor,” Kaze said.

“Independently.”

Daynuh studied him with the interest he'd given the split plank.

Brian saw the comparison happening and hated it before the man spoke.

“Well?”

“I'm looking for where the rest screws on.”

“I'm a person.”

“Didn't say you weren't. You came up here looking like a knob somebody took off a serious man.”

Charles bent slightly, smiling.

“You want us looking at your head or listening to you?”

“Both would be customary.”

“Then say something. Head's finished.”

Brian opened the notebook.

“I thought there might be value in a coordinated public position.”

Arthur looked downhill.

The watch officer was coming up with the original watchman. No line of soldiers followed them.

“Here comes the public,” Arthur said.

Brian straightened.

Shake stepped in front of him.

“Good. You may announce me.”

“I don't work for you.”

“I assumed you were a promotional item.”

“I'm the founder of Tampon Pandemic Productions.”

Shake turned toward the approaching officer.

“He's not with us.”

Kaze looked at the milkshake.

“Neither are you.”

The officer stopped at the edge of the level ground.

He had come prepared to speak to armed men. He took in Billy's immense teeth, Daynuh's size, Kaze's tattoos and Arthur's quiet attention.

Then Charles.

Then the talking cup attempting to stand at the front.

His preparation died without leaving a forwarding address.

“Kaze?”

Kaze lifted one hand.

“You got my message.”

“The tallyman gave it.”

“Clearly?”

“With the pencil marks.”

The officer held his gaze.

“He says you returned the pencil.”

“Want it back?”

“I want to know whether your message was a threat to my station.”

The absurdity around them didn't make the question less serious. The man kept enough room between himself and Kaze to draw a weapon, though nothing in his expression suggested he mistook that for a solution.

Kaze's smile thinned.

“If I wanted your station gone, why would I borrow a fucking pencil?”

“People warn.”

“Do I look like people?”

Meatwad rolled into the gap beside him.

“A little.”

Kaze looked down.

Meatwad looked up.

“Mostly the legs.”

For one extraordinary second, nobody helped.

Daynuh pressed his lips together. Arthur looked at the road. From the banjo came one small, betrayed sound from Chris.

Kaze pointed at Meatwad.

“I'm trying to do something.”

“Oh. Okay.”

Meatwad moved two feet to the left and watched him do it.

The officer waited until Kaze looked back.

“You claimed responsibility for the rupture.”

“Yeah.”

“And the tollhouse.”

“Yeah.”

“Christopher Graham?”

Chris answered through the banjo.

“Depends who's asking.”

The officer's eyes moved to Billy.

Billy smiled without explaining his interior.

“I'm the man asking,” the officer said.

“Then yeah.”

The officer took a breath.

Charles stepped closer to the wall.

“You asking because you don't know, or asking because you want them saying it in front of you?”

“Who are you?”

“Charles.”

“Are you part of this group?”

“I live with people. You live with people. That make you responsible for their socks?”

“Charles,” Billy said.

He looked back at the fish.

“You wanted fresh air.”

Charles opened his hands and took another conspicuous breath.

Shake immediately occupied the silence.

“I can clarify the hierarchy.”

“No,” Kaze said.

Shake occupied the next silence more quietly.

The officer looked back at Kaze.

“I can't answer a district report by pretending I didn't find you.”

“Then don't.”

“Where are you going?”

Arthur's eyes lifted.

Kaze stayed against the wall.

“Somewhere else.”

“Which road?”

“The one I fucking pick.”

The officer nodded once. Not agreement. Acknowledgment that he'd received the answer he was going to get.

“Who sent the report?” Kaze asked.

“District command.”

“Who do they answer to?”

“Noxian command.”

“A name.”

The officer considered him.

“Grand General Swain.”

Kaze repeated the name softly.

Then smiled again.

“Tell him he can ask me himself.”

“I'll report what you said.”

“There you go.”

Brian stepped forward.

“A distinction may be useful here.”

Everyone looked down.

He had wanted everyone looking at him. The physical experience contained several drawbacks.

“Our company has no operational responsibility for these incidents.”

Arthur looked at him.

“You walked up here to say that?”

“Clarity matters.”

“It did,” the officer said. “Till you came.”

Brian's jaw set.

“I'm identifying an independent commercial interest.”

“Then identify it somewhere else. I'm speaking to him.”

Shake pointed at Brian.

“I've been trying to establish that for minutes.”

“You just offered to clarify their hierarchy.”

“As a courtesy. I am extremely generous with information.”

From inside, Frylock's voice reached Chris rather than the road.

“Shake's going to get himself arrested.”

Chris leaned toward the outward channel.

“Shake. Ask him if the cell has seating.”

Shake turned toward Billy.

“I know what you're doing.”

“Then you're ahead of everybody out here.”

Brian put himself between the cup and the officer's sightline.

“I can help organize a conversation that doesn't become this.”

The officer looked at him for a long moment.

“Were you in the restaurant?”

“No.”

“The tollhouse?”

“No.”

“Did you see the rupture happen?”

“No.”

“Then all you've added is another person I'll have to describe.”

Brian looked at the watchman's little writing book.

The man had opened it.

“I haven't done anything.”

“I'm aware,” the officer said.

Daynuh's laugh finally escaped.

It wasn't delicate.

Brian turned on him.

“At least I'm attempting to contribute.”

Daynuh leaned forward, elbows on his knees.

“You want a contribution? Quit lifting your chin like you're waiting for somebody to hang a price tag under it. He asked about a crime. You came in advertising yourself. Now he's got to remember which bald head was talking and why the cup says it's your supervisor.”

“I never said supervisor,” Shake said.

“You fucking radiate supervisor. All mouth, no legs, arrive after the work and demand a better place to stand.”

Chris made an appreciative noise through the banjo.

Shake looked around for a different audience.

Brian looked at Daynuh again.

“Busta Rhymes could command this entire conversation without—”

“Then fetch him,” Daynuh said. “Quit sending the travel-size fucking introduction.”

Brian shut his mouth.

Meatwad came closer.

“I like your head.”

Brian looked down at him.

“Thank you.”

“Makes you look like you got plans.”

Brian's shoulders lifted.

“I do.”

“I don't think they're working, but I can tell you got 'em.”

The watchman covered his mouth with the back of his writing hand.

The officer didn't.

That was somehow worse.

He closed the conversation himself.

“I've heard you. I'll send the account. I won't promise what follows it.”

Kaze stepped away from the wall.

“Didn't ask you to.”

The officer held his ground while Kaze passed him. Only after the tattooed back had turned did he look again at the three residents standing beside Billy.

“And you?”

Shake raised one yellow hand.

Charles said, “Taking a walk.”

Meatwad looked at Shake until the hand came down.

“Same,” Shake said.

The officer went back toward the harbor with his watchman. His account would have witnesses, admissions and several descriptions he would have preferred to acquire on a different day.

It would not include the Sins' destination. Arthur had kept the map folded.

Shake watched the officer disappear below the bend.

Then turned to Brian.

“Now. What can your company offer?”

Brian lowered his notebook.

“You just told him I wasn't with you.”

“Publicly. I maintain a private sector.”

Charles laughed through his nose.

“He means he wants something.”

“I can negotiate for myself.”

“Then do that. Don't bring a sector into it.”

Brian considered Shake. A talking milkshake had drawn immediate attention without having to explain why he deserved it. That observation annoyed him. It also interested him professionally.

“Do you perform?”

Shake spread his hands.

“You've seen the range.”

“I've seen you interrupt the police.”

“Nobody interrupted me back. That's stage presence.”

Charles turned toward Billy.

“You hear this? We go outside once, he starts selling the air.”

“You've been breathing an awful lot of it,” Shake said.

Billy settled his great weight comfortably beside the wall.

“Continue. I am fascinated by the economy.”

Meatwad had found a smooth stone in the dry verge. He pushed it toward Brian.

“This one could perform.”

Brian stared at it.

“It's a rock.”

“He don't interrupt.”

“Then he's ahead,” Charles said.

Meatwad positioned the stone upright. It fell over. He turned it onto a flatter edge and tried again.

“There. That's his face.”

“I mean a repeatable act. Something with an introduction, a structure, a finish.”

Chris leaned toward his outward channel inside BELLY.

“Ask what the rock charges.”

“You don't know his life.”

“Busta Rhymes would understand building an identity before an audience understands the ambition.”

“Which one you talking about?”

Brian glanced down.

Death Mountain lay at his feet, bald, silent and apparently ahead on audience engagement.

“Neither.”

“If we are discussing representation, I own the mountain.”

“No you don't.”

“I discovered the commercial possibility.”

“I found him in the dirt.”

Meatwad looked pleased.

“Yeah.”

“There. That sounded like you knew what you wanted.”

“I do know.”

“Those are two different men.”

“So you have seen the range.”

Then deliberately turned his back on him.

Behind it, Meatwad rolled the stone toward Billy, pushing patiently when it caught against a crack. Charles watched Brian's back with a small, satisfied smile. He'd obtained an argument, fresh air and a new person who regretted speaking to him. A productive outing.

“This was an opportunity.”
