Season 3, Episode 55: “The Last Take”

“Don't move.”

Billy stopped with one foot above the grass.

Daynah pointed.

Under the lifted shoe, Mahnah's tail moved out of the way with the slow, offended dignity of a fallen king being asked to take his dick off the dinner table.

“My apologies,” Billy said.

Mahnah settled his head back down.

Morning had found the Sins where they'd stopped beyond the rise. The inland road lay below them. Their fire was out, their things were packed, and Chris had decided to spend breakfast inside himself.

Chris had folded inward through the chest-and-jaw inversion. Billy unfolded outside with his hat, his own banjo and a sincere interest in being allowed to walk somewhere.

So far he'd made it four feet.

“He could've moved it sooner,” Billy said.

“You could've looked.”

“I did look. I saw a magnificent animal. I assumed he had accounted for his extremities.”

“He's asleep.”

“An unfortunate time to abandon responsibility.”

Chris's voice came out of the banjo.

“You kicked a dog in your head and still managed to blame the dog.”

“Christopher, I have kicked no one. This is a success story.”

Kaze laughed from the road.

Arthur was beside him, studying the torn survey they'd kept from the works. The wrapped Maul hilt stayed with Arthur's gear. No glowing weapon, no dramatic pose. Just another dead man's thing they hadn't finished deciding what to do with.

“We going?” Kaze asked.

“I want a look at that crossing first.” Arthur tapped the paper. “See whether this road actually joins where he said.”

“It'll join.”

“Then it'll be a very satisfying walk.”

Kaze looked back up the slope.

“You coming, Billy?”

“Shortly. Christopher has requisitioned a moment of domestic privacy, during which he intends to speak continuously through my instrument.”

“I'm cleaning something.”

“That will be a first.”

“You live in a man who's inside another man and you still talk like you got a summer house.”

Billy glanced upward.

“I had a summer house.”

I have narrated no deed, sale or fucking property tax assessment establishing that claim.

“It was a log.”

That sounds financially attainable.

“It had a view.”

“It was floating downstream,” Chris said. “That's a fucking eviction.”

Arthur had already started walking.

Kaze followed him, still grinning.

Daynah watched them take the bend. Then he looked at Billy.

“You two finish your housework. I'll get him moving.”

Mahnah shut his eyes harder.

An ambitious counteroffer.

Inside, Chris had a rag, a controller and the particular expression of a man discovering the fingerprints on his property had become a census.

Charles stood by the couch.

“I didn't touch it.”

“You got candy on it.”

“That's not mine.”

“It's your thumbprint.”

Charles examined the controller without taking it.

“You ever think somebody's putting my thumb on things?”

Chris stared at him.

“Somebody breaks into my stomach. Steals your thumb. Plays my game. Puts the thumb back while you're asleep.”

“That's what I'm asking.”

“Your first fucking question should be how they knew which end to put back.”

Meatwad came around the table with the camera.

“We ain't making the movie about that.”

“Another one already?” Chris asked.

“More of the movie. Charles wants to do his answer again.”

Charles straightened.

“It didn't come out right.”

“You said it,” Meatwad told him.

“I heard it. That's how I know.”

Frylock drifted past the couch, keeping clear of Chris's work.

“He wants to replace the part where you asked whether he'd ever had a job.”

“I had jobs.”

“Charles,” Chris said, “prison laundry ain't a residency.”

“You got clothes clean, didn't you?”

“I wasn't there.”

Charles spread his hands.

“Then you don't know what I did.”

From the other end of the couch, Shake made a long, ugly noise through his straw.

Everyone looked at him.

“I'm waiting for the creative discussion to begin.”

Frylock glanced at the camera.

“It's been recording for a while.”

Meatwad checked.

“Aw, man. I was carrying it.”

“Wonderful,” Shake said. “More footage of the floor. Our leading man.”

Meatwad turned it toward him.

“There. Now we got something worse.”

Chris laughed so suddenly that Billy's banjo barked outside.

Daynah looked over.

Billy nodded gravely.

“A successful production meeting.”

At the harbor, Doctor Evil was also holding a successful production meeting.

His principal production was a spaceship he did not have.

They'd cleared a space on the quay. The submarine mechanic crouched beside it. Dutch stood behind the man, arms loose, making no effort to look like anybody's employee.

Brian occupied his little chair on its wooden riser near the cleared space. Clifford stood farther back with his recovered helmet under one arm. The real lightsaber stayed on his belt.

Starkiller arrived alone.

He took out his coastal chart and spread it on the stones. The mechanic weighed one corner with a loose pebble.

He looked at the submarine. Then at the mechanic.

“You found something?”

“I looked at what I've got.”

Doctor Evil lifted a finger.

“Which is the first stage of innovation.”

“It's a boat,” the mechanic said.

“An advanced boat.”

“Still a boat.”

Brian leaned forward.

“Gentlemen. We did establish that yesterday.”

Galen looked at him.

“Then let him finish.”

Brian settled back as if he had personally authorized that development.

The mechanic used a dirty finger to mark the hull's rough length on an empty corner of the chart. No secret schematic. Just a man showing which problem he actually understood.

“I can keep air in this thing underwater. I can keep it moving. You're asking me to tell you how to leave the sky, survive what's above it and find a place that isn't on any chart I've seen.”

“Yes.”

“I can't.”

Doctor Evil smiled at Galen.

“He's being modest.”

The mechanic looked up.

“No.”

Beautiful. A technical term even management can fucking understand.

Galen crouched opposite him.

“What powers it?”

The man explained the machinery he maintained. Galen listened, asking short questions when the description reached a term he didn't recognize. Doctor Evil tried twice to help by saying technology. Neither man needed it.

When the mechanic finished, Galen studied the submarine again.

“No hyperdrive. No navigation system that could find my galaxy.”

“I don't know what the first one is.”

“You'd know if you had one.”

That earned a tired little smile.

“Then I don't.”

Doctor Evil stepped closer.

“The important thing is that we're now collaborating.”

“We're talking,” Galen said.

“An essential early phase of collaboration.”

“You told me you'd employed people who built spacecraft.”

“I did.”

“Are any of them here?”

Doctor Evil looked toward the submarine.

Clifford adjusted the helmet under his arm.

Dutch didn't help.

“No,” Doctor Evil said.

Galen stood.

“Then stop talking like they're about to arrive.”

Brian made a very small noise.

The Doctor's eyes moved toward him.

Brian coughed.

“Harbor air.”

“Yes,” the Doctor said. “It's very brave of you to be outdoors.”

Brian's pen appeared.

Professional consultation. International scope. Provided leadership with necessary height of perspective.

He underlined perspective.

Then he looked at the chair beneath him and crossed out height.

Galen pointed to the coast on the chart.

“Do the ships here travel beyond this?”

“Some,” the mechanic said. “Ask their captains where. I don't run them.”

“Who knows the most about the machines in this world?”

The mechanic sat back on his heels.

“I've heard plenty of talk about Piltover. I haven't worked there. I won't tell you there's a man waiting with what you need.”

Galen studied the name when the man indicated it on the chart.

“How far?”

They discussed the route the chart actually showed. Stops, coast, water between places. A place to ask better questions, with no promise of an answer waiting there.

Doctor Evil let them finish.

That restraint cost him visibly.

“We could provide an institutional base,” he said at last.

Dutch turned his head.

“Could we?”

“In principle.”

“Whose principle?”

“Dutch. I'm conducting business.”

“With what?”

The question stayed there.

The Doctor's smile thinned.

“An arrangement would, of course, require terms acceptable to the company.”

“And the people in the boat,” Dutch said.

Galen folded the chart along its existing crease.

“I didn't ask to join.”

He held it until the mechanic's hand was clear, then tucked it away.

“You gave me an answer. That's enough.”

The mechanic rose.

“Wish it was a better one.”

“It's better than his.”

Galen looked directly at Doctor Evil.

The Doctor brought his little finger toward his mouth, reconsidered the gesture and lowered it.

Behind him, Brian was trying very hard to look as if the person delivering this criticism had been his invited guest.

Clifford took one step forward.

“So, uh. You know how to use these?”

He touched the saber on his belt without drawing it.

Galen's eyes dropped to it.

“Keep it away from your neck.”

Dutch looked out over the water.

Clifford's hand stopped moving.

“Yeah. All right.”

Galen left them there.

No new bodyguard. No promise to build a ship. Just the ugly little benefit of finally getting a straight answer.

Brian waited until he was out of conversational range.

“Well,” he said. “I believe that went professionally.”

Dutch looked at Doctor Evil.

“Next time somebody comes looking for a ship, we tell him what kind we got.”

“I never represented this vessel as a spacecraft.”

“You kept letting him think you could help.”

“And we have helped. We eliminated an entire avenue of inquiry.”

The mechanic picked up the little stone he'd used to hold the chart flat. He dropped it back on the quay.

“I'm going below.”

“Thank you,” Dutch said.

The man nodded to him and crossed the plank.

The Doctor watched him go.

“A fine employee. Quite literal.”

“He listened to the question.”

“Dutch, if you're concerned about the cost of this consultation, you'll be delighted to learn there wasn't one.”

Dutch moved close enough that the Doctor stopped arranging his cuffs.

“Those boys didn't cost you much either.”

Clifford looked away.

Brian's pen stopped over the journal.

The Doctor lowered his hands.

“I did not invite him aboard.”

“Keep it that way.”

“That was already the arrangement.”

“Good.”

Dutch held his eyes for another moment, then turned toward the submarine.

Clifford followed him as far as the plank.

“You want me to—”

“No.”

“I just meant if you wanted company.”

Dutch stopped.

Clifford stood with the ridiculous helmet against his ribs and nothing useful to put in the space where three people should have been.

“Not right now,” Dutch said.

He went below.

Clifford stayed on the quay.

Behind him, Brian shut his journal gently.

Doctor Evil glanced at the book.

“Please tell me you haven't written a song.”

“No.”

“Good.”

“It's an assessment.”

“Of course it is.”

Brian reopened it.

“Would you like the assessment?”

“Not right now, Brian.”

He said it in Dutch's exact rhythm.

Brian looked toward the plank and put the journal away without answering.

“Get down from the chair,” Doctor Evil said.

Brian's face tightened.

“Why?”

“We're finished.”

“I can see that.”

“Excellent. We should commemorate the occasion.”

Brian climbed down from the riser. He left the chair on top of it.

The Doctor looked at the chair.

Then Brian.

Then the chair again.

“Both, Brian.”

“I was getting it.”

He went back up and brought the chair down first. The Doctor picked up the wooden riser himself, holding it well clear of Brian as they crossed toward the plank.

At the inland crossing, Arthur stopped where the survey said the old road should meet the newer one.

It did.

Kaze spread his hands.

“Satisfying?”

“Overwhelming.”

They could see the span from there. No welcoming party. A cart was coming across from the far side, its driver leaning to spit between the rails. The world had stubbornly continued having other people in it.

Arthur folded the survey.

“You want to go on ahead again, go.”

Kaze looked at him.

“What?”

“I'm not walking faster because you decided to get bored.”

“I didn't say shit.”

“You make a face.”

“This is my face.”

“Then that's unfortunate.”

Kaze laughed.

Arthur stepped off the road to let the cart through. Kaze stayed where he was until Arthur caught his sleeve and pulled him half a step aside.

The cart rattled past.

Its driver nodded to Arthur. He gave Kaze a second, longer look, then minded his horse.

“You think everybody's looking at you,” Arthur said once the wheels had gone.

“He was.”

“People look at a loose hog, too.”

“You calling me a pig?”

“I'm saying the attention ain't always admiration.”

Kaze put a hand to his chest.

“That hurt.”

“Didn't appear to.”

They stood together at the verge.

For a moment, neither tried to improve it.

Then Kaze nodded toward the road behind them.

“We could've kept the shelf.”

“I didn't want it.”

“After he started talking.”

“After he told us to leave.”

“He didn't have a way to make us.”

Arthur looked at him.

“I know.”

That was the whole answer.

Kaze scratched the side of his jaw.

“You been getting real fucking annoying lately.”

“Had good company.”

Kaze grinned at him.

“Come on, then.”

“Where?”

“Back. Before Chris puts a fucking price on breakfast.”

Arthur started with him.

“You could ask to sit inside once in a while.”

Kaze glanced over.

“Why?”

“You like the game. You like his food.”

“You trying to get rid of me?”

“For an hour? I'd survive it.”

Kaze laughed.

“Go in there with Charles calling himself the warden?”

“You afraid of him?”

“Afraid I'll have to hear him.”

“Then you understand my mornings.”

Kaze shoved his shoulder. Arthur moved with it rather than planting his feet, and the two of them went down the road laughing at the same stupid thing for different reasons.

Neither saw the boy from the cart lean around his father's back to watch them go.

The father caught his sleeve and brought him forward again.

“Eyes here.”

“Who were they?”

“People walking.”

He kept the horse moving.

That was all he gave the boy. Some people didn't want another story to take home.

Inside, Chris had reached the end of the controller cleaning and the beginning of the argument about who had made it dirty.

He put the controller at his station.

“Nobody touch that.”

Charles looked at Meatwad.

Meatwad looked back.

“He means you,” Meatwad said.

“He didn't say my name.”

“I can,” Chris said.

Charles turned toward the camera.

“Let's make the picture.”

Meatwad set the camera on the table and aimed it at the couch. He checked the little screen, shifted it left and stepped out of the shot.

The open doorway beyond the couch's left end was in the picture too, with a strip of the dry platform and its raised lip beyond it.

Charles sat.

“You ready?”

“I been ready.”

“What's the question?”

Charles looked toward Chris.

“Ask me how I came to be here.”

Chris folded the rag.

“You took up with a fucking fish and then wanted to negotiate.”

“Not you. Him.”

Meatwad rolled closer to the camera.

“How'd you come to be here, Charles?”

Charles leaned forward, elbows on his knees.

“People put you someplace. Then they tell you why you're there. Then they keep telling it till you think you said it.”

He pointed at the lens.

“That's why you gotta have your own picture.”

Meatwad considered that.

“Billy ate you, though.”

“I know how I got here.”

“You told me to ask.”

“Ask it where I can answer.”

Shake sat down beside him.

“We need a host.”

“We got two,” Meatwad said.

“Exactly. That's how badly it's going.”

Frylock hovered near the table.

“If you want your own interview, ask.”

“I don't want an interview. I want editorial control.”

Chris lifted the rag toward him.

“You want to contribute, wipe your nasty shit off my controller next time.”

Shake stood.

“I don't use that controller.”

“Good. Keep practicing.”

Frylock glanced toward the workshop passage.

“While you're in there, I still want to look through the salvage.”

Chris narrowed his eyes.

“For what?”

“Anything that measures electrical activity. This entire place behaves like living tissue. I'd like to know how much of it responds independently.”

“By doing what to it?”

“A controlled stimulus.”

“That's science for poking shit till somebody screams.”

“I'd start small.”

“That's small science for poking shit till somebody screams.”

Frylock looked at the wall.

“You don't want to know how your house works?”

“I want to know it still works when you get done.”

Outside, Billy lowered the banjo toward his face.

“Christopher. Are we discussing an experiment?”

“He wants to zap your fucking wallpaper.”

“I said measure,” Frylock told him.

“He wants to measure it while he zaps your fucking wallpaper.”

Billy considered that.

“Tell our learned friend he may submit the idea to me before inserting anything into my anything.”

Chris pointed toward the exterior monitor.

“Ask him first.”

“You're making it sound worse than it is.”

“Then tell it better.”

Frylock looked toward the banjo's owner on the screen. Billy was waiting.

“Fine. Later.”

“He says later.”

“A splendid appointment,” Billy said. “I shall remain unprodded until then.”

Frylock left the wall alone and turned toward Meatwad.

“Did you ask him?”

Meatwad looked at Chris.

“Not yet.”

“Ask me what?” Chris said.

“I was thinking about having a door.”

Chris looked toward the passage.

“We got doors.”

“One that's mine.”

Shake threw up his hands.

“Here we go. The mansion.”

“I didn't say mansion.”

“He wants an entire house,” Shake told Chris.

“I said I was thinking about a room.”

“First the room. Then a hallway. I know how these people operate.”

Chris looked at Meatwad.

“What you need a door for?”

“So I can close it when I want.”

“You can go anywhere in here.”

“So can he.”

Meatwad looked at Shake.

Shake nodded.

“That's right. And I will.”

Frylock drifted between them.

“He's asking for somewhere his things stay where he puts them.”

“I got a stone,” Meatwad said. “I put it someplace, I want it to be that place.”

“Your fucking mountain rock?” Chris asked.

“It's a good rock.”

“I didn't say it was a bad rock.”

“Shake did.”

“Because it's a bad rock,” Shake said. “It's not even the whole mountain.”

Charles leaned toward Meatwad.

“You get a door, you gotta have somebody watch it.”

“Then I ain't alone.”

“You don't see me. I'm on the other side.”

Meatwad looked at him for a long time.

“You know that ain't helping.”

Chris set the controller down out of reach.

“I ain't building you a fucking apartment this morning. Pick a place for your stuff, show me where, and I'll tell them to leave it.”

“Tell them now.”

Chris looked at the couch.

“Leave his shit.”

Charles nodded immediately.

Shake folded his arms.

“This is how communities collapse.”

“You heard about the silence bottle,” Frylock said. “Try living up to it.”

“I haven't touched that bottle.”

Frylock glanced at the open Pepsi still beside the couch where Chris had put it the night before.

“Then this is an excellent opportunity to start leaving things alone.”

Meatwad went to get his stone. He set it well back from the couch, in a place where nobody needed to walk, then came back to check the camera's view again.

Frylock watched him do it.

“Better?”

Meatwad looked back at the little stone.

“For now.”

Shake made another noise through his straw.

Frylock turned toward him.

“You know, you can dislike something without making everybody hear it.”

“Then how would they know?”

“They might get a nice surprise.”

Meatwad checked the camera one last time.

“You can still see both of us if we sit on the couch.”

“I don't need both of us,” Charles said.

“You need somebody to ask.”

Charles looked at the empty place beside his.

“Yeah.”

“That's why I put it there.”

Chris took the rag toward the workshop side. He left the lounge by the dry passage, still inside the same living domain, to put it away with his own things.

Meatwad stopped the recording.

Charles got up.

“Come here. Me and you gonna work out the questions without them.”

“You gonna tell me your answers first?”

“I'm gonna show you what to ask.”

“That's what I'm asking.”

They went around the turn in the dry passage toward storage, still arguing about which one of them had asked the wrong question.

Shake stayed by the couch.

“All right. I'll warm up.”

He reached down and pressed the camera's record button himself.

Its little light came on.

In the workshop, Chris set the rag down. A moment later, he heard Frylock say something from the direction of the lounge.

He couldn't make out the words.

Then Shake's voice rose.

Chris turned toward the passage.

There was a splash.

Not a little one.

Something cracked against the couch. A sharp flash lit the passage. Shake shouted, and Chris was already running.

Frylock came up from beyond the raised edge, flying crookedly over the lip of living flesh. He struck the couch instead of clearing it and slid onto the floor.

Shake stood back from the edge.

The upper rim of his cup was scorched. His straw bent in the middle.

Dark little pits marked one glove where the splash had caught it.

Chris dropped beside Frylock.

“What the fuck happened?”

“He fell,” Shake said.

Frylock tried to lift himself.

He rose a few inches.

Then struck the floor again.

Chris reached for him and stopped when he saw what the acid had done.

“Billy.”

Outside, the banjo spoke his name without a joke attached to it.

Billy sat upright.

“Christopher?”

“Get Daynah. Get everybody back here.”

“What has happened?”

Chris looked at the face in front of him.

Frylock's eyes were open.

“Acid,” Chris said. “Frylock. It's bad.”

Billy was on his feet.

Daynah stood with him.

The dog rose between them, suddenly awake.

“Where?” Daynah asked.

Billy pointed toward the bend Arthur and Kaze had taken.

Daynah ran.

Inside, Charles and Meatwad reached the lounge together.

Charles stopped in the doorway.

Meatwad didn't.

“Frylock?”

Chris shifted to keep him from rolling into the wet acid on the floor.

“Stay there.”

“What's wrong with him?”

“Stay there, man.”

Frylock moved his mouth.

Chris leaned closer.

“Say it again.”

No words came out.

Shake looked toward the dry passage.

Then at the camera on the table.

He took one step toward it.

“Leave my shit,” Chris said.

Shake froze.

Chris wasn't looking at him anymore.

“Billy. Listen to me. I need you right here.”

“I am here.”

“I know. I know you are. Just—”

Frylock's eyes moved toward the sound of Meatwad's voice.

Meatwad was saying his name again.

Chris put one hand carefully under Frylock's head.

The next breath didn't come.

He waited for it.

Meatwad waited because Chris was waiting.

Then Chris stopped calling for help.
