Season 3, Episode 22: A Bigger Hammer

“He's standing in the wrong place,” Briar said.

Below the road, a block of pale stone hung from a quarry crane. It was larger than the cart waiting to receive it. Six workers stood well away from its shadow.

One man stood directly beneath it.

Kaze stopped beside Briar at the roadside wall. They'd been walking inland through Noxian hills for most of the morning, following the road she'd chosen after their first night together. The sea had disappeared behind them. Here, hammer blows carried between slopes stripped white by generations of stonecutters.

“Maybe he works there.”

“They keep asking him to move.”

“Maybe he doesn't work there anymore.”

A bearded foreman in a leather apron waved both arms at the man beneath the block.

“That chain is slipping! Get out from under it!”

The stranger looked up. He was lean, bare-chested and covered in blue tattoos, with close-cut hair and a braided beard. Dust whitened his trousers. He seemed considerably more interested in the stone than the man shouting about it.

Oh, no.

I knew that face. Baldur. A god from the lands I'd once called home. I'd seen him meet a final death there, and yet here he stood, apparently attempting to make another appointment.

His mother, Freya, had once enchanted him against harm. Her protection had also taken the feeling out of his life: warmth, cold, pain, pleasure. He could still rage, hope and hate. Gods, could he hate. I didn't know how he'd come to be standing in a Noxian quarry. I recognized the reckless invitation beneath that falling stone.

Baldur reached up, caught the hanging chain and pulled.

The crane's wooden arm snapped.

The block struck him squarely and smashed through the platform beneath his feet. Stone chips flew across the working floor. The foreman fell backward. Two men ran for the access ramp; another ducked behind a loaded cart.

Kaze leaned farther over the wall.

“Well. He definitely don't work there now.”

For a moment, nothing moved beneath the block.

Then it tilted.

Baldur pushed himself out from underneath, one shoulder hanging at a wrong angle. He caught his wrist, rolled the shoulder back into place and stood. Blood ran down his chest from a split in the skin, then stopped as the wound closed.

He looked at the block with naked disappointment.

Kaze laughed.

Baldur's head turned toward the sound.

“That funny?”

“A little.”

“Come down here.”

Kaze glanced at Briar.

She was still watching the man who'd crawled out from beneath several tons of stone.

“You want to,” she said.

“I was gonna ask if you did.”

“Yes.”

“See? That's why I like you.”

“We've hardly started walking.”

“Making good time.”

They went down the ramp.

Briar walked close enough that the edge of her pillory brushed his arm.

“Yesterday, you said you'd just landed.”

“Yeah?”

“How far had you jumped?”

“From the next headland. Why?”

“Could you jump over these hills?”

Kaze looked toward the ridge beyond the quarry.

“If I got a good place to land. You want a ride?”

“I'm wondering why we're walking.”

“You picked the road.”

“I picked where it went. You decided to use your feet.”

He started to answer, stopped and looked at her.

“You got me there.”

She smiled, pleased by the admission.

“Ask me again when you're done down here.”

“You could just say you want me to hold you.”

“I could.”

She let the possibility hang while she stepped around a pothole. Kaze watched her, then caught up.

“You're doing that on purpose.”

“I'm trying to impress somebody.”

His own line from the wall. She'd remembered, and had found a better place to put it. Kaze's laughter followed her the rest of the way down.

The quarry had been cut into the hillside in broad steps. The working floor lay below the road. Beyond it, an older excavation dropped another level, beneath a great overhanging roof of rock. Three narrow stone piers had been left to support that roof. A faded warning board barred the path down.

On the opposite side, a spoil ridge rose toward scrub-covered hills. Briar stopped on that ridge while Kaze crossed the floor. She had a clear view of Baldur, the broken crane and the workers collecting their belongings with urgent efficiency.

The foreman intercepted Kaze.

“You know him?”

“No.”

“Then don't encourage him.”

Kaze looked past the man's shoulder. Baldur was turning his hand in front of his face, opening and closing the fingers.

“How long he been doing this?”

“Since we found him here at dawn. Says he doesn't know where he is. Won't leave. Won't listen. Broke a wedge trying to drive it through his own hand.”

“And you kept working?”

“We stopped when he got under the crane.”

The foreman pointed toward the blocks stacked beside the carts.

“Those were supposed to leave at noon. Harbor watch sent a rider this morning. Their gate fell down yesterday. They want stone, they want it quickly, and apparently asking where we'll find more wagons is insubordination.”

Kaze looked at the waiting load.

“For the gate?”

“You heard me.”

He had. Kaze's smile returned, smaller and considerably less helpful.

Briar looked at him. She'd watched him bring that gate down the previous afternoon. Now here was another man rearranging his day around the hole they'd left behind.

“What's funny?” the foreman asked.

“Nothing. Long morning.”

“It's getting longer.”

Kaze didn't mention his part in it. He glanced once more at the neatly squared blocks, then at Baldur. Whatever private amusement he'd found, it hadn't discouraged him from going farther into the quarry.

The foreman gestured helplessly at the wreckage.

“He wouldn't move.”

“All right. I see the problem.”

“Can you get him out?”

“I didn't say I was fixing it.”

Kaze stepped around him.

Baldur watched the black living plates along his right arm. The fibers between them shifted as Kaze flexed his hand.

“What are you?”

“You first.”

“Baldur.”

“That's your name.”

“And yours is written on your stomach. Saves us some time.”

Kaze's smile widened.

“Where'd you come from, Baldur?”

“There was snow. Trees. Then I was here.” He looked around the quarry. “These people say it's Noxus. That means nothing to me.”

“Yeah, a lot of people say that before somebody takes their house.”

Baldur ignored the joke.

“You aren't like them.”

“I'm better company.”

“Prove it.”

The demand came so quickly that Kaze blinked, then laughed again.

“Hold on. What are we proving? You dropped a fucking building on yourself. You want me to clap?”

“I want you to hit me.”

Kaze studied him. The wound on Baldur's chest had vanished. His shoulder moved freely. Nothing in his face suggested that either recovery had brought relief.

“Why?”

“Because I'm asking.”

“That's a terrible reason.”

“Afraid you'll hurt your hand?”

“There it is.” Kaze nodded to himself. “You got tired of waiting for the question to work.”

Baldur moved closer.

Kaze's attention sharpened. Beneath the ordinary tension of the encounter, his deeper warning sense caught the immediate threat in the stranger's body. This man could put force behind the invitation. Kaze felt it, understood it, and stayed where he was.

Above them, Briar sat on a broad piece of discarded stone. Her red pillory rested across her shoulders, its hemolith helping her keep the hunger in her blood quiet enough to think. She'd been made as a weapon herself. She was curious about what this one was asking for.

“Don't do it beside the carts,” the foreman said.

Baldur didn't even look at him.

Kaze did.

“You should probably move those.”

The foreman swore and ran.

Baldur struck first.

His fist drove across Kaze's mouth, turning his head and splitting his lower lip against his own teeth. Thick black blood gathered at the wound. Kaze slid one shoe back through the dust, caught his balance and looked at him again.

The pain had reached him. The grin came afterward.

“Okay.”

He stepped in and punched Baldur beneath the ribs.

The god folded around the fist and left the ground. He crossed the quarry floor backward, hit a stack of cut blocks and drove through them into the wall beyond. Stone burst outward around his shoulders. The impact cracked the cliff face from the floor to the first terrace.

Kaze shook blood from his chin. His lip was already drawing closed.

Briar had stood up.

So had Baldur.

He climbed out of the broken stone, breathing hard. Blood darkened his beard. He pressed a hand against his ribs while they settled beneath the skin, then stared at Kaze with an expression that briefly looked like hope.

“Again.”

Kaze tilted his head.

“That wasn't enough?”

Baldur's expression collapsed.

“Apparently not.”

He came across the floor in a rush.

Kaze's right eye tracked the shift in his hips before the kick. Baldur swerved left, cut back sharply and drove a heel toward his head. Kaze ducked beneath it, caught the passing thigh and turned with the momentum.

He smashed Baldur into the ground.

The quarry floor caved around the impact. A loaded cart jumped on its wheels. Its driver let go of the reins and fled uphill as the horse fought its traces.

Baldur rolled through the broken surface and struck upward from one knee. Kaze caught the punch on his living right forearm. The impact jolted through his shoulder. Before Baldur could withdraw, Kaze closed his left hand over the man's wrist, stepped behind him and drove a knee into his spine.

Baldur's back bent. Kaze lifted him by the captured arm and hammered him face first into the floor.

Once was enough to bury his head in stone.

Kaze released him and crouched beside the crater.

“You feel that one?”

Baldur pulled his face free. His nose was broken, his mouth full of blood. As Kaze watched, the damage drew back into place.

“No.”

He spat and swung from the ground.

Kaze leaned away. The fist passed his throat. Baldur planted his other hand, kicked himself upright and drove both fists down against the floor.

A straight rupture tore through the stone toward Kaze.

He jumped. The shock passed beneath him and burst against the wall, bringing loose rock down over the broken carts. Baldur sprang after him.

They collided above the working floor.

Baldur caught Kaze around the waist and carried their combined momentum into the cliff. Rock punched inward around their bodies. The upper terrace cracked, and a shower of white fragments swept across the quarry like thrown gravel.

Briar ducked behind her stone as pieces struck it. On the road, the foreman dragged a stunned worker away from the rim.

Inside the new hollow in the cliff, Baldur drove a fist toward Kaze's face.

Kaze turned his head. The punch buried itself beside him.

He drove his forehead into Baldur's nose, caught the back of his neck and shoved his face against the rock. Then Kaze planted both feet beneath himself and launched out of the hollow with Baldur in his grip.

They hit the opposite wall of the quarry above the old excavation.

The sound was enormous. It reached me as one blow, then another as a slab of cliff sheared free and struck the floor below. Dust climbed higher than the road.

Kaze stood on a newly broken ledge. Baldur lay at his feet, neck twisted under one shoulder.

For a few breaths, there was only falling stone.

Then Baldur's hand moved.

His head shifted back into place. He sucked in air and sat up.

Kaze stared down at him.

“Oh, that's some bullshit.”

Baldur laughed once, without pleasure.

“Now you're understanding.”

I knew that broken neck wouldn't finish him. Kaze had just learned it the hard way. Knowing what a spell prevented, though, was a long way from knowing how it had been made.

“No, wait.” Kaze held a finger up. “You said you didn't feel it. You mean you didn't feel enough, or you didn't feel anything?”

“Nothing.”

“That whole time?”

“Nothing.”

Kaze looked from the man to the ruined cliff, then back again.

“The sun?” he asked.

Baldur's eyes narrowed.

“What about it?”

“Can you feel that?”

“No.”

Kaze stopped smiling. He turned his face slightly toward the warmth on the open ledge, then watched Baldur stare back at him.

“How long?”

“Long enough.”

The answer was vicious. Baldur glanced down at his unmarked chest and rubbed a thumb over the place Kaze's fist had first struck him.

“For a moment, I thought you might be different.”

“You thought I was different before I did anything.”

“Yes.”

The admission took some of the performance out of the exchange. Kaze looked at him with fresh interest.

“Nigga, you could've led with that.”

Baldur stood.

“Would it have made you hit harder?”

“Would've changed what I was trying.”

For the first time, Baldur's attention settled completely.

“Then change it.”

Kaze looked at his own right hand.

Living fibers ran across his waist. A dark sheath formed at his left hip. He drew Black Marrow with his right hand, the long blade completing itself as it cleared the sheath. The empty sheath withdrew into him.

Baldur watched the irregular living edge.

“There you are.”

“Don't get excited yet.”

He lunged before Kaze finished speaking.

The sword shifted with Kaze's warning instinct. Its spine met Baldur's forearm, turning the reaching hand away. Kaze stepped outside the other fist and drove the point through Baldur's chest, pushing him back against the cliff.

It emerged between his shoulder blades and entered the cliff behind him.

Baldur stopped.

His hands closed around Kaze's wrist. He looked down at the blade passing through him, then up at Kaze. Blood touched his lips.

For an instant he seemed to be listening for something.

“Well?” Kaze asked.

Baldur's fingers tightened.

“No.”

The word came out small. The next came out furious.

“No!”

He wrenched himself forward along the blade and drove his forehead into Kaze's face.

Kaze's nose broke. Black blood oozed over his upper lip. He pulled the sword free and kicked Baldur away from the cliff. The god fell into the old cut below, hit the sloping rubble and rolled onto the lowest floor.

Kaze pressed his nose straight. Beneath his fingers, damaged tissue began knitting. It hurt. His eyes watered. He laughed through it anyway, then spat blood over the ledge.

Below him, Baldur got up with his chest already closing.

“What did you do?” Baldur shouted.

Kaze looked down.

“Stabbed you.”

“You were holding something back.”

“Yeah. Several things.”

“Use them.”

The amusement left Kaze's face.

“Ask different.”

“I saw that weapon. I saw your body. You have more.”

“And now you're telling me what to do with it.”

Baldur kicked a fallen block across the pit. It struck the wall beneath Kaze and burst apart.

“Do you think this is amusing?”

Kaze glanced at the enormous damage they'd done together.

“I did.”

Briar had moved along the spoil ridge to keep them in sight. She stood above the old cut, well away from its overhanging roof. Dust coated her pale hair. She looked from Baldur to Kaze without calling either of them back to order.

“Who did it?” she asked Baldur.

He looked up at her.

“What?”

“Made it so you couldn't feel.”

“My mother.”

His mouth twisted around the word.

“She called it protection.”

Briar's fingers rested against the red frame around her neck.

“Did you ask her to?”

“No.”

For a moment, his fury had somewhere older to go. Then his gaze snapped back to Kaze.

“You can do something.”

“Maybe.”

“Then do it.”

Kaze stared at him. His nose had finished healing. Black blood remained across his mouth and chin.

“See, now I don't want to.”

Baldur jumped.

Kaze met him at the ledge. He kept Black Marrow clear in his right hand and caught Baldur's throat with his left. The god's momentum drove Kaze back half a step before his feet settled.

Baldur struck at his face.

Kaze turned, hauled him sideways and drove him down through the edge of the ledge.

Stone broke beneath both their feet.

They fell into the lower excavation. Kaze landed astride Baldur and forced him into the floor with his left forearm across his throat. Baldur grabbed that arm with both hands and strained. Cracks widened beneath his shoulders, but the hold stayed.

“Listen to me,” Kaze said.

Baldur spat at him.

Kaze wiped the spit from his cheek with his shoulder, irritated rather than surprised.

“All right. Don't.”

He drove his knee into Baldur's chest, rose and kicked him across the lower floor. The god struck the base of the far wall beneath the overhang.

Kaze looked up.

There was a whole hillside above that stone roof.

He smiled again.

Briar saw the look, followed his eyes and stepped farther back from the rim.

Kaze went to the nearest supporting pier.

He swung Black Marrow through it. Cursed energy ran along the blade, a contact-cutting technique called Cleave helping the edge part the thick stone. He circled the narrow support to finish the cut from the other side.

The pier remained standing.

Kaze crossed to the second.

Baldur tore himself out of the rubble and rushed him. Kaze turned the sword clear of his own body, slipped the reaching hands and drove an elbow into Baldur's jaw. A sweep took the god's legs out from under him.

Before Baldur rose, Black Marrow passed through the second pier.

Another cut completed. Another support that hadn't yet fallen.

Kaze was holding the result back. Black Marrow could leave cuts pending until he fully seated it in its sheath. Once he did, the stone he'd actually reached would part along those lines.

Baldur caught him at the third pier.

His shoulder drove into Kaze's ribs. They struck the stone together, cracking its surface. Baldur followed with two short punches into the same side. Kaze grunted, planted his feet and shoved him back at arm's length.

The third cut passed between them, through the damaged support.

Baldur looked at the blade, then at the still-standing piers.

“You missed.”

Kaze drove his heel into Baldur's stomach.

The impact threw him beneath the deepest part of the roof. Kaze launched the other way, leaving a crater where he'd stood. He crossed the lower pit, struck the opposite wall with one foot and rebounded toward the open sky.

For an instant, Baldur saw him above the rim.

The sheath grew again at Kaze's left hip.

He seated Black Marrow completely.

All three piers came apart.

The roof dropped.

It didn't fall in neat pieces. An entire shelf of pale stone tilted into the excavation, carrying the soil, scrub and larger rock above it. The first impact threw a wall of dust across the pit. Then the rest of the hillside moved.

Baldur leaped toward the open end.

A slab struck him in midair and drove him back down. More stone followed. The lower excavation filled, spilled over, and surged across the working floor, burying the broken crane and crushing the abandoned carts against the far wall.

Kaze landed beside Briar on the stable side of the spoil ridge. Loose grit bounced around their feet. Below them, the slope kept settling with deep, grinding reports.

When the dust reached them, Briar shut her eyes and turned into his shoulder. Kaze put his free arm around her, more to hold her close than to shield either of them from dirt.

The roar diminished.

Something struck from beneath the fallen rock.

Another blow followed.

Kaze listened, head slightly tilted.

“Still there.”

“You thought that would kill him?” Briar asked.

“No. I wanted him over there.”

Below, a boulder shifted. Baldur's hand appeared between two slabs, seized an edge and began forcing it outward.

The curse hadn't broken. The man hadn't surrendered. But he'd have to move a substantial part of the hill before he could continue the conversation.

Kaze's right hand stayed on the hilt until the seated sword folded back through his palm. Its temporary sheath withdrew with it.

Across the quarry, the foreman stood on the access road with his workers. One had an arm wrapped in a bloodied shirt. The horse had broken free of the cart and was running up the road with part of the harness dragging behind it.

The foreman looked at the place where his working floor had been.

Then he looked at Kaze.

“You!”

Kaze raised his eyebrows.

The man opened his mouth, saw the dark blood on Kaze's face, and closed it again.

There would be no more stone leaving that quarry today. Wages, orders, carts, tools: all of it lay under the hillside or stood waiting beside it. The harbor watch would have to find another supplier for the gate Kaze had demolished. This one had just acquired a god in its foundations.

Briar touched his side.

“He hit you there.”

“Yeah.”

“Does it still hurt?”

Kaze took a breath, testing the place beneath her fingers. The soreness was fading as his body repaired itself.

“Less.”

She looked toward the buried god. Another slab moved.

“I wouldn't want that.”

“The rocks?”

“Not feeling anything.”

Kaze glanced at her pillory, then back at her face.

“You still feel things with that on.”

“Yes. It helps me choose what I do with them.”

They began climbing away from the quarry, following the unbroken side of the ridge. Kaze's pants were torn along one knee where the cliff had caught him. White dust covered the fabric. The black stains down his chest and the red smears on his forearm hadn't gone anywhere just because the wounds had closed.

Briar kept looking at his face.

“What?” he asked.

“You have someone else's blood on your ear.”

He wiped the wrong one.

“Other side.”

He tried again.

“Now it's in your hair.”

Kaze stopped and looked at his hand.

“You could've helped.”

“I am. I'm telling you where it went.”

He laughed despite himself. She stepped closer and took his chin between her fingers, turning his face toward the light.

“There,” she said. “You've made it worse.”

“You always this sweet after somebody puts a hill down?”

“I haven't had many opportunities to compare.”

He leaned toward her. She met him halfway for a brief kiss, then drew back and wiped a dark mark from the corner of her mouth.

“You're disgusting.”

“You came over here.”

“I know.”

Below the ridge, stone crashed again. Baldur was making progress.

Kaze looked back. A section of the rubble shifted, and a tattooed arm pushed up through the dust.

“He really wants you to stay,” Briar said.

“He wants something out of me.”

“So do I.”

Kaze turned toward her slowly.

She smiled.

“Better view.”

He looked uphill. Beyond the next crest, the land dropped toward a river valley. There was solid exposed rock beneath their feet and another broad shelf visible across the shallow ravine ahead.

“I can do that.”

“You're carrying me.”

“Wasn't gonna throw you.”

“Good. We're agreeing before you start.”

He crouched and slid one arm beneath her knees, the other behind her back. Briar settled against him, adjusting the red frame so it cleared his jaw. Her hands rested where she could reach his shoulders.

“Like this?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Hold on.”

She did.

Kaze launched from the rock.

The quarry fell away beneath them. Briar's grip tightened as the air rushed past, then she lifted her face into it. They crossed the ravine in a clean arc. Kaze landed on the far shelf, bent through the impact and kept her securely against him while small stones skipped down the slope.

The river came into view below: a broad green curve between fields, with red roofs gathered around a crossing farther inland.

For a moment, Briar said nothing.

Then she laughed, delighted.

Kaze looked down at her.

“That one worked?”

“Again.”

He glanced back toward the hidden quarry, then at the woman in his arms.

“You got much better delivery.”

This time, he was pleased to oblige.
