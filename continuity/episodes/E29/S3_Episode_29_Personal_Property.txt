Season 3, Episode 29: Personal Property

“Say it again,” Billy said.

Outside on the Zaun service road, Chris closed his eyes.

“Our fountain.”

Inside Billy's protected interior home, the great catfish stood beside the bronze carp they'd stolen from Heisenberg's collection house. It had no basin, no plumbing, and no water. Chris had nevertheless called it his fountain, which Billy intended to enjoy until the last fucking syllable wore out.

Meatwad came around the couch carrying the camera.

“Hold on. I wasn't ready.”

“You were ready enough to overhear,” Billy said.

“Yeah, but I can't put my ears on the television.”

The little ball of meat aimed the lens upward. Charles Manson, his fellow unwanted roommate and partner in Manson & Meatwad's Inside Job, positioned himself beside the fish.

“Now, what happened here is a question of ownership.”

Arthur took his hat from the couch.

“You weren't here.”

“I'm here now.”

“For the explanation. Not the stealing.”

Charles put one hand on the carp. “Everything comes from somewhere, man.”

“Take your hand off him,” Billy said.

On the live exterior monitor, Chris opened his eyes again.

“Can we leave before somebody gives that thing a birth certificate?”

Daniel folded Harl's coastal sketch. His brow had healed; the soreness from the robbery was already easing. The small simian still carried his spear and his bottle, and still looked as though putting one down would be admitting defeat.

“Good news. Dad's taking us out.”

“I ain't your dad.”

“That's what makes the abandonment hurt, Chris.”

Arthur chuckled despite himself. Billy released both men, and the three moved down the service road, leaving the stolen money secure inside. Twenty-one hundred new silver coins. Enough to make the robbery worthwhile. Not enough to stop them arguing about a decorative fish.

Beyond the busy approach roads, Chris deployed his blue Subaru on an open stretch of hard ground. He checked it before Arthur climbed into the passenger seat. The vehicle had fuel left from their earlier journey; recalling it hadn't replenished a drop, and Chris kept the next coastal stopping places in mind.

Daniel rose alongside on his cloud, Harl's paper tucked away. Chris secured the banjo where he could hear it, then changed the same car into its winged flight form. Metal shifted around the cabin. Arthur tightened his belt without needing to be shown again.

“If Billy starts talking about how high he could jump back in the day,” Chris said, “you can turn that thing over.”

“I can still hear you,” Billy replied through the banjo.

“That's how conversations work.”

They left Zaun behind while there was daylight to use. Chris followed the coast they'd already traveled, with Arthur watching the landmarks and Daniel ranging close enough to return. They wanted the particular Noxian harbor Kaze knew, not a random landing somewhere in an empire.

At dusk they stopped on bare coastal ground. Everyone got clear before Chris recalled the vehicle. Billy took the exterior for the night, and Chris went inside, where Meatwad immediately asked whether flying was difficult.

“The flying part's fine.”

“What's the hard part?”

Chris looked at Charles lying across the couch with both feet up.

“Deciding who you bring back.”

They slept, ate, and continued at first light, Chris taking the exterior again to pilot. By the following afternoon, the damaged harbor crane was visible ahead.

The gate below it looked worse.

One tower still stood. Much of the other lay across the lower approach in a slope of broken masonry. Wagons crawled around an improvised inland diversion. Men were breaking stone where there had once been an arch.

Chris brought the aircraft down on clear ground above the road. Arthur studied the wreck through the canopy.

“Reckon we're getting warmer.”

Below them, at the outdoor eating house overlooking the water, Kaze was explaining that he did not want to buy passage for a siege engine.

“Then why,” the proprietor asked, “have you brought one to the harbor?”

Kaze looked back at the ballista on its wheeled carriage. Its dark frame occupied most of the space beside the terrace. Samira sat on the low wall beyond it, her injured left wrist still braced, one boot against the stone.

“Because that's where I was going.”

“People are asking.”

“Tell them it's my luggage.”

“No,” said the quiet voice from the weapon.

The proprietor slowly lowered the cloth in his hand.

Kaze smiled. “She doesn't like that.”

Naganeka of Zuretta was imprisoned within the ballista. Kaze had taken the weapon from a sale in Lask and removed its newer transport bands. The older binding remained. Whatever else he thought he'd acquired, it wasn't an obedient toy.

He and Samira had followed the coastward road through the previous afternoon, stopped overnight, and reached the port that morning. They'd taken the inland diversion around the gate Kaze himself had destroyed.

Samira had enjoyed that part.

“You could clear the road,” she said.

“I'm sitting down.”

“You're standing.”

“About to sit down.”

“Ambitious day.”

He moved close enough that she had to tilt her head to keep looking at him.

“Come find somewhere quieter with me. I'll make an effort.”

Her smile arrived slowly.

“You can start by finding my guns.”

“Thought you liked my hands.”

“I like several expensive things. They aren't interchangeable.”

“That's a fucking shame.”

She caught his chin with her sound hand, kissed him once, and pushed him back just enough to look him over.

“You heard me.”

Then Daniel called from above the road.

“Kaze!”

He came down on his cloud beyond the terrace. He'd asked a carter below the landing ground about Kaze; the man had pointed out the siege engine obstructing the eating house. From the slope, Daniel had spotted its shirtless owner beside the wall. Chris and Arthur followed on foot after recalling the empty aircraft.

Kaze's grin spread. He left the wall and met Daniel halfway, caught his offered hand, then pulled him into a hard one-armed embrace.

“Where the fuck have you been?”

“Robbing a man with a much nicer office than us.”

“You didn't wait?”

“He was open.”

Chris reached them and stopped in front of the ballista.

“Jesus Christ.”

“Missed you too,” Kaze said.

Chris leaned sideways to inspect the carriage. “You went out by yourself and found a crossbow with a mortgage.”

“It talks.”

“Of course it does.”

Arthur had stopped at the edge of the terrace.

Samira was on her feet.

For a moment the harbor carried on around them: hammers below the ruined gate, a cart wheel scraping stone, somebody shouting down to a boat. Arthur looked at her face, then at the brace on her wrist.

He knew precisely who she was.

He'd broken that wrist and shot her through the chest during the Sins' robbery of a private sale. Her death had been real. Her return was real too, however little either of them understood it. Arthur's hand stayed clear of his revolver.

“Well,” he said quietly.

Samira's good hand rested beside the ordinary pistol at her waist.

“Finish that sentence carefully.”

Kaze looked between them. “She's been wanting her stuff back.”

“I gathered.”

“Have you?” Samira asked.

The proprietor put down his cloth.

“Not here.”

No one moved.

He pointed at the broken gate. “Whatever this is, take it somewhere I haven't already lost customers.”

Arthur glanced toward the road, then back to Samira.

“Fair request.”

Aye. Sometimes the wisest man in a conversation is the one whose tables are still intact.

They moved to the bare shelf above the harbor, with Kaze pulling Naganeka's carriage behind them. Samira kept Arthur ahead of her. He allowed it without pretending not to notice.

Once they were clear of the road, Chris yielded the exterior. Billy stood in his place, vast and broad-mouthed, looking from Samira to the ballista.

“Well, this is a considerable improvement on the fish.”

Chris's voice came through the permitted banjo outlet.

“Don't start collecting her.”

“I hadn't introduced myself.”

“You were looking at the wheels.”

Samira interrupted them.

“My sword. My pistols.”

Billy looked at Arthur.

Arthur took off his hat, ran a hand through his hair, and put it back on.

“Before that. You planning to shoot me?”

“I've been planning it for days.”

Daniel's grin disappeared. He stepped away from the line between them, bottle lowered. Kaze stayed beside the carriage.

Arthur nodded once.

“Then let's talk plain.”

Samira drew the cheap pistol with her right hand. She didn't raise it. Arthur watched the muzzle, then looked back at her.

“You can try with that one,” he said. “Or I can give back what I took, and we don't do this today.”

“That's your apology?”

“Didn't say it was.”

“No. You didn't.”

Her jaw tightened. Kaze opened his mouth; she turned her eye toward him.

“Don't.”

He let the word he hadn't said stay unsaid.

The wind pulled at Arthur's coat. Samira shifted the pistol in her palm, feeling its unfamiliar balance. She'd killed a man with one of these miserable guns. It would fire. That wasn't the question.

“You broke my wrist before you shot me.”

“I remember.”

“I want you to remember it when you see me holding both of them again.”

Arthur's expression hardened a little.

“I expect I will.”

She looked at him for another long moment, then put the ordinary pistol away.

“Not today.”

Billy waited until Arthur gave him a small nod. Then he released the stolen weapons onto the clear ground: Samira's two specialist pistols and the broad blade made for her by Lani and Miel. No presentation, no condition about joining them. The things were hers again when she bent to take them.

She knelt carefully, keeping her left wrist out of the work. First one pistol, then the other. She checked their condition and contents instead of trusting the reunion. The blade she turned so the afternoon light ran along its edge.

Chris watched through the live feed inside.

“She knows those things better than I know half my relatives.”

“You like half your relatives?” Meatwad asked beside him.

“No.”

“Then she knows them way better.”

Outside, Samira sat back on her heels. She rested her good hand on the sword's grip and let out a breath she'd been holding.

Kaze crouched beside her.

“There.”

“Don't sound proud. You brought me to the men who stole them.”

“Saved you another fucking journey, though.”

She laughed once, unwillingly, then looked past him at Arthur.

“They need checking before I put them through anything interesting.”

“Your wrist too,” Arthur said.

“You don't get to give me advice about that.”

He accepted the correction with a small tilt of his head.

Daniel walked over to Kaze while Samira arranged her recovered weapons for carrying.

“Where's Briar?”

“Went north. Wanted to see what was up there.”

“All right?”

“Yeah.”

Daniel nodded, then looked over the enormous weapon Kaze had brought in her place.

Kaze tapped the ballista's frame.

“This is Naganeka.”

Billy lowered his head toward it.

“Madam. If you wish to travel inside, there is ample room for you and your carriage.”

“Do you swallow everyone you travel with?” she asked.

“Only with suitable arrangements.”

“Some of us got a room,” Daniel said.

“Some of you have made a room less suitable,” Billy replied.

Naganeka spoke again, low enough that they had to stand close.

“I will not exchange one forgotten storehouse for another.”

Billy's expression lost its amusement.

“You won't be put in my home against your wishes merely because this gentleman brought you.”

Kaze lifted both hands. “I didn't ask you to.”

“You were about to offer me a siege engine.”

“I was about to ask if it would fit.”

“It would fit.”

“See? Positive conversation.”

Naganeka declined the invitation. For now the carriage stayed on the shelf beside them, its wheels braced with stones. Billy remained outside with it while he admitted Arthur, Daniel and Kaze into the living space within him. Samira chose to stay out, checking the straps that would let her carry her own property south.

Inside, Kaze had barely reached the couch before Charles stepped in front of him.

“You've been gone a while.”

Meatwad held the camera. The lens followed Kaze as he looked past Charles, toward the enormous bronze carp standing near the recovered counting cage.

“What the fuck is that?”

“That's exactly what we're here to find out,” Charles said.

Chris was at his workbench. He turned on the stool.

“It's a fountain.”

“It's dry.”

“It's a future fountain.”

Kaze walked around the fish. Arthur went to the cash cage, while Daniel sat down with his bottle.

“You get this with the money?” Kaze asked.

“Billy took it,” Chris said.

“Chris wanted it,” Daniel added.

“I did not want it when he took it.”

Meatwad moved the camera toward Chris.

“When did the love start?”

Chris pointed at him without quite managing to stop smiling.

“Don't make it weird.”

Charles took the camera from Meatwad.

“Ask him about the construction. People want to understand the construction.”

Meatwad turned back to Chris. “How's it gonna work?”

Chris pulled a scrap of packing paper closer and started drawing a rough shape.

“Well, you don't just shove water into the ass end and hope for the best. You need somewhere for it to go, somewhere to come back from. We got the fish. We don't got the rest.”

Kaze leaned on the bench.

“Point it over the acid. Call it done.”

Chris stopped drawing.

“That's not a fountain. That's a fish taking a piss.”

“I'd watch it.”

“So would I,” Meatwad said.

Charles lowered the camera. “We've got an audience already.”

Chris looked from the drawing to the carp. Its broken mounting feet rested unevenly on the floor.

“First thing it needs is a stand. Look at that. Sits like a dog trying to shit on a hill.”

He pulled a short piece of salvaged packing timber from beneath the bench and checked it against the fish's damaged base. Then he found a second piece of the same thickness.

Kaze took one. “How long you need?”

Chris marked it against the base and passed him a hand saw.

“There. Hold it down.”

Kaze drew the saw hard toward himself. The teeth caught, and the timber jumped against the bench.

“Easy,” Chris said. “Let it bite first.”

“It bit.”

“You yanked it. You're trying to win an argument with a tree that's already dead.”

Chris guided the first short strokes, then stepped back. Kaze tried again. A thin line opened along the mark.

“There you go.”

“I could break this in half.”

“I know you can break it in half. I need both halves to agree where.”

Meatwad leaned closer. Charles lifted the camera to follow the saw.

Kaze watched Chris take up the second piece, turn it, and examine the grain.

“You're doing yours different.”

“Different piece of wood.”

“They were the same fucking crate.”

“You and Daniel live in the same house.”

Daniel raised his bottle from the couch. “I'm load-bearing.”

Kaze stopped sawing long enough to laugh. Then he finished the cut, and Chris fitted the two pieces beneath the carp's feet. With a few ordinary fasteners from the bench, the fish stood level on its new wooden runners.

Kaze tested it with a careful push.

“That's it?”

“That's it.”

He looked at the sawdust on his hand, then at Chris.

“You made that sound like we were building a fucking church.”

Chris brushed the dust from the fish.

“You did good.”

Kaze gave him a look, but he left the finished stand where Chris had put it.

Beside the workbench, Meatwad asked Charles for the camera back. He wanted a close shot of the carp's face. Charles handed it over, then leaned into the picture himself.

“This was a collaborative effort.”

Chris pointed the saw at him.

“You collaborated from over there. Stay over there.”

Arthur interrupted from the cage.

“You boys want to admire something useful?”

Kaze came over. The silver trays sat inside the opened cage, coins heaped where the gang had counted them.

His grin returned.

“Now that looks like a good fucking afternoon.”

Daniel told him how it had happened: the stolen cases returned, Dessa's bargain, the counting cage dropping down the shaft. Chris supplied the part about the brake housing, and Arthur corrected Daniel's claim that he'd been standing perfectly calmly throughout.

“You were upside down when I looked.”

“Calm upside down is still calm.”

They told him about the man Daniel had killed and the people they'd left alive. Kaze listened, then asked the question that mattered next.

“Heisenberg know it was you?”

Arthur shifted a tray back into place.

“His people saw enough.”

“So what's the plan?”

“Hadn't picked one past finding you.”

Kaze looked around the room. Chris had picked up his pencil again. Daniel had his bare simian feet up. Charles kept stepping sideways into Meatwad's shot, trying to get himself between the camera and the money.

For a moment Kaze simply laughed.

“I missed this stupid shit.”

“We had a whole meeting about replacing you,” Daniel said.

“With who?”

Daniel pointed at the carp.

“Strong silent type.”

“He's already got Chris.”

“We're exploring it,” Chris said.

They let the laughter run out naturally. Then Arthur rested both hands on the edge of the cage.

“We ain't staying here to fight the harbor every morning. And I'd rather go after something worth taking than keep circling men we've already robbed.”

Kaze nodded toward the exterior monitor, where the ruined gate stood down the slope.

“Had some fun here already.”

“I noticed.”

Daniel sat forward.

“We could head inland together. See what Noxus has besides busted gates and people trying to buy crossbows.”

“Big place,” Arthur said.

“Good. More than one thing in it.”

Kaze told them about Lask, the sale and the man who'd wanted Naganeka for a siege. He didn't pretend to know where that man's army was now. He told them about the quarry too, and the stranger with the axe who had made him work for every inch of ground.

Chris watched his face change as he described the fight.

“You had a vacation.”

“He put me through a fucking wall.”

“You look rested.”

Arthur looked from Kaze to Daniel.

“Find a place to hear what's paying. Actual work, if it's interesting. Something else if it ain't.”

“And if Heisenberg's people come looking?” Daniel asked.

Arthur closed the cage door as far as its damaged edge allowed.

“They know how to make an appointment.”

Kaze's smile thinned, interested rather than reassured.

“They can come.”

Billy's voice reached them from outside.

“Then I suggest we take the road in daylight. Our new acquaintance has declined to become luggage.”

Kaze looked toward the monitor. “We'll talk to her.”

“I believe that was her preference.”

Back in Zaun, earlier that same day, Serrat had been explaining the missing money to Heisenberg.

The collection-house manager sat stiffly in a chair, one arm pressed across his stomach. Beside him stood one of the clerks Billy had swallowed and released. Heisenberg had asked for an eyewitness who hadn't been behind the desk.

He listened to both accounts before speaking.

“You received the six cases.”

“Yes.”

“Intact.”

“Yes.”

“And then they stole the collections.”

Serrat nodded.

The chemist leaned back. His spectacles caught the light from the office window. Outside, machinery kept turning. Purple Pain, his highly profitable drug, still had customers. One ruined collection house hadn't stopped his business.

It had, however, embarrassed him.

“Who authorized withholding the workers' pay?”

“I was covering the loss.”

“With money you had already collected?”

Serrat shifted in the chair and winced.

“It encourages them to protect the stock.”

Heisenberg looked at Serrat again.

“How much did they give her?”

“Three hundred silver. I watched her count it.”

“They paid her.”

“Yes.”

Heisenberg removed his glasses and folded them.

“You made it profitable to rob me.”

Serrat started to answer.

“No. I'm not asking for another explanation of what you intended. I understand what you intended.”

The clerk stared at a point above the desk.

“Did the woman order you to help the robbers?” Heisenberg asked him.

“No.”

“Did she tell you they were coming?”

“No.”

“Then don't add it because you think I want to hear it.”

He put his glasses back on and slid a sheet of paper toward Serrat.

“Every outstanding wage. By name and amount. You're doing nothing else until that list is correct.”

“And Dessa?”

“Continues working. You don't threaten her. You don't explain how close she came to punishment. You don't speak to her.”

Serrat took the pencil.

Heisenberg turned to the clerk again.

“Tell me exactly how the money left the room.”

The man swallowed.

“The catfish ate it.”

“The money.”

“The cage. Everything inside it.”

“And you.”

The clerk nodded.

For the first time, Heisenberg left a question unanswered for several seconds.

“They let you go.”

“Both of us.”

Heisenberg looked back at Serrat. “After paying her.”

“Yes.”

Heisenberg picked up a second sheet.

“Start again. From the moment they entered. If you remember words, give me the words.”

He hadn't learned where the Sins had gone. He hadn't found a weapon that could beat them. But the men they'd spared had reached him, and now he was listening without Serrat choosing which parts he heard.

Up on the Noxian coast, Billy let Kaze back outside before the light failed.

Samira had her sword secured and both recovered pistols stowed alongside the ordinary pistol and knife she'd acquired on the road. She adjusted the weight against her sound shoulder.

She stood beside Billy, watching the workers far below.

“Billy says you're going inland.”

“Looks that way.”

“I'm taking mine to the people who built them.”

“Want company?”

“For that? No.”

Kaze stepped closer, smiling.

“For something before that?”

She glanced at Billy.

The great catfish raised his brows.

“I have looked upon the sea before, madam.”

“Then give it another go,” she said.

Billy turned with magnificent dignity and studied the harbor.

Samira caught the front of Kaze's waistband with her sound hand and pulled him close enough to kiss. When she released him, she didn't step away immediately.

“Find me after my wrist stops aching. I'd like to enjoy an evening without remembering your friend.”

“I can help you forget him.”

“You'd better be very interesting.”

“You know I am.”

“Sometimes.”

She tapped his chin and stepped back.

He let her go. She took the southward path from the shelf, carrying her own weapons at last. At the bend she looked back once, then continued. Billy waited until she was beyond ordinary hearing before turning.

“A spirited acquaintance.”

“Don't fucking start.”

“I hadn't reached the interesting part.”

Kaze laughed and went to the ballista. He checked the stones at its wheels, then sat on the carriage's drawbar.

“We're heading inland tomorrow,” he told Naganeka. “You want to come?”

“To another buyer?”

“No.”

“Another siege?”

“Haven't picked one.”

She was silent for a while.

“I will travel with you. What I choose to do when we arrive remains mine.”

Kaze looked back at Billy.

“She fits right in.”

Inside, Charles had reclaimed his place in front of the camera. Meatwad held it steady while Chris returned to the workbench.

“The important thing,” Charles said, “is I brought everybody together.”

Arthur looked up from his seat.

“You didn't leave the couch.”

“That's the center. You understand? They came back to the center.”

Meatwad kept filming.

“I thought they came back to Billy.”

Charles leaned closer to the lens.

“Billy is around the center.”

Chris made a small choking sound over his drawing.

“Don't laugh,” Charles told him. “This is serious.”

“I know.”

Chris bent over the paper until he could speak again.

“That's what's fucking me up.”
