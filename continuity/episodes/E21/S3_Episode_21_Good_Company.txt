Season 3, Episode 21: Good Company

“If you're going to jump, wait until I finish this.”

Kaze looked up.

A pale woman with white hair streaked pink sat on the broken parapet above the coastal road, holding a length of dark sausage between two fingers. Her dark clothes were torn at the edges. A heavy red restraint enclosed her neck and wrists. Her bare feet swung over a drop that ended among rocks and white water.

She swallowed, then pointed the sausage at him.

“All right. Now.”

“I just landed.”

“Oh. I missed it.”

“You were eating.”

“I can do both. You should've said something.”

Kaze glanced at the fresh cracks around his shoes, then back at her. “You always review strangers like this?”

“Only when they do something interesting.”

He smiled. Gold above, silver below. Her attention caught on the different metals.

“Those are nice.”

“Come closer. You can get a better look.”

She leaned forward on the wall. “You can come up here. I found it first.”

The previous afternoon, he'd left the others in Zaun. Their robbery had put six cases of Heisenberg's drug inside Billy's protected interior home, along with a collection of people who could argue over almost anything. Kaze had taken his twenty-silver share and told the gang he was going back toward Noxus for a while. At his request, Billy let him out onto an empty stretch of service road. He left the others there together.

He retraced the coast they'd actually traveled: long launches from solid ground, landings on headlands, the sea kept beside him. He rested through one night because sitting above the surf suited him, then continued at dawn. No ship to wait on, no cargo to shepherd. By the second afternoon, the damaged crane at the familiar Noxian harbor had come into view.

He'd chosen the high road above it. The woman on the wall was the first person he'd stopped to speak with.

Noxus spread far inland from this port. An empire that admired strength, aye, but strength could mean a good sword arm, a clever bargain, or knowing whose sword arm to purchase. Beneath the banners, people still sold dinner and worried about roofs. Kaze had returned to a place where he'd already helped make both things more difficult.

He stepped onto the parapet beside her. It was barely wide enough for his shoes. She turned her head to follow him, the red frame shifting with her shoulders.

“Briar,” she said.

“Kaze.”

“I know.” She nodded at the name tattooed across his stomach. “You brought a label.”

“Saves time.”

“Does the one on your forehead work?”

“Depends who's reading it.”

She studied the words: Do Not Resuscitate.

“I'd be curious.”

“About what?”

“Whether you stayed dead.”

Kaze laughed, a brief, surprised sound.

“That's a fucked-up introduction.”

“You wrote it on your face.”

Fair point. I've known men who required considerably more encouragement to discuss themselves.

Briar offered him the remaining sausage. He tore off a piece and tasted it.

“Where'd you get this?”

“Man with a cart. Down there.”

“Good?”

“Very. He's angry, though.”

Kaze looked down toward the carts, then at the food.

“You pay him?”

“No.”

“That'll do it.”

She looked back toward the harbor. “I wanted to see where the ships went. They keep getting smaller, and then you can't tell.”

“Usually somewhere else.”

“Helpful. Are all your answers like that?”

“Nah. Sometimes I'm trying to impress somebody.”

“Have you started?”

He shook his head, smiling now. “You talk a lot of shit for somebody sitting in a dinner plate.”

Briar glanced down at the enormous restraint.

“It's a pillory.”

“I know what it is.”

“Then you were wrong on purpose.”

“You catching on?”

She grinned, showing sharp teeth. “I think so.”

The thing around her wasn't ordinary prison iron. Blood magic had made Briar, an adult woman built as a weapon by the Black Rose, a secretive Noxian cabal. The stone in that restraint quieted an appetite that could otherwise swallow her attention whole. Her makers had wanted obedience. She'd escaped captivity and acquired rather different ambitions.

At present, one of those ambitions appeared to be deciding whether this stranger was worth the rest of her afternoon.

Kaze crouched beside her, looking toward the port. From here, the harbor gate stood above the lower road: two square towers joined by a stone arch, an iron portcullis raised beneath it. A heavy bolt thrower sat on the nearer tower. Below the gate, an outdoor eating house occupied a terrace overlooking the water.

“I'm getting something hot,” he said. “You coming?”

“Are you buying?”

“First time, sure.”

“What's the second time cost?”

He turned toward her. “Depends how the first time goes.”

Briar held his gaze for a moment, then dropped from the wall. She landed easily on the road and started downhill.

“You can explain while we eat.”

The eating house smelled of onions, charred meat and spilled beer. Its proprietor, a broad man with a scorched apron, watched Briar negotiate a bench without catching her pillory on the table. Kaze sat opposite her and put a silver coin down.

“Two plates. Whatever that is.”

The proprietor glanced toward the grill. “Lamb.”

“Good. That.”

“Bread?”

“Yes,” Briar said immediately.

“And bread.”

The man took the coin and went to work. Across the road, wagons climbed toward the gate between laborers carrying tools. Noxian soldiers checked the uphill traffic. The coastal wind kept lifting one corner of the proprietor's awning until he pinned it beneath a brick.

Briar watched Kaze's right shoulder. Black living plates overlapped there, threaded with dark fibers that shifted when he reached for his cup.

“Does that itch?”

“What?”

She nodded at it.

“No.”

“It looks like it might.”

“You wanna touch it or you just criticizing the furniture?”

“Can I?”

He held his arm across the table. She ran two fingers along a raised seam near his wrist, careful at first, then curious when the surface moved beneath them.

“That's you.”

“All of it.”

“I thought you were wearing something.”

“Apparently I need another label.”

She smiled and let his arm go.

“I wasn't complaining.”

“Didn't sound like it.”

The plates arrived. Briar breathed in over hers, then looked at the proprietor.

“That smells wonderful.”

The man's guarded expression softened a fraction. “Eat it while it's hot.”

She did, with immediate and unmistakable pleasure.

Kaze had just cut into his own meal when a familiar man came through the gate above them.

Hadran's clerk.

The last time they'd met, the clerk had paid to recover his employer's stolen bronze horse after hiring a red-masked swordsman to do it the difficult way. Arthur had taken the money. Billy had returned the sculpture. The clerk had seen considerably more of the Sins than he'd enjoyed.

Now he stopped so abruptly that a porter behind him swore.

His eyes went from Kaze's face to the lettering on his stomach.

Kaze lifted his cup.

The clerk turned and hurried back toward the soldiers.

Briar followed his movement. “Friend?”

“Nah.”

“He recognized you.”

“We had a business disagreement.”

“Did you pay for something?”

“Eventually, he did.”

She laughed around a mouthful, then swallowed before speaking.

“He's pointing.”

“I can see him.”

“You don't look surprised.”

Kaze watched the clerk talk to the officer beside the gate. “I mean, what's he gonna say? We robbed him. He knows that. I know that. The horse fucking knows that.”

“A horse?”

“Long story.”

“You can tell me.”

He looked back at her. Her interest was straightforward. Not a demand for a confession. She wanted to hear the ridiculous thing he'd started.

So he told her about the bronze horse, leaving the private workings of Billy's home out of it. Briar's questions concerned its size, why anybody wanted a horse that couldn't move, and whether making it valuable had been a mistake.

By the time the soldiers reached the terrace, Kaze was using his knife to explain the raised foreleg.

“Put that down,” the officer said.

Kaze looked at the little eating knife.

“This?”

“Stand up.”

Four soldiers had come downhill with him. Two carried shields and short swords. Another held a crossbow. The last remained beside the stairs, leaving a route back toward the gate. The clerk stopped behind that man.

The officer was older, with a white scar through one eyebrow and a captain's insignia fixed to his coat. He kept several paces between himself and the table.

“Captain Rask. Harbor watch. You're coming with us.”

“I'm eating.”

“Master Hadran has identified you as one of the men who robbed his sale.”

“Hadran isn't here.”

The clerk stepped far enough forward to be seen.

“I am.”

“There you go. He identified me. Get your story right.”

Rask's jaw tightened. “Where's the gunman? The one who killed Samira?”

Arthur had killed her at that sale. The clerk had witnessed the aftermath and knew whom he'd seen return. Kaze hadn't come back alone to find the rest of the gang's actions forgiven.

“Go ask him.”

“I'm asking you.”

Kaze set the knife beside his plate.

“Okay. And I'm not telling you.”

The proprietor backed away from his grill. People at the adjoining tables began moving toward the rear steps. Briar kept her hands beside her food, watching the soldiers instead of eating.

Rask addressed her. “You. Away from him.”

She looked at the space between her bench and the nearest shield.

“Are you taking my dinner?”

“I'm telling you to leave.”

“Then move.”

The shieldman shifted, opening a gap. Briar stood, picked up her plate and walked behind the table. She set it on the low terrace wall.

Kaze watched her go, then returned his attention to Rask.

“See, this is what I don't—hold on. You saw what happened down there?” He pointed toward the damaged breakwater. “Or somebody told you?”

“I saw the damage.”

“And you came with these guys.”

“I came to arrest you.”

Kaze rose. The bench scraped behind him.

His attention snapped uphill before the crossbowman moved. The warning in his instincts was slight, but it reached him before he'd consciously found its source. The crew on the tower had turned their great bolt thrower toward the terrace.

He looked at Rask again.

“Oh. You brought more guys.”

Rask's hand dropped.

The crossbow fired.

Kaze drew across his left hip. A sheath grew there from his own living tissue, and Black Marrow came out through it: a long, dark sword with bone-like ribs beneath its edge. The temporary sheath folded away. The blade turned into the bolt's path almost before his eyes followed it, sharing his alarm through the same living bond.

The bolt split. Its halves struck the table behind him.

Then Kaze was inside the nearest shield.

His left hand caught its rim. He drove it backward through the soldier holding it and into the terrace's stone column. Metal folded around the man's crushed chest. The column burst at the base, and the man died pinned beneath the broken shaft.

Rask pulled his sword and lunged for Kaze's neck.

Kaze caught the captain's sword arm with his free hand, turned beneath it and hurled him uphill.

Rask struck the gate arch back first. Stone cracked around the impact. His body fell onto the road below with his head hanging at an impossible angle. He was dead before his sword came down beside him.

The second shieldman backed toward Briar, then lunged for the red frame around her shoulders, trying to drag her between himself and Kaze.

“Don't grab that.”

He did.

Briar drove her forehead into his face. His grip loosened. She planted a foot against his breastplate and kicked him across the terrace. He hit the opposite wall hard enough to break its top course, then slumped among the fallen stones, breathing in wet, uneven pulls.

The crossbowman was already running. So was the soldier by the stairs. The clerk went with them, stumbling once before the uninjured soldier hauled him upright.

Above the gate, the heavy bolt thrower released.

Kaze's head turned. He had time to see the iron head coming down at his chest, the thick wooden shaft behind it, the two men at the machine preparing to duck.

He moved sideways. The bolt tore through the awning and buried itself in the terrace floor.

Kaze looked at the gate. Then he smiled.

“Stay off the road,” he told Briar.

He launched from the terrace.

The paving collapsed beneath his feet. He crossed the road in a rising blur and struck the tower just below its parapet, driving one foot into the stone. His next push tore that corner outward and carried him over the top.

One crewman abandoned the loading lever. The other reached for a hatchet.

Kaze hit the first with his shoulder. The man went through the far battlement and fell dead onto the rocks beyond. The second swung. Black Marrow cut through the raised arm and continued across his throat. He collapsed beside the machine.

With the crew dead, Kaze looked down through the machine's mounting at the tower beneath his feet. He wanted to see it come down.

With his free hand, he seized the bolt thrower's heavy frame, tore it from its mounting and drove it into the roof. Wood splintered against stone. He dropped through the hole he'd opened and drove his heel into the floor below.

The tower shook from foundation to parapet.

Across the road, Briar braced beside the low wall as windows burst outward. The iron portcullis twisted in its guides. A crack raced through the arch from Kaze's earlier impact, widened, and dropped a slab the size of a wagon into the road.

Kaze came out through the tower's outer wall in a spray of masonry. He landed on the slope above the eating house while the broken corner folded behind him. Upper stonework followed, battering the arch apart. The gate disappeared inside dust and falling iron.

The crash rolled out across the harbor. Men on the lower quays stopped working and looked uphill.

When the dust thinned, there was a mound where the road had passed beneath the arch. The second tower still stood, exposed rooms gaping along one side. A cart lay crushed at the edge of the collapse. Its driver crawled away dragging one leg, shouting for help.

Kaze looked back at the ruin with visible satisfaction.

Briar looked at him, then toward the terrace.

“My plate's broken.”

He followed her gaze. A chunk of falling stone had smashed it against the wall.

“Fuck.”

“I liked that.”

For several seconds he appeared to consider whether there was anything useful to say about a meal he'd brought an entire gate down beside.

“I'll get you another one.”

“You should.”

The proprietor emerged from behind his grill, white with dust. He saw the fallen column, the blood, the ruined awning and the body beneath the shield. Then he looked at Kaze.

“Get out.”

Kaze stepped over a broken bench.

“We were leaving.”

“Now.”

Kaze stopped, his expression sharpening. Briar watched him without speaking.

The proprietor's hands shook. He stayed where he was.

Kaze looked around the terrace, then at the ruined plate in Briar's hands. A little of the edge left his face.

“All right.”

He turned and went up the slope. Briar put the broken plate down and followed.

Behind them, the man began shouting for someone to help the injured driver. His business was still wrecked. Letting Kaze leave hadn't restored a fucking thing.

They walked until the harbor was hidden below the ridge. Blood speckled Kaze's bare chest and dried along his waistband; stone dust clung to his locs. Black Marrow folded back into his right hand, leaving blood across his fingers. He wiped them on a strip torn from a discarded sack caught in the roadside thorn.

Briar watched him do it.

“Were you going to kill the man who cooked?”

“For a second.”

“Because he wanted you gone?”

Kaze rolled the dirty cloth between his hands.

“Because of how he said it. Which is—yeah, I know. After all that.” He looked back toward the harbor. “I didn't like being told.”

“I could tell.”

He dropped the cloth beside the sack.

“You gonna tell me I should've behaved?”

“I'm going to tell you I still want dinner.”

He laughed, then stopped when she continued.

“And I didn't like that soldier deciding where I belonged. Away from you. Over there. Somewhere convenient.”

“You could've left.”

“I did leave the table. He came after me.”

“I saw.”

“Then remember that part.”

Kaze nodded. “I will.”

She walked beside him for a while, bare feet finding the smoother places between the stones. He slowed enough that they kept the same pace.

“You weren't scared,” he said.

“I've met worse than soldiers.”

“Me?”

“I only just met you.”

That earned a quiet laugh. She hadn't offered him absolution or decided she understood him. Sensible woman.

An hour inland, they found a roadside inn where the evening meal had already begun. A mule stood beneath the stable roof. Its owner, a carter with one eye, was arguing cheerfully with the innkeeper about the size of his cup.

No one there had watched the harbor gate fall.

Kaze paid four silver for food, a room, and water to wash off the road. Briar heard him ask for one room and caught his eye.

“Confident.”

“Hopeful.”

“That's different.”

“You want another?”

She considered him long enough to make him wait.

“No.”

They cleaned up separately before returning to a table beside the hearth. The food was less impressive than the lamb, but the bowls survived long enough to be emptied. Briar tried the sharp cheese, made a face, then tried it again.

Kaze watched. “You like it or not?”

“I'm deciding.”

“You look personally offended.”

“It keeps changing.”

He tasted a piece. His eyebrows rose.

“Goddamn.”

“See?”

He pushed it away. Briar pulled it back.

“I'm not finished deciding.”

After a while she asked about the sword. He showed her his empty palm, then let a dark seam move beneath the skin.

“It's part of me. Most of the time it stays in.”

“Does it talk?”

“Fuck no. I'd never get a minute.”

“It moved before you looked.”

“I felt the shot coming. It felt it with me.”

She studied his hand, then held out her own as far as the pillory allowed. He rested his fingertips against hers.

“This helps me think,” she said, shifting the red frame. “People see it and think they should take it off.”

“You want it off?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes I want it exactly where it is.”

“Then there you go.”

Her fingers moved against his.

“You don't want to fix it?”

“You didn't say it was broken.”

She looked at him for a moment longer than the question required.

Outside, the carter finally won a larger cup. His triumphant announcement reached them through the doorway, followed by the innkeeper telling him he'd be paying for what went into it.

Briar smiled and looked down at their hands.

“I like choosing things,” she said. “Even when I choose something awful.”

Kaze glanced at the cheese.

“You've committed.”

“I wasn't only talking about that.”

He leaned back, smiling slowly. “That's rude as fuck.”

“You asked if I was criticizing the furniture.”

“And?”

“I've had more time to look.”

He laughed properly then. She seemed pleased with herself, and made no attempt to hide it.

Briar watched the fire for a moment.

“When they made me, they had people they wanted dead,” she said. “They were very clear about that. Afterward, I kept finding other things I wanted. Nobody liked those as much.”

“Like what?”

“Staying somewhere after the work was finished. Talking to someone I wasn't sent to kill.” She looked back at him. “Being asked what I thought before somebody decided.”

Kaze rubbed his thumb over her fingers.

“You got a lot of opinions.”

“I'm enjoying having them.”

The conversation wandered. She wanted to know where he'd go tomorrow. He wanted to know what she'd been looking for beside the sea. Neither had a grand answer. He liked the prospect of a road without the gang deciding the next profitable thing. She wanted to keep going somewhere because she liked it, instead of because someone had sent her.

“You could come with me,” he said.

“Are you asking about tomorrow or upstairs?”

“Both.”

“At least that answer was useful.”

He leaned closer across the table.

“I been trying to make a good impression.”

“You broke a gate.”

“Big impression.”

“Terrible dinner.”

“I'm recovering.”

Kaze's smile settled into something more direct.

“Come here. I want to kiss you.”

Briar stood and came around the table. She stopped close enough that he had to look up at her.

“Then get up.”

He did, and she lifted her face to meet him.

Their first kiss was brief. Briar stayed close afterward, smiling against his mouth before she drew back.

“Those do feel different.”

“You been thinking about my teeth this whole time?”

“Not the whole time.”

She took his hand and led him toward the stairs. He followed willingly, suddenly far less interested in the road beyond the door.

They kept the rest of the night to themselves.

Much later, the room had gone quiet around them. The lamp was out. Moonlight lay across the floorboards, and somewhere beneath the window the mule was making a prolonged complaint about its circumstances.

Briar lay beside Kaze, awake and comfortably close.

“You talk less after sex,” she observed.

“Give me a minute.”

She laughed softly.

He turned his head toward her. “You all right?”

“Yes.” A pause. “Still coming tomorrow?”

“I asked you.”

“I'm asking again.”

He reached for her hand beneath the cover.

“Yeah. I want you there.”

She held it. Neither added anything for a while.

By morning, the innkeeper had heard there was trouble at the port from a rider arriving along the lower road. He hadn't yet learned enough to connect it to the two guests leaving his upstairs room. Kaze heard the rider describing a collapsed gate while Briar finished the last of the cheese with an expression of grim personal determination.

“You don't have to win,” Kaze told her.

She swallowed.

“I already paid for it.”

“I paid for it.”

“Then I'm helping.”

They left by the inland road before the rider's account was finished. Behind them, the harbor had bodies to bury, a blocked approach and surviving witnesses. Elsewhere, Kaze's friends had their own stolen goods and people looking for them. Going away hadn't made any of it stop.

Briar walked beside him beneath the first warm sunlight. At the fork above the inn, she pointed to the road climbing between the hills.

“That one.”

“Why?”

“We haven't been there together.”

Kaze looked up the slope, then back at her.

“All right.”

She started first. He caught up, brushing his hand against hers as they passed the last fence.

For once, the lad didn't ask whether there was a faster way.
