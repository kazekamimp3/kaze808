Season 3, Episode 68 — Constructive Criticism

Daynah heard the hammer from the ropewalk.

Three strikes. A pause. Three more. Then a little voice said, with enormous satisfaction, “There!”

He looked past the Subaru's missing mirror toward an unfinished timber pavilion above the harbor lane. A roof frame stood on temporary props. Beneath it, a man in a yellow hard hat was admiring a nail that remained an inch outside the wood.

Daynah walked over.

He wasn't looking for work. That's what he told the man while taking the hammer from him.

“You're gonna leave somebody's skin on that.”

One measured blow seated the nail. Daynah handed the hammer back.

“Thank you! Bob. Bob the Builder.”

“Daynah.”

Bob smiled up at nearly nine feet of carpenter. Behind him stood exercise steps, unfinished rails and a broad platform facing the water. A painted board leaned against the wall: Richard's name above a row of smiling people stretching.

“Could you hold this end?” Bob asked.

Daynah looked at the beam, then at the little builder's hopeful face.

“For a minute.”

That's how they get you. Nobody has ever rebuilt a stranger's fucking house on purpose. You agree to hold one end, then your grandchildren are eating lunch in a wheelbarrow.

Daynah set Kahuhu, his massive forged gauntlets, on the stone footing. He lifted the beam while Bob checked its length with a tape.

“Lemme see that.”

Bob handed it over.

Daynah pushed the metal hook at its end. It didn't move. A bead of dried glue shone around it.

“You glued this?”

“It was loose.”

“It's supposed to move. Takes up the thickness of the hook.”

Bob's smile softened with patient concern.

“Not anymore.”

Daynah looked along the frame. One corner stood visibly farther out than the other.

“How much of this did you measure with it?”

Bob spread his arms.

Oh, wonderful. A fucking panorama.

Daynah lowered the beam onto its supports and walked to the crooked corner. Bob followed, explaining that the pavilion would be used for exercise, which meant the people inside would be moving anyway.

“The building ain't meant to go with 'em.”

Daynah braced the corner upright and pointed at the diagonal support.

“Leave that until this is fixed.”

“But we're nearly finished.”

“That's why I'm mentioning it before somebody's nearly dead.”

Bob crouched and pulled the support free.

The corner sank.

Daynah drove his shoulder beneath the roof beam. A row of joints cracked above him. Loose boards slid down the tilted frame and struck the platform in a clattering heap.

He spread his stance, caught the beam with both hands and stopped the roof halfway into the room.

Bob stared.

“You've moved it.”

Daynah turned his head very slowly.

“Put. The fucking prop. Back.”

Bob seized the beam instead.

He pulled.

Daynah's heels gouged the platform. The whole load shifted across his shoulders, opening the far wall at its corner.

That got his attention.

“Little bastard's strong.”

“You're holding it wrong,” Bob said.

Then he drove his shoulder into Daynah's stomach.

Daynah went backward through the unfinished wall. Three uprights broke in succession. The roof hit the platform behind him with a boom that rolled down to the harbor.

He landed among the broken framing, one hand planted in the dirt. A nail had drawn a long red line across his forearm.

Bob stepped through the opening, hammer in hand.

“Now look what you've done.”

Daynah got up.

He didn't shout. He picked a splinter out of his arm and looked at it.

“I was gonna be nice about your fucking tape.”

Bob swung at his knee.

Daynah moved his leg, caught the hammer wrist and drove a short right hand into Bob's middle. The breath came out of him in a startled squeak. Daynah turned with the trapped arm and slammed him face-first into the remaining corner post.

The hard hat fell off. Blood opened along Bob's hairline.

“Stay down.”

Bob grabbed the post with his free hand.

He tore it out of the structure.

Daynah released the wrist and got his forearms up as the timber came across. It struck with a deep wooden thud, lifted him off his feet and drove him through the stacked exercise steps. Nails projecting from its side raked his hip. The steps burst beneath his back.

Bob advanced with the post held like a battering ram.

Daynah rolled clear of its next thrust. The end buried itself in the earth beside his head. He caught the shaft, stepped outside Bob's line and wrenched it sideways. Bob came with it, boots leaving the ground.

Daynah drove the butt back into him.

Bob hit his workbench hard enough to split it down the middle. Tools jumped into the air. A jar of screws burst against the wall, scattering bright metal through the dust.

Daynah spat grit.

“At least that joint came apart when it was supposed to.”

A groan ran through the roof frame behind him. Its unsupported outer edge was sliding toward the lane.

He dropped the post, snatched up Kahuhu and fastened the gauntlets as he crossed the wreckage. The blunt forged hands closed around the lowest beam. He lifted, turned the weight away from the road and lowered it across a stack of heavy timber. The stack settled. Held.

Behind him came hammering.

Fast hammering.

Not the dainty little tap-tap of a man hanging a picture. This sounded like a woodpecker had found out what his wife did in the birdhouse.

Daynah looked around.

Bob had dragged the broad wall panels together. He was kneeling beside a huge wooden box, driving nails through its corners with furious, bloody concentration. A lid lay next to it. The box was long enough for Daynah and wide enough for his shoulders.

Daynah looked at the box. Then at Bob.

“You building me a coffin?”

Bob knocked the last nail home.

“A proper fit.”

Daynah strode across and stamped down on the exposed bottom edge. Its thin supporting strip snapped. The floor sagged away from the wall.

“That ain't carrying me to the fucking hole.”

Bob glanced at the failure. Then he pulled a thick crosspiece from the wreckage and shoved it under the base.

Daynah watched him drive the first nail.

“Other end. You'll split it there.”

Bob moved the nail.

“Yes. There.”

I give him thirty seconds before he's asking Bob who taught him to bury people. Forty before he supplies a better shovel.

Bob added another brace. His blood dripped onto the wood.

“Better?”

“Depends what you call better.”

“Try it.”

Daynah looked at him. Bob looked back.

Then Daynah stepped into the coffin.

“Gladly.”

He planted both feet on the reinforced bottom and put his weight into it. The wood creaked but held. He crouched to inspect the connection beneath the rim.

Bob snatched up the lid and slammed it onto his head.

The huge box tipped. Daynah's shoulder hit its inside wall, and the whole thing crashed onto its side. Bob sprang onto it and drove two nails through the lid before the dust had settled.

He raised the hammer for a third.

“Can we fix it?”

From inside came Daynah's voice.

“You could've asked that before you put me in the fuckin' thing.”

The lid bulged.

Bob jumped clear as Daynah's gauntlet punched through it. Wood blew across the platform. Daynah caught the broken edge, tore the lid and upper side away together, and stood out of the box with splinters sliding off his shoulders.

The reinforced base remained intact beneath him.

Bob lunged with the hammer.

Daynah slipped inside the swing. One gauntlet closed over Bob's forearm. The other struck him beneath the ribs, lifting him onto his toes. Daynah turned the captured arm across the builder's body and drove him down through what remained of the bench.

The impact crushed the center of it into the dirt.

Bob lay among broken boards, gasping. His hammer hand hung at an angle it hadn't had before. Daynah kicked the hammer beyond his reach and released him.

Nothing else needed hitting.

For a while there was only Bob's breathing and the soft ticking of debris falling from the roof.

Daynah looked down at the wrecked coffin. His forearm had stopped bleeding freely; the torn skin was still red and open. He pulled a nail from the cloth at his hip.

Bob moved his good hand toward the box.

“Bottom held.”

Daynah shut his eyes.

“Yeah.”

“You helped.”

Daynah opened them again.

“Don't you fucking put my name on it.”

By dusk, a trading vessel from northern Shurima was unloading at the export depot. Brian and Jar Jar came ashore with its passengers after the northbound road and the actual crossing. Jar Jar had worked during the passage; Brian had carried what he could and repeatedly explained that a working voyage was technically a tour.

Nobody had booked him.

They found Baldur at a drinking stand above the landings, turning a steaming cup against his bare chest.

The lean, tattooed god watched the liquid spill over his skin. He didn't flinch. He set the cup down hard enough to crack its handle.

Brian stopped.

“I know you.”

Baldur looked down.

“That's unfortunate.”

“No, like, I know your whole thing. The not feeling anything. I'm an artist.”

“Then paint me somebody else to talk to.”

Brian opened his journal instead.

“Music. People say they feel my shit.”

Baldur's attention sharpened for one miserable second.

“Physically?”

“Sometimes they leave.”

Baldur stared at him. Brian hurried on.

“Because it moves them. That's the expression. I do layered work. Head, shoulders, different phases of my life—”

He showed Baldur the signed page. Starkiller's endorsement sat beneath the earlier performance.

Baldur glanced at it.

“Words on paper.”

“From somebody very difficult to impress.”

“And how long did you follow him?”

Brian's fingers tightened around the book.

Jar Jar looked over from the nearby baggage scales. He had been asking a returning porter about vessels from beyond this world. The answer had been another headshake, followed by a useful offer of a room above the landing office.

“Brian! Mesa checking where wesa sleep.”

“I'm in a meeting.”

“Yousa standing by a cup.”

“That's where a lot of meetings start.”

Jar Jar gave Baldur an apologetic nod and followed the porter up the stairs to see the room.

Brian turned back to his prospect.

“I could perform something short.”

“No.”

“You haven't heard the length.”

Baldur leaned down until his face was close to Brian's.

“I listened to a man promise me a cure. He couldn't do it. Now he's dead. Are you promising me a cure?”

Brian closed the journal.

“I'm primarily entertainment.”

“You should try it.”

Baldur straightened as a sailor approached from the steps. The sailor pointed toward the outer end of the harbor.

“Lower landing. Below the lime shed. First bell tomorrow. You want Zaun, that's the boat.”

“I'll be there,” Baldur said.

He left without the cup.

Brian remained beside it, thumb between the journal's closed pages.

“Strong reaction,” he muttered.

Across the depot, Kratos stood at the broken foundation of Shang Tsung's shelter. Mimir hung at his hip, his horned head turned toward the crewman describing the killing.

The man pointed at the stained stone, then toward the road Baldur had taken. Kratos asked about the tattoos, the failed treatment, the manner of the attack. He listened to the answers without touching his axe.

“He is still searching,” Mimir said when they walked away.

“And killing those who fail him.”

“Aye. But he hasn't lived what we remember. We should discover how much he knows before giving him another enemy.”

Kratos looked toward the lights coming on above the landings.

“I will speak to him before he finds one.”

They reached the drinking stand as Brian was explaining to Jar Jar that refusing a preview was common among serious patrons.

“Brian,” Kratos said.

Brian turned. His face opened.

“Kratos! Holy shit. Mimir!”

Mimir blinked down at him.

“Well. You've rearranged yourself.”

Brian spread his arms.

“Independent now. Full operation.”

“I see the legs.”

“There's a lot more to it than legs.”

Kratos regarded the tiny man he remembered, then the wrapped hilt at his side.

“There usually is.”

Brian's smile became more careful.

He introduced Jar Jar with such extravagant praise that the Gungan gently moved Brian's pointing hand away from his chest.

“Mesa can say hello.”

“I'm establishing your credentials.”

“Hello.” Jar Jar inclined his head to Kratos, then Mimir. “Mesa Jar Jar Binks.”

“Mimir. Pleasure.”

Brian looked between the head and the belt.

“See, that's a respectful height. Daynah had me round the back. I had to wait for the man to turn before I could join a conversation.”

“I remember,” Mimir said. “Do you still shout when it doesn't concern you?”

“That's networking.”

“Him was here. Hot drink on him, didn't even move.”

“Where did he go?”

Jar Jar shook his head.

“Mesa upstairs when him left. Brian talked with him.”

Brian's thumb stopped stroking the journal's spine.

“You need his location?”

“His destination,” Kratos said.

“I have a destination. A time. Very actionable package.”

Mimir's eyes narrowed.

“There's a price coming, isn't there?”

Brian opened the journal to fresh lyrics.

“One song. Honest constructive feedback from both of you. And signatures.”

Kratos looked past him toward the harbor road. Multiple landings descended beneath warehouses; lanterns moved among the moored boats.

“Tomorrow morning.”

Mimir studied Brian's face.

“And you heard him agree to it?”

“Yes. The sailor came right here. Baldur said he'd be there. He wants another place with people who can fix his problem.”

Kratos held out his hand.

Brian hugged the book against his chest.

“It's not a threat. It's an exchange.”

“I was asking to see the length.”

Brian gave him the book.

Kratos turned the pages. One eyebrow moved almost imperceptibly.

“One song.”

“That's what I—”

“No additions.”

Mimir spoke before Brian could redefine the word.

“You give us the landing first. We hear you out. Honest criticism, mind. You don't get to order your own review.”

Brian glanced at Jar Jar, who was watching him expectantly.

“Lower landing. Below the lime shed. First bell. Boat to Zaun.”

Kratos handed back the book.

“We have time.”
