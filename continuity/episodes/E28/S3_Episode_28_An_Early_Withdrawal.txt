Season 3, Episode 28: An Early Withdrawal

“I think we ought to give the drugs back,” Daniel said.

Arthur looked up from the six wooden cases beside Chris's workbench.

“You hit your head?”

“Frequently. Keep up.”

The simian martial artist had a bottle in one hand and a very deliberate expression. Daniel was drunk, as usual. Arthur had learned that this rarely made his ideas less dangerous.

Chris turned in his chair, still holding his game controller. On the first monitor, his outlaw stood beside a horse in Red Dead Redemption Two. On the second, Zaun's freight road rolled past from the viewpoint of Billy's hat.

Outside, Billy stopped walking.

“I swallowed those in good faith.”

His voice reached them through the live feed. The enormous catfish man carried the others in his protected interior home, a place where living walls enclosed a workshop, a gaming lounge and considerably more stolen property than any respectable stomach required.

Four nights had passed since the Rivermark robbery. The six cases of Purple Pain remained intact. Kaze had gone back toward Noxus, and the people he'd left behind had discovered that stealing Heisenberg's merchandise was easier than persuading somebody to buy it.

Daniel laid a folded notice on the bench. He'd pulled it from a board outside the receiving works that morning.

Chris saved his game and put the controller down.

The notice identified the missing shipment by its case marks. Purple Pain was Heisenberg's drug: strength and euphoria sold with a dependence that kept customers returning. Buying the stolen lot meant losing access to his future supplies. A separate reward offered payment for information leading to its recovery.

“Look at that,” Chris said. “We stole six boxes and he made every other box in the city a fucking hostage.”

Arthur read the notice. “Man understands customers.”

“He understands frightened customers,” Daniel said. “There's a difference.”

“Ain't always much of one at the till.”

Daniel took a drink from his frosted bottle, then pointed at the cases.

“Dessa knows where his money goes.”

“Dessa knows you killed her guard,” Arthur said.

“Yes. That's why I'm bringing something she wants instead of asking whether she enjoyed meeting me.”

Chris got up and checked the lid he'd opened after the robbery. The packing beneath it hadn't shifted.

“You want her to help us rob her boss.”

“I want to find out what she wants.”

Arthur folded the notice along its existing crease. “And if she wants your head?”

Daniel touched his neck thoughtfully.

“Strong opening position. We'll negotiate.”

Billy leaned toward a shop window outside, examining his reflection. The hat-camera filled their second screen with his amber eye.

“My contribution to this enterprise ought to be recognized before we start returning things.”

“You ate six boxes,” Chris said through the banjo channel.

“Without breaking a vial.”

“That's the job.”

“And yet a man puts a slice of parsley on dinner and expects applause.”

“A slice of parsley?”

Billy smiled at the window.

“I knew that'd fetch you.”

Far away, on the road beyond Lask, Briar stopped at a fork. The pale, white-haired woman had traveled with Kaze since his return to Noxus. This morning, she'd been thinking about another direction.

Kaze lowered the ballista's drawbar. Its rear wheels settled into their own tracks. Samira, the mercenary still seeking the return of her stolen weapons, stood beside it with her injured wrist braced against her body.

The northern road ran inland between low hills. The other bent back toward the coast.

“I'm going that way,” Briar said, pointing north.

Kaze looked from the road to her. “Something up there?”

“I don't know. That's why.”

She shifted the red restraint around her neck and wrists, considering how to say the rest.

“I wanted to see where you went. I did. Now I want to pick a road without waiting for you to pick it.”

“Then pick it.”

“I just did.”

He grinned. “Yeah. You did.”

Briar glanced at Samira, gave her a small nod, then turned back to him.

“Try eating something that isn't a fight.”

“No promises.”

She went north at a quick, easy pace. Kaze watched until the road bent, then took up the drawbar again.

“Zaun?” Samira asked.

“Coast first. Then my people.”

“And my weapons.”

“And your weapons.”

From the ballista came Naganeka's low voice.

“Does this company always travel by argument?”

Samira looked at the imprisoned Darkin weapon.

“You're getting the quiet version.”

They started toward the coast, the carriage wheels cutting fresh tracks through the roadside grass.

Back in Zaun, Dessa answered Daniel's knock with a short iron bar in her hand.

He stood alone outside Rivermark's side office. Behind her, the broken warehouse doorway had been braced with fresh boards. Someone had scrubbed the platform. A dark stain remained between two steps.

“Before you swing,” he said, “I've got your six cases.”

“You had them when you left.”

“Kept them together. Very professional.”

She struck at his face.

Daniel caught the bar in one ice hand. He didn't pull her forward or take it away. He held it while she tried once to wrench it free.

“Renn,” she said.

Daniel's smile faded.

“The man you put through my door. His name was Renn.”

“All right.”

“It isn't all right.”

“No. It isn't.”

She released the bar. He laid it against the wall, away from the doorway.

“What do you want?” she asked.

“Money. I was hoping you had a more complicated answer.”

Dessa looked past him. Billy waited across the road with Arthur, making no attempt to resemble an innocent pedestrian.

“He charged the loss against the works,” she said. “Stock. Cash. Damage. We're still producing what we can, and the collectors still come. Pay doesn't.”

Daniel glanced through the office. Several workers watched from beyond the open inner door. One turned away when Dessa noticed him.

“How much are you short?”

“Three hundred silver gets my people paid through yesterday. It doesn't bring Renn back.”

“I wasn't offering that.”

She stared at him for a while.

“Why should I believe you'll pay?”

“Because we want to rob him again. People remembering we paid what we said is useful.”

“So is people being afraid of you.”

“You're afraid of him. How much good's that doing him right now?”

Her eyes narrowed. Daniel let the question sit. Aye, there was the sober thought in the drunken bastard's head. Heisenberg had made Dessa responsible for a loss she couldn't prevent, then expected responsibility to feel enough like loyalty.

“There's a collection house above the old pumping cut. Heisenberg's money goes up there from half these streets. His clerk comes down here and tells us what we're allowed to keep.”

“You take us in with the cases.”

“You give them to me first.”

“That's trust.”

“That's the price of the conversation you're having.”

Daniel looked across at Arthur. The cowboy had heard enough to meet his eye and give one small nod.

Billy brought the six cases out onto the office's dry floor at their request. Dessa opened the one Chris had inspected, checked the contents and closed it again. All six were hers to recover now. Nothing remained hidden beneath the gang's promise.

“Three hundred,” she said. “Before you leave.”

“If we get the money.”

“If you don't, you can explain it to them.” She nodded toward the workers. “I'm done explaining you.”

She drew him closer by the front of his blue wrap.

“And when they ask, you made me take you there.”

“Did we?”

“That's what I'm telling them.”

Daniel glanced at the iron bar against the wall.

“Your performance needs work. I felt quite unwelcome.”

She let go without smiling.

Arthur stepped closer.

“You ain't going to like how we do it.”

“I don't like you now.”

“Good. Saves us disappointing each other.”

An hour later, after Billy yielded the outside to him, Chris stood inside Heisenberg's collection house, looking up at a bronze carp the length of a dining table.

The thing hung over the counting cage. Its mouth poured water into a tiled basin, and somebody had polished every scale while the factory streets below lay under soot.

“Look at this smug motherfucker,” Chris said.

Arthur followed his gaze.

“You reckon it's expensive?”

“It has indoor rain.”

Billy's voice emerged from the banjo outlet Chris carried.

“Fine craftsmanship.”

“Don't start.”

The collection house stood across a narrow industrial cut, with its counting room on the upper landing and a freight platform hanging beneath it. The street entrance lay behind the Sins. Across the open shaft, a maintenance gallery reached the lift's brake machinery. Far below, dark water hurried between the pumping works.

Dessa's workers had brought the six cases on the works' handcart. She'd dismissed them at the entrance after the guards checked the returned shipment. Daniel stayed beside the stock. Arthur watched the counting cage. Chris watched the carp, which was becoming a problem for reasons the security staff hadn't considered.

Inside the cage, two clerks poured silver from collection bags into shallow trays. Money made a soft, continuous music against the iron mesh.

Serrat, the house manager, came down from his desk holding Dessa's receipt. He was narrow, neatly dressed and wearing a small key on a chain beneath his collar.

“The goods are recovered,” he said. “The account will be adjusted after inspection.”

“My people get paid today,” Dessa replied.

“That isn't your decision.”

Daniel leaned on his spear. “Who makes it?”

Serrat looked at him properly for the first time. His eyes went from the bare gripping feet to the black tail, then up to the pale eyes.

“You.”

“Sometimes. Not usually about wages.”

Serrat stepped back. Two guards near the street door brought their rifles up.

“You're the one from Rivermark. Dessa's report mentioned the tail.”

Daniel sighed. “I was hoping she'd described me taller.”

Arthur's right sleeve shifted. A heavy revolver settled into his cybernetic hand, its cylinder checked with a brief turn of his thumb.

“Let's keep those pointed at the floor,” he told the guards.

They hesitated. Behind the counting mesh, a clerk dropped a scoop into a tray.

Serrat's hand moved to the lever beside his desk.

Daniel advanced. Serrat pulled the lever before he reached the desk.

The cage shutters slammed down. Beneath the counting room, great chains rattled over their wheels. The entire cage began descending on the freight platform, taking the clerks and the collections toward the lower tunnel.

“Ah,” Daniel said. “Your money's leaving.”

Chris was already moving toward the maintenance gallery.

One guard swung his rifle after him. Arthur caught the barrel with his left hand, drove the butt back into the man's jaw and put him flat against the door. His right kept the revolver trained on the second guard, who lowered his weapon before Arthur had to ask again.

Daniel tucked his bottle into his sash and vaulted the railing.

He landed on the moving cage roof with both feet and one hand. Ice cracked beneath his palm where he'd arrested the impact. Through a narrow inspection gap he could see the clerks crouching beside the money trays. His pale eyes also caught movement below them: another man climbing the lift's service frame.

“Stay down,” he called. “This is about to become very inconvenient architecture.”

A steel fist punched through the roof. Daniel had already moved his head aside.

The armored man dragged himself through the opening. He wore a powered loading harness over his work clothes, with thick pistons at the elbows and iron pads covering his fists. Purple veins stood along his neck. An empty vial hung in a little rack beside his shoulder.

“Get him off it!” Serrat shouted down. “There's another vial in it for you!”

The man planted his feet and hit Daniel with the side of one enormous forearm.

Daniel got his ice forearms between the blow and his ribs. The impact still hurled him across the shaft.

He struck the opposite brick wall hard enough to cave it inward, vanished into dust and came out through the falling bricks feet first.

The armored man jumped after him.

Daniel caught a wall bracket with his tail and swung beneath it. The man's iron fists smashed through the place where his chest had been. Brick burst into the pumping cut below.

“You work here,” Daniel said, hanging upside down. “They gonna charge you for that?”

The man tore a fist loose.

“Shut up.”

“That's what they told her!”

He let go before the second swing reached him. A small cloud caught him below the gallery. He rolled across it, came upright and sprang back toward the descending cage.

Above him, Chris reached the brake housing. He pulled its inspection cover open and followed the linkage to the descending platform's locking mechanism.

“Billy, you can take the cage. I'm getting this gate out of your way.”

Omega Teemo's voice came from his equipment, close and cold. The yordle soul bound into Chris's rifle and mask had noticed a moving muzzle.

“Rifle across the shaft.”

Chris ducked behind the housing. A shot tore through its outer cover. He had left his mask retracted; now its plates slid into place around his face, the amber headlamp narrowing into the brow.

Across the gap, Serrat had pulled a third rifle from behind his desk and fired.

Arthur moved between the desk and the stairs. “You done?”

Serrat backed away, working the rifle's action.

Arthur kept coming. When the barrel rose, he knocked it aside and struck Serrat once in the stomach with his left fist. The manager folded over the cybernetic hand, slid to his knees and lost the rifle.

“Good,” Arthur said. “Now you are.”

Below, the harnessed man landed on the cage so hard the roof buckled. Daniel came down beside him and thrust his spear toward an exposed knee.

The man twisted. The point skidded off a steel brace. His hand closed around the shaft.

Daniel let it go.

He ducked beneath the man's elbow, planted his ice palms on the roof and kicked both bare feet up beneath the fellow's chin. The harness amplified his arms, not the hinge of his jaw. His head snapped back, and his grip opened.

Daniel's right foot caught the spear as it fell. He turned on one planted hand and swept the shaft into the man's ankle.

The armored man staggered, caught the roof rail and ripped it free.

“All right,” Daniel muttered. “That one's on you.”

The rail came down like a club.

Daniel rolled beneath it. When he came up, he pulled two hairs from his head and blew them from his palm.

Two rough copies of him sprang onto the roof. Each carried a brief imitation of his spear. They rushed the armored man's knees from opposite sides.

He kicked one apart in a burst of loose hair, then caught the other's spear and crushed it. That was enough. The real Daniel was already behind him.

An ice hand locked around the back of the harness. Daniel drove his heel into the man's knee, forced him down and thrust his spear through the exposed gap beneath his shoulder plate. The point emerged through his chest.

The man convulsed, reaching for Daniel. His powered fist closed on empty air.

Daniel planted one foot against his back and hauled the spear free. Then he kicked the body off the cage, through the remaining rail and into the shaft wall. Bricks burst from the impact. The man struck the lower stone ledge and lay dead, one broken piston tapping beside him.

The surviving hair double unraveled into drifting strands. Daniel turned his spear in his hand. Blood ran from a cut over his brow where he'd hit the wall.

He wiped it with the back of his wrist.

“Worst fucking employee discount I've ever seen.”

Chris forced the gate latch back with the inspection cover's bent edge, then wedged it there. The safety gate stopped sliding into the space above the cage.

“That's your opening,” he said.

Billy took the outside in one quick inversion. He stepped off the gallery immediately, landing on the cage beside Daniel. Its roof boomed under his weight.

“Much obliged.”

“Could've said that before you landed on my fucking feet,” Daniel said.

“You have such nimble feet. I trusted you.”

Serrat, on his knees beside Arthur, watched his silver disappear.

“The escape gate,” he gasped. “It's still open.”

Arthur looked at the gate, then the rising counterweight. If the weight reached the top, the cage would be deep in the lower tunnel. His crimson eye opened wider beneath the hat brim.

He raised the revolver toward the counterweight's upper guide, clear of his friends below. Held pressure gathered inside the gun. Dark tendons tightened along its frame; metal complained in a low, ugly whine.

Then he fired.

The charged round punched through the guide and the masonry behind it. The rising weight tore sideways, struck the broken rail and jammed with a crash that shook dust from every beam.

The cage lurched. Its roof tilted beneath Daniel.

He caught the roof's remaining rail. Billy bent his knees and rode the jolt. Inside, the trays slid and silver poured across the floor.

Arthur lowered the smoking revolver. One shot spent; the fractured casing around its forward frame was already knitting back together.

“Take it,” Arthur called.

Daniel sprang back onto his cloud and rose to the gallery with his spear and bottle. His brow had already stopped bleeding. He looked at Billy, then at the bronze carp still pouring water overhead.

“You're thinking about the fish.”

“I can think about two things.”

Billy dropped onto the platform's rim, hooked his hands beneath the counting cage and tore its mounting feet loose. He tipped it toward his opening mouth.

The jaw spread around the iron structure while the hat stayed absurdly ordinary above it. Then the whole counting cage disappeared into him, silver, trays and all. The living clerks went separately into containment; the room and its contents went into protected storage.

The unloaded freight platform bounced against its chains. Billy climbed its bracing to the nearest wall support, caught the gallery's stone lip and hauled himself back up. Daniel moved aside as his heavy tail swept over the railing.

For a moment all anybody could hear was the water falling from that ornamental fish.

Serrat stared at the empty platform.

Billy licked a drop from one barbel.

“Your security has a delightful crunch.”

They were back on the upper landing when Dessa emerged from the stairwell. She had taken cover there when the rifles came up. The six returned cases remained beside the entrance, untouched.

She looked into the missing counting room.

“You took the whole thing.”

“We had a disagreement with the service,” Arthur said.

Serrat struggled upright against his desk.

“Heisenberg will find you.”

Arthur turned toward him. The manager flinched before the cowboy moved any closer.

“You tell him what happened. Tell him you watched it. Don't go improving the story till he starts believing you nearly won.”

Dessa held out her hand.

“Three hundred.”

Arthur glanced at Billy.

“Let me in.”

Billy admitted him. Inside, the counting cage stood in the storage area with its spilled silver still strewn across the floor. Chris, full-sized beside it, stared at the structure's reinforced door.

“We could use this.”

“We are using it,” Arthur said. “Open it.”

Chris retrieved a pry tool from the bench and worked on the damaged door seam. It gave after a few hard movements. Arthur went in, counted out the agreed silver from the loose money and put it into one of the collection bags.

Outside again, he gave the bag to Dessa. She counted it without apologizing for the time.

Behind her, Serrat pressed a hand against his stomach.

“You can't take company funds.”

Dessa tied the bag closed.

“Put it against the account.”

She took the handcart with the six cases. Two frightened clerks, released by Billy onto the landing at Daniel's request, helped her steer it out. They wanted to get away from him badly enough to accept any task leading through the street door.

The guards followed after Arthur told them to leave their rifles. The man he'd struck held his jaw. Serrat remained beside the desk, alive, watching the last of his staff walk out.

Billy looked up.

“Now.”

“Billy,” Chris said from inside.

The catfish man reached for the bronze carp.

“We just got a fucking counting room!”

“And it needs a centerpiece.”

Billy tore the fixture from its mounting. Water burst from the severed feed pipe, striking Serrat's desk and washing the receipt into his lap.

The carp vanished into Billy's mouth.

Inside, Chris watched it arrive beside the counting cage. Without the pipe and basin, it lay on one side with its mouth hanging open.

Daniel's voice came from outside.

“Looks thirsty.”

“Don't you fucking encourage him.”

They left by the street entrance. Arthur walked beside Billy; Daniel rode a low cloud above the road, sitting cross-legged with his bottle against one knee. A torn patch at his brow remained sore, but the cut was closing. None of them had acquired a reason to linger under Heisenberg's sign.

By late afternoon they were behind the abandoned loading ramp again, out of the main traffic. Billy let Arthur and Daniel into the interior, then yielded to Chris, who remained outside to watch the service road while the others finished sorting the money.

The count, after Dessa's three hundred, was two thousand one hundred silver.

Arthur sat back on his heels.

“Well.”

Daniel nodded. “That's a much better noise than six boxes.”

Arthur relayed the figure through the banjo outlet, with Billy's permission. Chris laughed; his voice came back to them with the live sound from his headlamp.

“Put mine somewhere the fish can't fall on it.”

Billy stood beside his bronze acquisition, turning it upright with both hands. Inside, he looked no less enormous for being the one who wasn't outside.

“A piece of this quality deserves water.”

“It deserves a buyer.”

Daniel leaned toward Arthur. “He's going to put it over the television.”

Arthur considered the open mouth.

“Might drown the horse.”

Chris went quiet outside.

Then, very clearly, through the monitor:

“Billy. Do not.”

The catfish man's smile widened.

“A man can't even consider interior design.”

Arthur divided nothing yet. The money was theirs together, and deciding who deserved how much could wait until they were all looking at the same pile. He left it in the recovered cage, now secure inside Billy, and came to the monitor.

“We ought to move before dark.”

Chris watched a wagon pass the mouth of the service road.

“Coast?”

“Could do. Kaze went that way.”

“He'll be pissed he missed this.”

Daniel joined Arthur at the screen.

“He can be pissed comfortably. We can afford a chair now.”

They laughed, and for a little while it was simply good to have won.

Chris watched the service road while Daniel unfolded Harl's coastal sketch beside the monitor. Arthur pointed out the approach they'd used, and Chris confirmed it from his own memory of the flight. They could head toward Noxus without waiting for a ship. Finding Kaze would still take more than knowing which enormous stretch of country he'd wandered into.

“We check the harbor first,” Arthur said. “Ask about the fellow who makes a nuisance of himself.”

Daniel looked up. “That narrows us down to everyone we've traveled with.”

“The shirtless one.”

“Much better.”

Billy bent over the bronze carp and inspected its mouth.

“We should bring a gift.”

Chris's answer arrived through the monitor at once.

“We are not giving him my fountain.”

Arthur turned his head slowly. Daniel covered his grin with the bottle.

Billy straightened to his full height.

“Your fountain?”

Outside, Chris stared down the road, realizing exactly what he'd said.

“I meant our fountain.”

“Oh, I heard you perfectly, Christopher.”
