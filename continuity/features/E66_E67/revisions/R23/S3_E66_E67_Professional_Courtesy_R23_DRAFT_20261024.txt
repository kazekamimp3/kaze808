Season 3, Episodes 66–67 — Professional Courtesy

The car appeared where Arthur had been about to put his foot.

He stopped. A blue bonnet filled the empty gravel in front of him, gold wheels settled with a small crunch, and Chris caught the ball on its return.

Arthur looked at the car. Then at Chris. Then under the car.

“Well,” he said. “That would've been rude.”

“I said move.”

“You pointed at some dirt.”

“That's where I wanted you to stop being.”

Behind them, the ropewalk's long shed threw a strip of shade across the export depot. Beyond its roof lay the harbor: stacked masts, white glare, the low black back of Dr. Evil's submarine. The Sins had stayed on the coast while their remaining cargo awaited a buyer. Several days had passed. The water had kept moving. So had everybody else's problems.

Billy had yielded the outside to Chris for the afternoon. Now his voice emerged from Chris's retracted headgear with the considerate sorrow of a minister examining a cremation urn full of hot dogs.

“There she is. All that polished metal, and still dependent upon Christopher.”

Chris lifted the bonnet.

Arthur walked slowly around the Subaru. He touched a tire with his boot, crouched to look behind it, then peered into the open engine bay.

“Where's the thing that pulls it?”

Chris tapped the engine housing. “Makes its own power. Turns the wheels through the gearbox.”

“All four?”

“Yep.”

Arthur crouched again. He followed the underside as far as he could see, then stood with a hand on the bonnet's edge.

“And you decide how fast?”

“Within reason.”

“Whose?”

“Usually whoever's still in the road.”

Billy made a soft, disappointed sound through the headgear.

“A chauffeur ought to inspire confidence, Christopher. Tell the gentleman about the brakes.”

“It has brakes.”

“There. Already a more comforting service.”

“You want me to tell him it has doors too?”

Arthur opened one. The padded interior caught his interest. He pressed a thumb into the seat.

“Got better furniture than some places I've robbed.”

“Clean your boots before you get in.”

Arthur looked from the seat to Chris.

“Didn't say I was gettin' in.”

“You were looking at it like you were.”

“Well, now I'm worried I'll lower the value by wantin' to.”

Daynah came around the shed carrying the tool roll Chris had asked him to fetch. Mahnah followed, nose low, then stopped beside the rear wheel. One sniff. Another. Chris saw the dog look for a place to stand.

“Don't.”

Mahnah looked at him.

“That is not a suggestion with a second round of voting.”

Daynah handed over the tools. “He's looking at your wheel.”

“He's looking at it with his bladder.”

The dog wandered to a weed growing out of the shed wall. Chris watched until the matter had been settled elsewhere.

From the harbor lane came a familiar rising whistle. R2-D2 rolled around the end of the ropewalk, blue dome turning toward the open bonnet. His central wheel bumped over a shallow rut. He took the last stretch at an impatient little trundle.

“Oh, there you are.” Chris crouched and pointed at the engine. “Checking the belt. You wanna look, look. Don't take something out and then make that ambulance noise at me.”

I don't speak astromech either. But some languages have a universal conjugation for get your fucking hand out of my way.

Chris let him inspect the reachable fittings while he checked the belt's seating and tension. Arthur watched the droid with narrowed, interested eyes.

“Does he know what he's doing?”

“More than most people I've worked with.”

“What'd he say?”

“No fucking idea.”

“Useful arrangement.”

“We skip a lot of meetings.”

Across the lane, Kaze sat on a low wall, shirtless in the afternoon heat, watching Chris work. His black shoes hung above the dust. He had started smiling before he spoke.

“Ask him how many horses.”

Chris looked up. “Don't start.”

“It's a measurement.”

“Of horses.”

“Of work.”

“Done by horses.”

“Your house is inside a fish, Chris.”

Billy's voice warmed.

“A gentleman remembers the neighborhood before criticizing the question.”

Kaze bent forward, laughing.

Chris picked up his wrench again.

Arthur rested his hands on his belt. “Seems a fine machine.”

“It is.”

“I'm interested.”

“I know. That's what's making this difficult.”

An armored man had stopped at the mouth of the lane.

Good. I wanted to see the car fly, not identify its owner from the upholstery.

Daynah lunged for the remaining two bags. Dr. Evil swept them off the desk toward Dutch. The doctor tried to follow, caught his sore ribs against the chair arm, and made a small noise of sincere betrayal.

Dutch caught one bag. The other struck the floor beside his boots.

Arthur appeared in the doorway.

“Dutch.”

Dutch's gun was already coming up.

“You son of a bitch.”

Arthur slapped the barrel aside with his right hand as Dutch fired. The round gouged the doorframe beside Arthur's head. He shoved Dutch backward into the office, stepped across the threshold, and caught the gun wrist before it could come around again.

Dutch drove his shoulder into Arthur, trying to force him against the jamb and get his trapped hand between them. Arthur's reconstructed legs held. The shove moved Dutch's own boots instead.

“Don't,” Arthur said.

Dutch hit him in the mouth with his free hand.

Arthur's lip split against his teeth. He turned back slowly.

“You always were better at askin'.”

Arthur stepped outside Dutch's lead foot, turned the trapped wrist away from both their bodies, and drove the gun hand against the desk. Wood cracked. The revolver fell. Dutch doubled forward; Arthur caught the back of his waistcoat and hauled him clear of the bag before he could reach down again.

Clifford had already gone through the rear door.

He ran along the terrace toward the stairs, revolver in hand, keeping the office wall between himself and Arthur as he took the route to the service boat. Through the shattered railing he saw Kaze on the lower landing.

And Kaze saw the bag in the boat.

“Oh, come on,” Kaze said. “You made it a water thing?”

The crewman was a boat length from the landing, pulling hard on one oar to turn into the channel. Kaze crouched. The stone beneath his shoes cracked as he launched.

He crossed the gap and landed with one foot on the gunwale, the other on a thwart. The little boat heeled until cold harbor water poured over its side. The crewman lost his oar and grabbed the edge.

Kaze caught the money bag before it slid beneath the bench.

The man drew a knife.

Kaze looked at it, then at the submerged side of the boat.

“You wanna do that now?”

The man lunged.

Kaze caught the knife wrist and turned with the boat's roll. The crewman's knees hit the bench. Kaze shoved his head down and struck once at the base of the skull. The body went loose across the wood. The knife dropped between the boards.

The boat continued tipping.

Kaze shoved off the thwart and jumped back toward the landing with the bag in his hand. Behind him, the hull rolled. Its side struck the water flat, throwing a sheet of spray against the pavilion's stone supports. The dead crewman disappeared beneath it.

Clifford fired from halfway down the service stair.

The bullet struck Kaze high in the shoulder as he landed. His upper body snapped around; thick black blood spattered the pale landing stones. He steadied himself against a post, looked up, and saw Clifford already retreating toward cover.

“That one's personal,” Kaze observed.

Above them, Daynah put his shoulder under the damaged office lintel. The outer wall had carried part of its load; now the beam was sagging toward the desk. He braced, boots biting into the floorboards, and lifted it back enough to stop the ceiling from following the wall outside.

“Arthur. Money. Now.”

Dr. Evil edged around the chair, watching the lintel bend.

“You've removed a load-bearing wall.”

Daynah shifted one boot over a floor joist and drove his shoulder higher.

“Yeah. Now I'm the fuckin' wall. Get out from under me.”

The doctor moved.

Arthur had Dutch against the inside wall. His hand held the collar beneath Dutch's jaw. Dutch couldn't step forward; Arthur wasn't tightening his grip.

Dutch saw it too.

“Go on,” he said. “Finish the family tradition.”

Arthur's jaw tightened.

Above them, the damaged beam creaked against Daynah's shoulder.

Then Arthur released him with a hard shove toward the doorway and picked up both bags.

“Walk.”

Dutch looked at the money, then at Arthur.

“That's what you came for?”

“Yes.”

Dutch stared at him. Arthur tucked the bags under his arm.

Outside, Chris had reversed into the loading recess, nose toward the road. He saw Clifford coming down the last section of stair and leaned across to lower the driver's window a little.

“Hey, Clifford. You miss your ride?”

Clifford turned and shot the driver's mirror off.

The housing exploded against the blue door. Chris ducked behind the wheel as another round struck the raised portion of the ballistic window, leaving a white crushed star in the glass. The protection held. The man behind it was suddenly very quiet.

Clifford ran for the side passage.

Chris slammed the car into gear.

The Subaru surged out of the recess, front wheels cutting across Clifford's route. He stopped short of the bumper and threw himself back behind a stone mooring block. The car struck the low wooden barrier beyond him and broke it into three pieces. Chris braked before the front wheels reached the edge.

Dust rolled past the open slit in the window.

“That was a fucking original mirror!”

From his headgear, Billy said, “Christopher, the man has previously lost considerably more down a cliff.”

“Then he should appreciate keeping things attached!”

Chris held the brake, keeping the car across the passage. Clifford crouched lower and brought his barrel around the mooring block toward the front wheel.

“Perhaps ask him to aim at a less sentimental component,” Billy said.

“Don't give him a fucking menu!”

Clifford fired from behind the block. The round struck the front wing above the tire, tearing blue paint and metal. Chris pulled the window up fully, held the car across the passage, and watched the cover rather than blindly driving over the edge after him.

Kaze stepped onto the lower terrace with the bag against his bloody shoulder. He saw Chris, the missing mirror, and Clifford behind the mooring block.

He started laughing.

“You shot the bit he checks himself in. Now he's gotta look at you.”

“Kaze,” Chris said through the cracked-open window, “do something useful.”

Kaze looked at the blocked passage, then at the raised stone shoulder of the wharf beside it. Beyond that shoulder, an older outboard pier ran parallel to the pavilion. It stood empty except for a capstan, several bollards and a line of sun-bleached railings. Clifford could use it to get around the car.

Kaze set the money bag behind a post.

Daynah's voice came from above.

“Not this building!”

Kaze pointed at the outer pier.

“That one.”

He stepped down onto its approach, lowered his right shoulder, and punched the stone abutment beneath the first arch.

The sound arrived through the ground before it reached the air.

A deep, brutal crack ran along the wharf. The arch kicked sideways. Stone blocks the size of cattle burst from its face, and the deck above them lifted in one solid strip before breaking under its own weight.

The capstan rose against the sky.

Then the first span fell into the harbor.

The next followed, pulled off its supports by the falling deck. Railings snapped in a wave away from Kaze. A mooring chain whipped straight, tore its bollard out of the third span, and vanished into a white wall of water.

Spray struck the pavilion windows. The Subaru rocked on its suspension. Across the lane, people dropped behind carts as fragments hammered the road.

For several breaths, the outer pier was simply gone behind water and dust.

When it settled, broken arches jutted from the harbor like teeth kicked halfway out of a mouth. The capstan lay sideways on a slab below the surface. No path remained around the car.

Clifford stayed crouched behind the mooring block, one arm over his face.

Chris stared through his damaged window.

Kaze stood beside the broken approach, black blood running down his chest. The wound in his shoulder was drawing closed beneath it. Stone powder had stuck to the wet skin.

He looked at the absence he'd made.

“There.”

The last loose stones kept falling. You could hear each one hit the water.

From upstairs came Dr. Evil's voice.

“We just recovered this property!”

“Building's still there,” Kaze called back.

Vessa pushed past the doctor to see the harbor. Her face went empty at the broken pier.

“That was mine too.”

Kaze looked up at her. His smile had gone, but he didn't look sorry.

“Yeah.”

Arthur came down the internal stairs with both bags. Dutch followed several paces behind, carrying his recovered revolver low. His eyes searched the terrace for Clifford.

Daynah lowered the office beam onto the intact side of the desk, tested the support, and stepped back. It would hold the damaged corner for now. Plaster sifted down onto the desk. Vessa moved her papers away from it without looking at him.

At the foot of the stairs, one of Vessa's returning guards raised a short gun toward Arthur. Before he could fire, Mahnah came through the front doorway low and fast. The dog's shoulder struck the guard above the knees and folded him over the edge of a gaming table. His weapon skidded across the floor.

Mahnah caught his coat between the shoulders and dragged him away from Arthur, claws scraping channels through the boards. He pinned him beside a pillar and held. The man's hands clawed at the cloth around his throat.

Daynah reached them and took the gun off the floor.

“Leave.”

Mahnah's eyes stayed on the man. Daynah touched his shoulder and repeated it. The dog released the coat, stepped back, and growled when the guard tried to rise.

Kaze retrieved the bag from behind the post and came through the rear door carrying it. He saw the guard reach toward a knife at his belt.

He kicked the man's head against the pillar.

The crack carried between the pillars. His body dropped flat. Mahnah stepped away from the blood.

Daynah looked at Kaze, then at the still hand beside the knife.

“You got what you came for,” he said.

“So did he.” Kaze pointed at the knife. “Just later.”

Daynah didn't smile. He took the money bag from him and went toward the door.

Outside, Chris had stopped the engine and climbed out behind the car's open door. Dutch emerged from the pavilion and saw Clifford behind the mooring block.

“Jack. With me.”

Chris looked over the top of the door.

“Your boy owes me a mirror.”

Dutch's gun came up.

Billy came out.

Chris's body rolled inward through the brutal, continuous inversion, and the enormous swamp-colored figure stood in the same place with his own banjo. The open car door pressed against his belly. He moved it aside without tearing it off.

“You are finished pointing that at him.”

Dutch didn't lower the gun immediately. Billy didn't look away.

From the banjo, Chris said, “I had the fucking door.”

“And now you have a wall,” Billy replied.

Clifford stood slowly behind the mooring block.

“You let me sit in there,” he said to Billy. “You let me fish. And now you're helping them rob us.”

Billy's gaze moved to him.

“I did let you fish.”

“Was that funny too?”

“No.”

Clifford looked from Billy to the bags in Daynah's hands.

“Doesn't make much difference from here.”

Billy didn't offer him a story about it.

Dutch lowered his weapon. He reached Clifford, took him by the sleeve, and pulled him away from the block toward the road. Arthur stood aside. Neither man thanked him.

Dr. Evil came out last, followed by his surviving crewman. The doctor saw the bags, the broken pier, and the overturned service boat drifting against the rubble. His expression changed when he failed to see the other crewman.

“Where's my man?”

Kaze pointed toward the boat.

The doctor looked at the water for a long moment.

“And the money.”

“Here,” Daynah said.

He didn't hold it up like a prize.

Dr. Evil faced Kaze.

“We did the work.”

“I saw.”

“Then you understand why this is objectionable.”

Kaze rubbed a wet thumb over his shoulder, looking at the black smear it left.

“You want me to pretend I thought it was free?”

The doctor stared at him.

Kaze looked back.

“You earned it. We took it.”

Dr. Evil's hand closed around the receipt in his pocket.

Vessa appeared in the doorway behind them.

“You aren't doing this here again.”

Arthur turned.

“Ma'am—”

“Don't call me that.”

He stopped.

She looked directly at Dr. Evil.

“You brought people who knew you. You can take them away.”

“They are not subcontractors.”

“Out.”

The doctor opened his mouth, glanced at the dead guard visible behind her, then closed it again. His hand came out of his pocket empty.

Arthur handed his two bags to Daynah, who passed all three to Billy. Billy swallowed them in one deliberate intake, preserving the money inside. The bags disappeared. Dr. Evil watched the last knot pass Billy's teeth.

Then Billy looked toward the pavilion's ornate, undamaged entrance sign. A wooden woman held a bowl above a carved border of shells.

Chris's voice sharpened through the banjo.

“Don't.”

“Fine workmanship.”

“We just got the money. We are leaving with the money.”

Billy leaned slightly to inspect the carving from where he stood.

“Look at the fingers. Somebody bothered with the joints.”

“It's a sign.”

“She's serving, Christopher. There is a difference between holding a bowl and offering one. That sculptor understood hospitality.”

Vessa stood beneath it. Behind her, plaster fell from the damaged office into the hall.

“It's staying.”

Billy looked at her.

“Madam, I was appreciating it.”

“Appreciate it walking.”

From the banjo, Chris said, “Nobody needs a wooden woman holding a bowl over my fucking television.”

“Over it? Good Lord, no. The bowl would obstruct your view.”

“You've already picked a wall.”

“A suitable wall exists. That is not a crime.”

Kaze looked back at the pavilion, then up at Billy.

“Bit late to start checking.”

Billy straightened. His hands stayed away from the sign.

“Another time,” he said, and turned away.

Vessa said nothing. She had heard him.

Billy yielded beside the stopped car. Chris got in and drove slowly back along the lane while the others walked. Mahnah stayed close to Daynah, occasionally looking behind them. The stolen money remained safely inside through the change.

Arthur lingered just long enough for Dutch to approach.

Dutch's revolver hung beside his leg. His bandage had bled through.

“You needed the money that badly?”

Arthur looked toward his family walking away.

“No.”

Dutch nodded once. Slowly.

“I see.”

Arthur looked at the blood soaking through Dutch's bandage.

“Don't come after them.”

Dutch's face hardened.

“You don't get to tell me who my enemies are.”

Arthur held his gaze, then turned and followed the others.

Back at the ropewalk, Chris parked and got out. A brief exchange brought Billy outside to release the three bags onto a clear patch of canvas. Daynah counted with Arthur while Kaze sat on the wall and wiped blood off his hand with a scrap of packing cloth. The shoulder had closed. The stain down his body had not.

Three hundred and sixty silver. All of it.

Arthur put it with the common purse. Two thousand eight hundred and fifty-one. Daniel's hundred and ten stayed separate, exactly where Arthur kept it. Nobody suggested otherwise.

Billy yielded, and Chris returned to the outside beside his car. He crouched at the damaged door. R2 rolled over, inspected the loose wiring where the mirror had been, and gave a low warble.

“Yeah,” Chris said. “I know.”

The droid extended a tool toward the exposed connection. Chris passed him the loose end and held the remaining housing steady while he isolated it. Then Chris secured the broken mount so it wouldn't tear farther into the door on the road.

The glass would need replacing. So would the mirror. The car still drove.

Kaze watched him work.

“Original mirror.”

Chris didn't look up.

“You destroyed a fucking pier.”

“Wasn't original.”

“To you.”

Kaze considered that, then gave a small, unwilling laugh.

Chris looked over his shoulder at Arthur.

“You enjoyed the ride, though.”

Arthur sat with the tied purse between his boots. His split lip had almost closed. He touched it with his tongue.

“Reckon I did.”

“Get you another one when I can see behind me.”

Arthur looked toward the lane they'd come from.

“Can do that without a mirror.”

At the pavilion, Dutch helped his surviving crewman turn the empty service boat back upright. They could see the dead man caught beneath the edge of the broken pier. Retrieving him would take more than reaching over the side.

Clifford sat on the lowest intact step and reloaded. He placed four spent cases beside him, one after another, then replaced them with cartridges from what he carried.

Dutch climbed out of the boat. Wet trousers clung to his shins. The sleeve binding his arm had slipped, and Clifford got up to knot it again.

“He was going to hit me with it,” Clifford said.

“I believe you.”

“The car. He was laughing before.”

Dutch held still while Clifford tightened the cloth.

“You don't need to explain him to me.”

Above them, the surviving crewman spoke to the doctor.

“Are we going back there?”

Dr. Evil looked toward the ropewalk. The Sins were visible between passing carts. Daynah's broad back. Chris beside his car. The thin, shirtless man sitting on the wall.

“Not with what we have here.”

“And him?” The crewman pointed toward the water.

“We bring him back.”

The doctor looked down at the pistol he'd taken from the pavilion, then put it away. Across the water, his submarine lay at its berth, a long walk from the man trapped under the broken stone. He told his remaining crewman to fetch a line from their boat.

Then he stood with Dutch and Clifford, looking down at what the afternoon had cost.

In his hand was the paper Vessa had signed when she paid.

Three hundred and sixty silver. Services completed. Received in full.

He smoothed the crease with his thumb.

Clifford looked at the paper.

“You keeping that?”

The doctor folded it carefully.

“Yes.”

For the first time that afternoon, nobody misunderstood what he meant.
