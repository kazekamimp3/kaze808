# EPISODE 84: COOLANT & CONTEMPT

There is a specific frequency of alarm that does not care about your feelings, your personal hygiene, or the fact that your spandex suit is currently sixty percent burnt caramel and forty percent dried Hutt slime.

It was a wet, flat bloop-screeech coming out of the Falcon’s secondary diagnostic console. Every four seconds, on the dot.

Bloop-screeech.

"If that console makes that noise one more time," I said, leaning over the pilot’s yoke with my forehead resting against the cracked plastic casing of the artificial horizon, "I am going to strip naked, coat myself in powdered sugar, and jump headfirst into the nearest cooling turbine."

From the copilot’s chair, Bee Moo didn’t look up.

Bee Moo had a pair of rusted wire cutters in one hand and the stolen security sidearm in the other. They were sitting cross-legged on the leather upholstery, boots digging straight into the seat cushions, systematically snapping the safety solenoids off the blaster’s power cell just to make it fire hotter. A thin trail of sulfur-scented kelp dangled from the corner of their mouth like a soggy cigar.

"Go ahead," Bee Moo said, teeth grinding on kelp fiber. "Turbine's dead anyway. You'd just bounce off the blades and make a wet sound."

Bloop-screeech.

"Thank you, Dr. Phil. Your bedside manner has really blossomed since you let four tons of amphibian cholesterol treat you like a novelty swizzle stick."

Bee Moo paused. The wire cutters hovered over an exposed copper trace.

Slowly, their head turned. The eyes were bloodshot, rimmed in yellow station soot, completely devoid of the twitchy, hand-wringing terror that used to make them flinch whenever someone dropped a wrench.

"Say 'cholesterol' again, Wade."

"Cholesterol," I said cheerfully. "Lipids. Triglycerides. Saturated trans-fats in a leather vest. You want the whole nutritional panel, or just the part where—"

Bee Moo threw the wire cutters.

They didn't throw them to scare me. They threw them with full, overhand shoulder torque, aimed directly at my throat.

I jerked my head to the left. The cutters slammed into the cockpit bulkhead three inches from my ear, denting the aluminum trim and clattering down across the sublight throttle quadrant.

"Okay," I said, exhaling slowly through my teeth. "Aggression issues logged. Filing that under 'Things To Discuss With Our HR Department If We Ever Hire One And Stop Smuggling Weapons In A Flying Dumpster.'"

Bloop-screeech.

The navigation screen flashed a violent, uncooperative red:

SECONDARY COOLANT LOOP: SEVERED
MANIFOLD PRESSURE: 14% AND DROPPING
CORE RUNAWAY IN: 18 MINUTES
HYPERDRIVE INHIBITION: MANUAL OVERRIDE DISABLED

Outside the cockpit canopy, the view was not what you’d call vacation material.

We were drifting dead-stick in the upper atmospheric eddies of Ooze-Nine’s gas giant—a swirling, sickly cauldron of ammonia clouds, liquid sulfur bands, and jagged metallic wreckage from orbital strip-mining barges that had decayed into the gravity well decades ago. Below us, the clouds looked like bruised bananas blended with motor oil. Above us, the iron silhouette of Station Ooze-Nine was a tiny, jagged tooth against the starfield, spitting out automated radar pings like an angry hornet’s nest with a docking fee.

"We have eighteen minutes before the reactor converts this entire ship into an expanding sphere of glowing aluminum confetti," I said, unbuckling the five-point harness. "The primary coolant trunk under the main hold is pissing freon and hydraulic blood. Which means somebody has to crawl into the bilge, bypass the rupture with a patch sleeve, and manually toggle the secondary valve."

I looked at Bee Moo.

Bee Moo looked at me, chewed another centimeter of raw kelp, and spat a fibrous clump onto the deck between my boots.

"Not doing it," Bee Moo said.

"What do you mean, not doing it? You’re smaller than me! You have bones that can actually compress without sounding like a pack of dry spaghetti snapping in half! I’m six-foot-two of scarred cancer meat! If I crawl into that access tube, I’m going to get wedged like a Thanksgiving turkey in a mail slot!"

"Sounds like your problem," Bee Moo said, snapping a modified power cell back into the stolen blaster with a sharp clack. "I got the cipher codes. I got the fuel drums. My job was on the station."

"Your job on the station was getting us shot at by three different local unions!"

"We're here, aren't we?"

"We are currently falling into a planet made of liquid battery acid at ninety meters per second!"

Bee Moo shrugged. It wasn't bravado. It wasn't an act. It was that terrifying, flatline indifference that only comes from somebody whose moral compass had its needle snapped off and boiled in oil.

"Then move fast," Bee Moo muttered, sliding the blaster into their waistband. "You've got seventeen minutes now."

I stared at them for three seconds, genuinely weighing whether it would be faster to throttle them or fix the ship. The ship had fewer teeth.

"You're a monster," I said, grabbing a heavy hydro-spanner and a roll of industrial thermal tape from the emergency locker behind the pilot's seat. "A sticky, unwashed, morally bankrupt little gremlin. When we survive this, I’m taking you to space church."

"Church is for people who haven't smelled Batuna's breath," Bee Moo rasped, leaning their head back against the headrest and closing their eyes. "Bring back a rag. Floor’s greasy."

I dropped down through the cockpit access ladder, cursed the day George Lucas was born, and crawled headfirst into the underbelly of the beast.

The crawlway beneath the Falcon’s main ring corridor is not designed for sentient beings. It’s designed for disgruntled pipe-fitters with three joints per arm and a death wish. The air was five hundred degrees, smelling of scalding freon and electrical ozone that tasted like pennies on the back of your tongue. Every three feet, a bundle of exposed high-voltage cables hung down like weeping willows from hell, spitting blue sparks into the bilge water.

Bloop-screeech.

Even down here, through six inches of deck plating, that damn console alarm was vibrating through the ship’s ribs.

"I hear you, you little bastard!" I grunted, dragging my shoulders through a pool of lukewarm coolant that immediately soaked through the knees of my suit. "Keep squealing and see if I don't replace you with an eight-track player from a 1978 Camaro!"

I reached the primary junction box. It looked like someone had taken a fire axe to a spaghetti dinner. The main supply line was shredded—caramelized syrup from the Episode 81 circus had gummed up the pressure-relief valve, causing the pipe to balloon and rupture like an overcooked hot dog. White, freezing vapor was blasting across the bulkhead, coating everything in a layer of crystalline frost.

"Okay," I muttered, hauling the patch sleeve over my shoulder. "Simple plumbing. Lefty loosey, righty—holy sweet mother of Mary and Joseph!"

A violent, bone-rattling shudder tore through the Falcon’s hull.

It wasn't engine vibration. It was the distinct, teeth-loosening hollow CLANG of magnetic docking grapples slamming into the dorsal hull plating right above my head.

Through the crawlway vents, the ship’s short-range comms array crackled to life with a blast of static that sounded like rocks in a blender:

"ATTENTION UNREGISTERED CORELLIAN FREIGHTER. THIS IS EYEHOLES SYNDICATE SALVAGE-ENFORCEMENT CUTTER 'GIZZARD-FOUR'. YOUR VESSEL HAS BEEN TAGGED UNDER WRIT OF IMMEDIATE ATTACHMENT. CUT YOUR SUBS AND PREPARE FOR BOARDING OR WE VENT YOUR ATMOSPHERE FROM THE OUTSIDE."

I froze, flat on my belly in six inches of toxic sludge, holding a half-glued patch sleeve in one hand and a wrench in the other.

"Are you kidding me?!" I screamed at the ceiling. "We haven't even finished the coolant repair! We're in the middle of a commercial break! Can't you repo guys at least wait until the reactor reaches critical mass so you die with us?!"

Then, from the corridor deck plates right above me, I heard the cockpit ladder creak.

Bee Moo’s boots hit the metal floor.

Slow. Steady. Dragging the heels.

Scrrrrape. Scrrrrape.

"Hey! Bee Moo!" I hissed through the ventilation grille. "We've got company! Dorsal airlock! Syndicate goons with magnetic clamps! Tell me you're getting the dorsal quad-cannons online!"

Bee Moo’s voice came down through the grate, muffled, bored, and dripping with venom.

"Dorsal turret's jammed with burnt sugar," Bee Moo said.

"Then clear it!"

"No."

"What do you mean, no?!"

Above me, I heard the unmistakable sound of the stolen heavy blaster being cocked. Then the sound of the main cargo lift whining as Bee Moo keyed the dorsal boarding collar controls.

"Bee Moo, wait! Do not open that hatch! They have automatic weapons, you little psycho!"

"They have fuel filters," Bee Moo said from the darkness of the cargo hold. "And I want their boots."

The dorsal airlock hissed.

The heavy magnetic clamps groaned as the outer hatch yielded.

And from up above, the first Syndicate boarding team dropped straight into the frying pan.

# EPISODE 85: DEEP SULPHUR REPO

The first rule of boarding an armed vessel that’s currently choking to death inside a toxic gas giant is: Check your corners.

The second rule is: Never board a ship where the crew has already lost its collective mind and started smelling like vinegar and bad intentions.

The Syndicate repo-crew didn't know either rule.

They dropped through the upper dorsal transfer chute in full tactical gear—three Trandoshan heavy mercenaries in pressurized yellow hazard rigs, backed by a cybernetic foreman with four mechanical arms and a pulse rifle welded to his right shoulder. They hit the deck plates of the Falcon’s main lounge with their boots locked, shotguns raised, looking for a trembling crew hiding behind the holographic chess table.

Instead, they found Bee Moo.

Bee Moo was sitting on an upturned cargo canister in the center of the bay, legs dangling, holding a freshly lit tabac-stick in one hand and the modified blaster resting lazily across their kneecap.

The lead Trandoshan leveled a heavy scattergun at Bee Moo’s sternum, his reptilian tongue flicking through the intake grille of his helmet.

"On your knees, meat," the lizard hissed through his vocalizer. "This hull belongs to the Eyeholes Recovery Guild. Any resistance will be met with immediate—"

Bee Moo didn't even wait for the verb.

They raised the stolen blaster—the one where they’d snapped the limiter coils clean off in the cockpit—and pulled the trigger at point-blank range.

The modified cell didn't fire a bolt. It fired a solid, blinding lance of overcharged plasma that looked like an angry purple lightning bolt. The blast struck the Trandoshan’s chest armor, blew the composite plating clean through his spinal column, and sent his two-hundred-pound scaly ass flying backward through the airlock chute, trailing smoke and severed pneumatic lines.

The remaining two mercenaries froze.

The cybernetic foreman stared at the hole in his point-man’s torso, his four mechanical arms twitching in genuine, algorithmic confusion.

"What... what is that weapon?!" the foreman blared. "That's an unauthorized civilian modification! That violates twelve regional safety covenants!"

Bee Moo took a slow drag off the tabac-stick, blew the smoke straight into the foreman's optical sensor, and cocked the weapon again.

"Die mad about it," Bee Moo said.

That was my cue.

I kicked the crawlway floor hatch open with both heels, erupting out of the deck like an extremely irritable, red-spandex groundhog covered in grey coolant sludge and raw homicidal intent.

"Surprise, mechanical boy!" I roared, drawing both katanas in a twin-blade cross. "Did somebody order the two-piece combo with extra MSG?!"

I didn't give the foreman time to calculate the angles. I closed the distance in two strides, slid between his lower mechanical legs, and took both of his hydraulic ankle cables off with a double reverse slash.

Sparks geysered toward the ceiling. The foreman let out an electronic screech, his balance gyros screaming as he tilted backward toward the open cargo bay ramp.

The last Trandoshan tried to swing his rifle toward me, but Bee Moo was already moving.

Not running. Not lunging. Bee Moo just walked up behind the lizard while he was looking at me, stuck the muzzle of the modified blaster into the soft, unarmored seal behind his left knee, and fired.

The Trandoshan collapsed with a roar of pure agony.

Before the lizard could hit the floor, Bee Moo grabbed him by the shoulder harness, spun him around, and calmly stripped the spare ammunition bandolier off his chest with one hand while holding the hot barrel against his jaw with the other.

"Bee Moo!" I yelled, parrying a flailing blow from the foreman's mechanical arm and severing the limb at the elbow with a wet shing. "We don't need his jewelry! We need to decouple their cutter before its docking clamps rip our dorsal plating off!"

Bee Moo didn't look at me. They were checking the charge on the Trandoshan's bandolier.

"These fit our sidearms," Bee Moo muttered. "And he's got twenty credits in his boot."

"You are looting the wounded while we are actively being boarded! That is literally a war crime! Not a cool, edgy antihero war crime—a tacky, cheapskate flea-market war crime!"

"DISCONNECTING IS NOT AN OPTION," the four-armed foreman gargled, dragging himself across the floor on his two remaining steel limbs, his shoulder cannon spinning up with an ominous, high-pitched whine. "THE GIZZARD-FOUR HAS LOCKED ITS GYROS. IF THIS HULL FALLS INTO THE GAS BANDS, WE ALL BURN TOGETHER!"

The entire ship shuddered violently.

A deep, tearing groan vibrated through the bulkheads. Looking through the cargo bay viewports, the horizon was vanishing. The Gizzard-Four—a fat, armored tug with twin tractor booms—was dragging us down. Its docking clamps were locked hard onto our hull, but its own thrusters were misaligned, fighting the Falcon’s uneven weight.

Both ships were caught in the gravitational vortex of the gas giant’s upper sulfur shelf.

Yellow clouds were slamming against the viewport glass like dirty tidal waves. The friction was turning the outer hull plating a dull, cherry red.

ATMOSPHERIC DRAG: CRITICAL
HULL INTEGRITY: 41%
DORSAL STRESS LIMIT EXCEEDED

"Wade!" the comm in my ear buzzed—not from the cockpit, but from the station's short-range relay that was still picking up Batuna’s broadcast sector.

Nobody was helping us. We were forty miles deep in an acidic swamp in the sky, clamped to a repo ship full of dead Trandoshans, with our cooling lines half-glued and our hyperdrive locked in a coma.

"Right," I muttered, shaking the grease off my blades. "Plan B. It's like Plan A, but with more property damage and a much higher chance of internal hemorrhaging."

I sprinted past Bee Moo toward the dorsal boarding collar ladder.

"Where are you going?!" Bee Moo yelled—the first time their voice had raised above a dead monotone in an hour.

"I’m going to go have a polite conversation with their pilot!" I shouted back, grabbing the rungs. "Keep that cybernetic blender from crawling into the hyperdrive room, or I swear to God I will make you eat that entire box of kelp without salt!"

I vaulted through the severed airlock into the umbilical tube connecting the Falcon to the Gizzard-Four.

The tube was vibrating like a guitar string under two thousand pounds of tension. Wind screamed through the magnetic seals—pure sulfur gas, burning my throat through the mask's filters like swallowed bleach.

Across the threshold, inside the cockpit of the Syndicate cutter, two more repo goons were frantically wrestling with their flight controls, screaming at each other in a dialect I didn't recognize but understood completely: We're all going to die because we tried to repossess an insured vehicle.

I didn't knock.

I hit the cutter's cockpit door with a running shoulder tackle, blew the lock off with a blast from my damaged .45, and rolled onto the control deck with both swords singing.

The fight took twelve seconds.

One goon went out the emergency canopy vent. The other ended up duct-taped to the secondary navigation console with his own communications wire, sobbing quietly while I took a look at the cutter’s tractor release mechanism.

"Let's see here," I muttered, scanning the Trandoshan control panel. "Green button? No, that’s windshield wipers. Red lever? That dumps the bilge. Ah, here we go: EMERGENCY CLAMP DETONATION."

I hit the switch.

Outside, the explosive bolts along the Falcon’s dorsal hull fired with a synchronized CRACK-BOOM that felt like a sledgehammer hitting the bottom of my boots.

The Gizzard-Four broke free.

Instantly, the cutter's lighter frame was seized by the sulfur storm. Without the Falcon’s massive Corellian bulk anchoring it, the tug flipped end-over-end, its stabilizers tearing off like wet cardboard as it was sucked down into the boiling yellow depths of the gas giant’s core, vanishing into a flash of orange fire three miles below.

I leaped backward through the collapsing umbilical just as the Falcon's outer airlock slammed shut, tumbling back onto the iron grates of our main cargo hold.

Bee Moo was standing there.

The four-armed cybernetic foreman was no longer moving. He was wedged head-first into a narrow maintenance conduit, his metal legs sticking straight up in the air like rusted fence posts, completely stripped of his shoulder cannon, his sidearms, and his tool pouch.

Bee Moo was holding a heavy wrench in one hand and a handful of copper wiring in the other.

"Did you... did you stuff him into the trash chute?" I panted, lying flat on my back, chest heaving.

"He wouldn't shut up," Bee Moo said.

They tossed the wrench onto the foreman's lifeless chassis with a dull clink.

"Did you fix the coolant line?" Bee Moo asked.

"I was busy!" I wheezed. "I was busy fighting a four-armed toaster and detaching a three-ton parasite from our roof! Did you fix the coolant line?!"

"No," Bee Moo said, walking toward the cockpit ladder. "I'm not a mechanic."

"You're not a lot of things! You're not polite! You're not sanitary! You're not currently making my life easier!"

"Hyperdrive's green," Bee Moo called down from the upper ladder.

I froze.

I scrambled to my feet, nearly slipping on a Trandoshan blood trail, and hauled myself up the cockpit ladder three rungs at a time.

I slid into the pilot's chair.

The console was still smoking. The emergency patch on the canopy had a spiderweb crack running down the center. But the main drive indicator—the big, rectangular toggle that had been glowing an angry, mocking scarlet for two straight episodes—was pulsing with a steady, tranquil, beautiful blue.

"How?" I stared at the display. "The patch sleeve wasn't even glued down! The secondary valve was seized!"

Bee Moo pointed lazily toward the floorboards behind the throttle quadrant.

Stuck directly into the high-pressure coolant manifold, jammed between two cracked brass fittings with the subtlety of a railroad spike, was the broken, severed barrel of the Trandoshan's heavy scattergun. It had been hammered into the breach, wrapped in three layers of greasy asbestos tape, and secured with the foreman’s heavy-duty industrial belt buckle.

Coolant was still weeping from the edges in a thin, gray hiss. But the pressure needle was hovering just above the red line.

It was ugly. It was dangerous. It was an insult to nine different branches of mechanical engineering.

It was working.

"You jammed a shotgun into our primary reactor loop?" I whispered in awe.

Bee Moo spat another shred of kelp onto the console.

"It fit."

Bloop-screeech.

The console gave one final, dying whine as the sensor sweep cleared.

Through the viewport, the yellow clouds of Ooze-Nine’s gas giant were thinning out as our maneuvering thrusters finally gained purchase against the upper slipstream. Ahead of us lay the clean, cold, beautiful black of open vacuum.

No repo ships. No station security. Just the distant, glittering pinpricks of the Mid Rim trade routes waiting to be robbed, cheated, or blown up by somebody with worse credit than us.

I looked over at Bee Moo.

They were already reclined in the copilot’s seat, boots crossed on the instrument panel, counting through a stack of greasy Syndicate credits they’d pulled off a dying lizard, whistling a tune that sounded like an off-key commercial jingle for cheap liquor.

"You know," I said, resting my hands on the hyperdrive levers, "most people have a redemption arc. They go through a dark night of the soul, they confront their inner demons, they emerge from the swamp as a better, more complete version of themselves."

Bee Moo didn't stop counting.

"Thirty-two... thirty-four... thirty-six..."

"You went into a swamp, had a twenty-minute business transaction with a slug that doesn't own pants, and came out as an unlicensed loan shark who commits casual assault."

Bee Moo slid the credits into their jacket pocket, patted the bulge, and turned their head slowly toward me.

The grin wasn't warm. It was thin, crooked, and completely rotten.

"Pull the lever, Wade," Bee Moo said softly. "Before I find something else of yours I want to sell."

I stared at them for two seconds.

Then I shook my head, took a deep breath, and shoved both hyperdrive throttles all the way to the firewall.

The stars stretched into long, blinding lines of brilliant white.

The Falcon shuddered, groaned like an old dog having a bad dream, and leaped into hyperspace—leaking freon, covered in station grime, carrying two katanas, forty-two stolen credits, and one completely ruined soul into the deep black.

And behind us, Ooze-Nine faded into the dark, not caring in the slightest.
