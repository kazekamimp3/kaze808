Starkiller's fingers curled once beside his leg.

“You have a way off this world.”

“I have described a previous accomplishment.”

“You offered it as an answer.”

“It was relevant.”

The boarding plank lifted.

Not the submarine. Not the sea. One length of wood rose between the quay and the hull, tipping its shore end upward until the deck end scraped free.

The mechanic stepped back from its path. Dutch caught Brian by the back of his shirt and drew him clear of the hatch edge.

The plank turned across the gap. Hung there.

Starkiller hadn't taken a step.

“Relevant is not enough.”

The mechanic stood against the open hatch. Clifford had the doctor by one sleeve, keeping him away from the exposed edge.

Dr. Evil looked at the suspended wood.

At the armored man.

Then down at Clifford's hand on his sleeve.

“Thank you, Jack.”

“Stay there.”

“Very authoritative.”

Clifford let go as though the cloth had become something damp and fucking personal.

Dutch put Brian on his feet behind the hatch coaming.

“Stay out of the way.”

Brian straightened his shirt.

“I was out of the way.”

“Stay farther out of it.”

On the quay, Starkiller waited for the answer he'd tried to pull into existence.

Dr. Evil spoke carefully.

“There is no spacecraft aboard. No hidden second vessel. If you take this one, it will continue to be a submarine while its crew is extremely upset.”

The plank shifted slightly in the air.

“Can you build one?”

“I can ask what would be required.”

“That wasn't my question.”

“It's the answer I can actually deliver.”

The mechanic looked at the doctor.

This time, the sentence helped.

Dr. Evil continued.

“You want a working ship. Killing the people who might identify the first difficulty will not produce one.”

Starkiller looked at the mechanic.

The man held his gaze for a moment, then glanced at the plank.

“First difficulty is that.”

“The hull?”

“The thing you took away so nobody could move.”

The wood descended.

Its shore end landed first. The other end struck the deck edge and settled.

Nobody applauded the restoration of the fucking floor.

The mechanic looked from the plank to the armored man.

“Where are you trying to go?”

Starkiller reached beneath the edge of his armor and brought out the folded coastal chart.

Dutch recognized it.

The same torn paper. The same thing the stranger had taken after the gate came down.

Starkiller opened it against the stone beside a mooring post.

The mechanic squinted across the gap.

“Put it flat.”

Starkiller did.

The man crossed the replaced plank. Slowly. Stopped on the quay a little beyond its end, where he could examine the paper without putting himself between Starkiller and the ship.

“That's the coast.”

“I know what it is.”

“Then what are you asking it?”

The helmet angled toward him.

“I asked where vessels departed.”

Dutch's voice came across from the deck.

“You killed them for directions.”

Starkiller didn't look up.

“They blocked the gate.”

“And that made them disposable.”

The mechanic stopped with one finger above the paper. He had heard enough about the weighing station to understand which silence he'd just walked into.

Dr. Evil spoke from the deck.

“Does the chart help?”

“With this coast,” the mechanic said.

He took his hand away.

“I can tell you which way another port lies. I can't tell you whether it has what you're asking for. I can't turn this into a drawing of another world.”

Starkiller folded the lower edge back against the wind.

“I need a vessel that can leave.”

“And then?”

“I find a route I recognize.”

The mechanic looked up at the darkening sky.

“So leaving isn't the whole job.”

“It is the first.”

Dr. Evil nodded as though he'd chaired the discovery.

“A preliminary objective.”

Dutch looked at him.

The doctor continued more quietly.

“A very preliminary objective.”

Starkiller straightened.

“What do you require?”

The mechanic let out a breath.

“To start? Questions answered. About what you mean by a vessel. What you've actually used. What you expect it to do. Then people who can tell me whether any of it means something we can attempt.”

“You can attempt it.”

“I can listen.”

He didn't dress the sentence up. Didn't give the doctor a more impressive version to sell.

“You know things I don't,” he said. “That might be useful. Doesn't mean I know how to build them.”

Starkiller's fingers pressed the chart flat.

He could take the man. Take the ship. Make the rest of the crew watch until somebody supplied a better word than no.

The hull would still be sitting in the sea.

The mechanic would still be a man who serviced it.

Starkiller folded the chart.

“Then listen.”

The mechanic stepped back onto the plank. Crossed to his own vessel without turning his back until the gap was behind him.

Dr. Evil waited until the mechanic had stepped clear of the plank.

“If we discuss the problem, we discuss it ashore. With the people who choose to attend. You will not board this vessel to encourage a better answer.”

“Tomorrow,” Starkiller said.

“Tomorrow we can assess what we know.”

“Here.”

The doctor looked at the mechanic.

“Would you be willing to explain the limitations?”

The man stared at him.

He had not been asked a question by his employer in a tone that tentative for some time.

“I can explain this ship. I can't promise him another one.”

“Neither have I.”

Dutch looked at him.

Dr. Evil kept his eyes on the mechanic.

“Not now, anyway.”

Brian had worked his way around the hatch coaming. He could see the armored man again.

“You met Kaze,” he said.

Starkiller turned.

Dr. Evil's head moved very slowly toward Brian.

“The man with his name across him,” Brian continued. “You know who I mean.”

“Today,” Starkiller said.

Something bright and awful happened behind Brian's eyes.

“I have information about him.”

“Brian,” Dutch said.

“We have a shared professional history.”

Dr. Evil came closer.

“Brian.”

“He killed me.”

The mechanic looked down.

Then back at the perfectly alive little man beside his knee.

Brian pointed toward Starkiller as though the conversation had become a job interview.

“That isn't a metaphor. I was part of his group. There was an incident. I was substantially taller at the beginning of it.”

“There were several incidents,” Clifford said.

Brian looked up at him.

“I'm explaining mine.”

“You said killed. Chris shoved me off a cliff. Arthur helped make the rest of it miserable. I woke up unable to walk.”

Dr. Evil looked pained.

“Jack, perhaps we could—”

“Then they kept treating it like a joke. The neck. The marks. You see the marks? Those weren't part of growing up.”

He was speaking to Starkiller now.

The armored man looked at him. At the helmet under his arm. At Brian beside the hatch.

“I came for a ship.”

“And I'm telling you why we have a mutual interest,” Brian said.

“We don't.”

“You haven't heard the proposal.”

“I have heard enough.”

The reply cut through the opening Brian had been preparing.

For one dangerous instant, he looked ready to explain why the refusal was technically premature.

Dr. Evil bent and put a hand on his shoulder.

“Your advice at the meeting,” he said quietly.

Brian looked up.

“What?”

“About the man who can move the boat.”

Brian's mouth remained open.

The doctor waited.

Across the gap, Starkiller had not moved.

Brian closed his mouth.

“I remember.”

“Good. So do I.”

He left his hand there for a moment before straightening.

Real advice. Real danger. Real recognition, placed exactly where Brian would feel it.

The doctor wasn't improvising affection. He was getting better at the lock.

“Before you go,” Dr. Evil said, “a name.”

The armored man looked at him.

“Starkiller.”

“Doctor Evil.”

He waited through the small space in which lesser men occasionally smiled.

Starkiller didn't.

“And this organization?”

Brian lifted his chin.

“Tampon Pandemic Productions. I founded it.”

The mechanic looked at the strip of water between them as though he might get another chance to fall in.

Dr. Evil said, “I direct the operation.”

“Founded and named,” Brian added.

“Yes. Brian has provided the historical background.”

Starkiller looked from the little man to the doctor.

He had not mistaken the company name for a medical specialty. He had simply found no useful reason to discuss it.

Brian appeared to take the lack of ridicule as a successful presentation.

Starkiller looked back at the mechanic.

“Tomorrow.”

“A discussion,” the man said.

“Bring what you know.”

Then the armored man turned and walked along the quay.

He had no ship. He had found someone with experience of having one built, and a crewman unwilling to pretend that made a replacement real.

It was enough of a lead to leave the plank where he'd put it.

Nobody followed him.

Inland, Chris stopped in the road and pointed into the ditch.

“Daynah.”

Daynah turned.

“What?”

“Your dog's taking that.”

Mahnah had his teeth around a stone road marker. He had worked it loose from the soft ground and was backing away with the delighted commitment of somebody finding exactly the wrong stick.

Daynah looked at Chris.

Chris held out both hands.

“I'm telling you. That's what you wanted.”

“You could also tell him to leave it.”

“I ain't his father.”

“You're half the fucking problem.”

“Then I'm reporting from inside the organization.”

Kaze turned back to watch.

Arthur kept going for three more steps, sighed and came back.

Daynah put a hand against Mahnah's shoulder. Gave the familiar release cue.

The dog set the stone down, then looked at Chris.

Chris looked away with the profound concentration of a man refusing to be implicated by a fucking witness.

“No,” Daynah told the dog. “You look at me.”

Mahnah did.

Daynah scratched his chest. Let him have the contact. Then turned him back toward the road.

“Go on.”

Kaze watched Daynah lift the loosened marker and bed it back in its hole.

“You know he was just improving the route.”

“Keep talking. I'll mark it with you.”

Arthur glanced at the fading light.

“At least we'd know where the trouble starts.”

Chris laughed.

Billy's voice came through his headgear.

“Christopher. You have done something commendable.”

Chris narrowed his eyes.

“Don't make it weird.”

“I shall try to contain my surprise.”

They went on while there was still enough light to choose their footing. When they finally stopped beyond the next rise, it was their own choice of ground. No promised shelter. No friendly foreman to carry a message.

On the submarine, Dutch waited until the stranger had passed beyond the nearest harbor shed.

Then turned to Dr. Evil.

“You will not offer him our people.”

The doctor looked offended.

“I asked the mechanic.”

“Keep asking.”

“I had noticed that it worked.”

Dutch studied him.

“You offered a spaceship because he stopped looking at you.”

“I mentioned an accomplishment.”

“He moved the walkway.”

“I was present.”

“Then remember what that felt like before you mention another.”

Dr. Evil's face tightened.

Clifford stood beside the hatch with the helmet against his hip. He didn't step between them. Neither man needed an audience to make the disagreement more expensive.

The doctor looked toward the sheds.

“A discussion ashore,” he said. “No guarantee. No invitation aboard.”

Dutch held his gaze.

Then nodded once.

“That is what was said.”

He went below.

Clifford remained.

Dr. Evil turned toward him.

“Your handling of the situation was very composed.”

“He took my helmet.”

“And you retrieved it.”

Clifford waited.

“With dignity,” the doctor added.

“I would have preferred to keep it.”

“Naturally.”

The doctor glanced at the black shape.

“Perhaps you could leave it off tomorrow.”

Clifford looked at him.

There. A practical recommendation. A little dignity returned. For a moment, he thought the conversation might escape alive.

“The resemblance seems to upset him,” Dr. Evil continued. “And I would hate for anyone to mistake our intentions.”

“I'll decide what I wear.”

“Of course, Jack.”

Clifford went below with his helmet.

The mechanic was still beside the hatch.

Dr. Evil looked at him.

“I suppose you have an observation.”

“Several.”

“One at a time.”

“Don't tell him I can build a spaceship.”

“I didn't.”

The mechanic waited.

The doctor looked toward the quay.

“I may have allowed an optimistic interpretation.”

“He pulled a walkway out from under us.”

“Nothing was under it at the time.”

“I was standing beside it.”

Dr. Evil's mouth tightened.

“Very well.”

The man continued.

“If I go tomorrow, I explain what I know. If I don't know something, I say so. Nobody fills the silence by offering a moon.”

“It was a moon base.”

“Is it here?”

“No.”

“Then please leave it where it is.”

Brian watched from the other side of the hatch. The mechanic had no little chair. No résumé in his shirt. He was telling the doctor something unwelcome and waiting to see whether it would survive the conversation.

Dr. Evil studied him.

“Would you prefer not to attend?”

“I'd prefer he hadn't come. Since he has, I'd rather tell him the truth while he still thinks it might be useful.”

“That is an extremely depressing recruitment policy.”

“I'm already employed.”

“I had noticed.”

The doctor glanced at the narrow boarding plank.

“We speak ashore. You explain this vessel. You make no promise about another one.”

“And you?”

Dr. Evil looked back.

“I will attempt to resist expanding the product line during questions.”

The mechanic considered him for a moment.

“That's what I need.”

“Apparently everyone has needs.”

“Yes.”

“Nobody mentioned this when I acquired a submarine.”

The man went below before the doctor could improve the arrangement.

Brian remained by the hatch, looking toward the quay.

Dr. Evil had almost followed the others when he noticed the little man hadn't moved.

“Brian.”

Brian looked up.

“Do you need something?”

The question landed somewhere the rest of the evening hadn't.

He took a moment before answering.

“No. I'm good.”

“Then come inside.”

Brian followed him down.

Later, in his own chair on its wooden riser, Brian opened the journal.

TRANSPORT CONSULTATION... THE INFAMOUS B.MOO....

He wrote three lines about strategic opportunities. Crossed one out.

Wrote KAZE in the margin. Pressed hard enough to mark the next page.

Then, beneath it:

My advice remembered... not just heard. Remembered....

He sat looking at the sentence.

Above him, the ordinary adult table stayed exactly where it had always fucking been.
