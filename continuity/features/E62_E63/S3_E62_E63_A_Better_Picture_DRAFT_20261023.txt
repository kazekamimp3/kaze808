A Better Picture

Brian had organized his stolen research into three piles by breakfast.

Dutch. Jack. People Who Did Not Appreciate Useful Information.

The third pile was mostly his own notes.

He sat on his bunk with the journal across his knees, practicing a sentence under his breath.

“I have faith in your judgment.”

He tried it again with a little less obvious thirst for a reward.

Shake looked over from the chair he'd occupied since the previous evening.

“Who are you saying that to?”

“Nobody.”

“Good. Sounds like you're about to ask for money.”

Brian shut the journal.

“It's called communication.”

“Then go do it somewhere. You're communicating all over my breakfast.”

The food was shipboard bread and cold meat. Shake had complained about it twice and eaten all of his portion.

Brian gathered the Dutch pages, leaving his rap journal beneath the pillow. Then he reconsidered, retrieved it, and took both.

He was not going to be caught without evidence of himself.

On deck, Dutch studied the inlet with Sella and the ship's engineer. Clifford checked the rope in the small launch tied alongside. His lightsaber hung at his belt, unlit. Dr. Evil stood under the shelter of the hatch, holding a cup and radiating the expectation that other people would supply progress.

The target lay across the water behind a timber tide gate. Three long sheds faced a narrow service basin. A separate stone storehouse stood higher on the shore, connected by a raised walkway.

“Four instrument cases,” Sella said. “Three in the waterside shed. The fourth's locked separately. Small one, polished brass fittings.”

“Why separate?” Dutch asked.

“More expensive.”

“People do make it easy to know where to look.”

The engineer pointed out the launch's throttle and reverse position to Dutch. Dutch tested them with the engine idling, listening to the change in pitch. He didn't pretend the controls were identical to every boat he'd handled before.

Brian watched from above.

A watch bell sounded across the basin. One guard left the raised walkway. Another came out of the upper storehouse to replace him.

The two stopped to speak.

“There,” Brian said.

Dutch looked up.

“What?”

“That's the handover. They both face the upper door when they talk. The bend below the walkway is out of their sight.”

Sella checked the angle.

“Only for a minute.”

“Then we use a minute.”

Dutch looked at the open water between the submarine and the gate.

“A minute won't get us all the way across.”

“We don't start here.” Brian pointed at the low sea wall halfway along the basin. “We wait behind that. We can see the bell rope from the corner.”

Dutch studied him.

Brian remembered the page about making a practical objection support the other man's purpose.

“Your call on when to move,” he added. “You'd have the angle. Not somebody on this deck telling you what you should be seeing.”

Dutch's face eased.

“Now that,” he said, “is an observation.”

Brian nodded as though the entire exchange had been a controlled experiment.

It had been partly a useful observation, partly a good read of Dutch, and partly a man discovering flattery after inventing a special name for it.

Still worked.

Dr. Evil lowered his cup.

“Take him.”

Brian looked at him.

“On the launch?”

“Unless you'd prefer to advise us from inside a luggage compartment.”

Brian immediately started down the ladder.

The step spacing was designed for taller adults. He turned and climbed backward, keeping three points of contact until Clifford reached up and steadied the case Brian had insisted on carrying.

“What is it?” Clifford asked.

“Canvas. Extra cord. Things we need.”

Clifford opened it, found exactly those things, and nodded.

Brian noticed the nod.

He stored it away with the same care he gave the papers.

Sella stayed aboard the submarine to wait for delivery. Dr. Evil watched the launch cast off.

Before they cleared the hull, Clifford checked the covered cargo space and shifted the empty canvas case forward.

Brian moved it back.

“I put that there.”

“It blocks the step.”

“It's organized.”

Clifford put one boot on the step and showed him exactly where a person trying to get out would land.

Brian looked at the boot, the case, and the narrow space between them.

He moved it forward again.

“Temporary arrangement.”

“Everything on a boat is temporary if you leave it in the wrong place.”

Brian almost wrote that down. Then he noticed Clifford watching and pretended to check the cord instead.

Dutch let the exchange finish. He waited until both men were seated before advancing the throttle.

For all the costumes, resentment, and ridiculous names, they could do a small thing properly when they agreed what the thing was.

Dr. Evil leaned over the rail and called after Dutch.

“Remember, this is an acquisition. Not an island proposal.”

Dutch's smile tightened.

“I know what we're here for.”

“Excellent. I also have a plan.”

Clifford looked out over the bow. Dutch concentrated on the engine. Brian looked between them, recognized the phrase from his notes, and tried not to seem pleased that he knew something.

The launch moved along the sea wall and settled behind its projecting corner. Dutch cut the engine. Water slapped softly against the hull.

Across the basin, the watchman resumed his patrol.

They waited.

Brian began to whisper a suggestion.

Clifford put one finger to his lips.

Brian stopped. A full minute passed in which his expertise survived being unspoken.

The bell sounded again when a supply handcart reached the upper door. The walkway guard turned to answer the porter. Dutch started the engine and took the launch out from behind the wall.

Clifford kept low in the bow. Brian crouched behind the forward bench beneath the doubled canvas he had insisted on bringing. The three men crossed the exposed gap before the guard finished with the porter.

At the tide gate, Dutch brought the bow gently against the timber.

Clifford stepped onto the narrow maintenance ledge and examined the chain securing it.

The saber ignited with a hard red glare.

He held it away from the hull and brought the blade through one exposed link. Metal spat into the water. The chain separated, and the gate swung inward under the launch's slow pressure.

Clifford held the blade still while the boat slipped past him. Only after the gunwale cleared did he step aboard again, keeping the burning edge well away from the wood.

Dutch guided them through.

A guard came out of the lower shed as the gate knocked against its stop. He saw the open chain, reached for the pistol at his belt, and began to shout.

Dutch shot him once from the helm.

The guard fell against the shed wall and slid down, leaving his warning unfinished.

Clifford jumped onto the landing, extinguished the saber, and caught the second guard by the coat as the man came through the door. They struck the jamb together. Clifford drove the guard's wrist into the wood until the knife dropped, then knocked him senseless with the heavy hilt.

Brian climbed onto the landing after them, short legs scrambling over the gap.

“Stay with the boat,” Dutch told him.

Brian almost argued.

Then he saw the first guard's blood dripping through the boards.

“Right. Transport control.”

He took the rope and secured the launch to the cleat.

Inside the shed, Dutch found the three large cases beneath oilcloth. Each carried the maker's mark Sella had described. He opened one far enough to see the wrapped brass instruments and shut it again.

“Jack.”

Clifford joined him. They carried the cases out one at a time, lowering them into the launch instead of throwing them. Brian pulled the doubled canvas over the load and tied it down to keep the corners from flashing in the light.

The third case caught on the gunwale.

Brian saw the cord trapped beneath it.

“Lift your end. Just a little.”

Dutch did. Brian freed the cord and guided the case flat.

“Good,” Dutch said.

That word landed harder than any insult had that morning.

A man in a blue working coat appeared from the side door carrying a hooked boat pole. He stopped when he saw the bodies.

Clifford pointed his revolver at him.

“Put it down.”

The man lowered the pole.

Dutch took him by the sleeve and pulled him toward the landing.

“Small brass case. Where?”

“Upper store.”

“Key?”

“Office. I don't have it.”

From above came a shout. The walkway guard had seen them.

A shot punched through the shed roof.

Dutch looked toward the upper storehouse, then at the three cases already in the launch.

“We leave with what we've got.”

It was the sensible choice. He made it cleanly.

Clifford fired once toward the walkway rail. The guard ducked. Dutch forced the blue-coated man into the launch and stepped down after him.

“Name?”

“Merrit.”

“You know the basin?”

“I work here.”

“Then you're coming.”

Merrit looked back at the pole he had dropped. His lunch hung from its hook in a cloth bundle. He left it where it was.

Brian untied the rope. Dutch reversed away from the landing, turned, and opened the throttle toward the broken tide gate.

The launch surged forward.

Behind them, a second alarm bell began ringing at the shore watch. Men appeared beside a larger harbor boat beyond the gate. One ran toward its engine housing while another dragged a line off a bollard.

“Right after the wall,” Merrit said. “Main basin. Gives you room.”

Dutch looked right. The harbor boat was already swinging out.

“They'll cut us off.”

“Then slow and use the inside turn.”

A bullet struck the launch's stern rail.

Brian dropped flat behind the cases.

Dutch saw a narrow gap beneath an old loading gantry on the left. Beyond it lay an open channel leading toward the submarine's berth.

“We fit there.”

Merrit turned sharply.

“Not at this tide.”

“We fit.”

Dutch turned the wheel.

Clifford saw the low crossbeam coming toward them and grabbed the gunwale.

“Dutch!”

The water had lifted the launch higher than the stain on the gantry posts suggested. Its bow passed under. The canopy frame did not.

Wood hit metal with a crack that flattened every other sound.

The frame tore backward. Dutch's forehead struck the bronze mounting bracket as the helm jerked beneath his hands. His head snapped sideways. His knees went out.

The launch slewed into a gantry post, splitting planks along the forward side.

Water came in hard.

Brian's shoulder hit a case. His journal stayed inside his coat, but the loose research sheets burst from the fold and scattered across the wet deck.

“Fuck! Fuck!”

Clifford caught Dutch before he went over the side. His awkward neck prevented a clean shoulder carry, so he dragged him backward by both armpits, bracing one boot against the bench.

“Engine!” he shouted.

Merrit reached the throttle and cut it. Then he caught Brian by the back of his coat as the tiny man tried to reach a page floating toward the split planks.

“Leave the paper!”

“That's important!”

“So's breathing!”

A shot struck the gantry upright above them. Splinters showered the launch.

Clifford laid Dutch against the bench, drew his revolver, and fired his second covering shot toward the men at the harbor boat. They dropped behind its rail.

The submarine lay less than a hundred yards down the open channel. Its deck crew had seen the collision. Two men were already putting their small service boat into the water.

Clifford shouted and waved with his free arm.

Brian clung to the canvas covering the cases. The launch dipped farther at the bow.

“Untie the load,” Merrit said.

“We just stole it!”

“Untie it so it doesn't pull everything down!”

Brian worked the knots with shaking fingers. The wet cord resisted. He found the loose end, dragged it through, and freed the cases from the hull.

Merrit shoved the nearest wooden case toward the open side as the service boat approached. It floated briefly in its oilcloth cover, long enough for a crewman to hook and haul it alongside.

The next two went after it.

Clifford passed Dutch across with the help of the second crewman, keeping his head from striking the new boat's gunwale. Merrit climbed over. Brian had to jump from the sinking bench into a man's waiting hands.

He landed against a hard chest, furious, frightened, and very much alive.

Clifford came last. He pulled himself into the service boat as water reached the launch's helm.

They left the wreck jammed against the gantry post.

The harbor boat could not follow through that gap. It had to go around the wall, buying the service crew enough time to reach the submarine under the cover of its hull and the busy outer berth.

Nobody had won a naval battle. They had stolen three cases, lost a boat, kidnapped a guide, and rescued each other before the consequences finished arriving.

Dutch opened his eyes on the submarine deck.

Clifford knelt beside him.

“Stay down.”

Dutch blinked at the sky.

“What happened?”

“You hit the beam.”

“I saw the beam.”

“Then you introduced yourself.”

Dutch tried to sit. Clifford caught his shoulder and held him where he was while the ship's medic came over.

Blood ran from the cut near Dutch's hairline. It crossed the edge of the forehead mango and followed one of the tattooed trees down toward his left cheek.

Brian squeezed between two crewmen, clutching the pages he'd managed to save.

He looked at Dutch.

“Oh, no.”

Clifford turned.

“Give him room.”

Brian leaned closer.

“It's running over Bob.”

The medic looked at him.

“What?”

Brian pointed at the left cheek.

“The portrait. Don't smear that.”

Clifford stared.

“He's bleeding from his head.”

“I can see that. I'm saying wipe around the face.”

Dutch's eyes moved toward Brian.

“Bob?”

“It's fine,” Brian said quickly. “I think it's fine. The blood makes the hair look bigger.”

The medic pushed Brian back by the shoulder and cleaned the wound.

Brian tried to peer around his arm.

“Careful on the left.”

Clifford stood.

Brian looked up at him.

“What?”

“If you say one more thing about the tattoo while he's lying here, I'm putting you in the wash bucket.”

Brian closed his mouth.

For six seconds.

“It's a good likeness,” he said quietly.

Clifford picked him up under the arms and moved him bodily to the far side of the hatch.

Brian's shoes continued walking for a moment after they had left the deck.

“I was being supportive.”

“Support that wall.”

Clifford put him down and returned to Dutch.

Dr. Evil inspected the recovered cases with Sella. The doubled canvas and oilcloth had protected the instruments; water beaded on the packing rather than filling the compartments. One corner was crushed, but the brass inside remained intact.

“Three,” Sella said.

“I can count,” Dr. Evil replied.

“Then you know why I haven't paid the balance.”

He closed the lid with unusual care.

“The fourth will follow.”

“And the launch?”

He looked toward the gantry.

The broken canopy frame had stopped moving above the water.

“Has chosen a permanent berth.”

Sella did not laugh.

“You replace it.”

“It was an old boat.”

“It was a floating boat.”

They settled on a thirty-silver deduction from the final payment for the ruined borrowed launch. The recovered cases remained secured aboard, awaiting the fourth. No balance changed hands yet.

Brian watched the negotiation from beside the hatch.

The doctor turned toward him.

“The cover was useful.”

Brian looked up.

“I said double it.”

“You did.”

“And the handover timing. We got in because of that.”

“Also correct.”

Brian held the damp papers tighter.

The doctor didn't add an insult. He went to see Dutch.

That absence worked on Brian longer than a compliment would have.

Merrit stood near the deck rail between two crewmen. Nobody had tied him yet, but neither man moved aside when he shifted toward the gangplank.

Brian approached with a dry towel folded over his arms.

“You pulled me out.”

Merrit looked down.

“You were reaching into a sinking boat.”

“For documents.”

“They don't breathe either.”

Brian held up the towel. Merrit took it and wiped water from his hair.

“Thank you,” Brian said.

The words came without a speech attached. Merrit nodded.

Then Brian glanced at the crewmen.

“He's probably fine.”

One of them looked toward the hatch.

“Doctor said wait.”

Brian stayed beside Merrit until Dr. Evil returned.

“We should let him go,” Brian said.

The doctor looked at the wet man, then at the stolen cases.

“Does he know where the fourth case is?”

Merrit said nothing.

“He does,” Brian admitted.

“Then we have discovered why he is still here.”

Merrit gripped the towel.

“I helped your men.”

“I appreciate initiative.”

“I want to go home.”

Dr. Evil nodded sympathetically enough that Brian briefly thought the exchange was going somewhere decent.

“And I want the complete set.”

The doctor took Sella's chart from a crewman and spread it on a dry hatch cover.

“Show us how to reach the upper store without driving through architecture.”

Merrit looked at Brian.

Brian lowered his eyes to the chart.

There was the boundary of his gratitude. He had found the courage to thank the man. He had not found the courage to insist.

Merrit pointed with one wet finger.

“There's a maintenance path from the old slip. At low water, you can walk along the foot of the wall. The stair comes up behind the stone store.”

“Guards?”

“One at the store. The tower watches the outer approach. By now, more.”

Dr. Evil nodded.

“Excellent. A scenic route.”

“I haven't said I'll take you.”

“No. You've described the route you'll be taking us on.”

He asked the crew to put Merrit in the spare utility compartment with water, food, and a man outside the door. The room had a narrow bunk and a vent too small for a grown man. The crewman at its door kept the key.

Merrit walked there because the men beside him were armed.

Brian watched him go, then looked down at the chart.

“He saved the cases too.”

“Then we must make sure his effort wasn't wasted,” Dr. Evil said.

It was a vile answer, delivered in the tone of a father encouraging a child to finish a worthwhile project.

Brian folded the wet towel Merrit had left behind.

He didn't follow him.

Before sitting with Dutch, Clifford went ashore to the patch of dry ground beyond the ropewalk. His coat was still wet. He carried it over one arm, leaving the saber clipped visibly at his belt.

Billy had settled beside the embankment with Mahnah asleep nearby. The enormous dog lifted his head when Clifford approached, then lowered it again at Daynah's touch.

“I can't do the fishing today,” Clifford said.

Chris answered through Billy's banjo.

“What happened to you?”

“The boat broke.”

“That's generally something boats do to people after a decision.”

Clifford looked at the ground.

“Dutch nearly drowned.”

The strings were quiet for a moment.

“You get him out?” Chris asked.

“Yes.”

“All right.”

“I'll come tomorrow. If that's still all right.”

“Tomorrow works.”

Billy shifted his banjo against his chest.

“Your river's still there, little man.”

Clifford nodded.

“I didn't want you waiting on me.”

“We ain't going nowhere this afternoon,” Chris said. “Come when you can.”

Clifford looked at Billy, then down at his wet coat. He squeezed water from one corner before he remembered he had already said everything he needed to say.

Daynah offered him a dry cloth. Clifford took it, rubbed the worst of the wet from his hair, and handed it back.

“Keep it,” Daynah said.

Clifford folded it into his coat. Then he walked back along the quay to the submarine. He still had a man to check on before he could think about spending an afternoon beside water that couldn't drown anyone.

Below deck, Dutch lay on a narrow cot with his wound dressed. The medic had told him to rest and report if he felt worse. The folded cloth beside his pillow already had a little blood on it. When footsteps passed in the corridor, he flinched at their first hard strike.

Dutch stared at the ceiling.

Clifford sat beside the door, his wet coat folded over one knee.

Dutch shifted against the pillow and looked at him properly for the first time since coming below.

“You got me out.”

Clifford nodded.

“Yes.”

“Could've taken the boatman and left me with the load.”

“Could've done a lot of things.”

Dutch looked at the damp cuffs of Clifford's shirt.

“Thank you, son.”

Clifford's jaw tightened at the last word, but he didn't reject the thanks.

“You need to listen when somebody tells you the boat won't fit.”

Dutch turned his eyes toward the wall.

“I thought we had room.”

“You didn't.”

Silence settled between them without anybody rushing to disguise it.

Dutch touched the edge of the bandage, felt the tenderness beneath it, and left it alone.

“It was meant to be a clean exit.”

Clifford looked at his own hands. They had stopped shaking after the rescue, though dried blood remained in the creases around his nails.

“Then let's take the next one while we've still got it.”

Dutch kept looking at the wall for several breaths.

“We should leave this business,” Dutch said.

Clifford nodded.

“Get the cases off the ship. Get paid for what they'll pay for. Move on.”

“That's what I mean.”

For a moment they agreed without anybody working to make it happen.

Then Brian appeared in the doorway.

He had dried his research pages between two towels. The top sheet still carried a brown tide mark.

“I understand why you're frustrated,” he began.

Clifford closed his eyes.

Dutch looked at Brian.

“Do you?”

“You made the right call taking the three cases. Leadership isn't getting everything. It's knowing what you can carry forward.”

Dutch studied him.

That part was true. It was also almost directly assembled from the page Brian held just out of view.

“Then you understand why we're done,” Dutch said.

Brian hesitated.

“Well, the doctor still needs the fourth.”

“The doctor can ask the doctor to fetch it.”

Clifford opened his eyes again.

Brian had not found that answer in the notes.

He tried another approach.

“People will think they drove you out.”

Dutch's gaze changed.

Clifford looked sharply at Brian.

“Don't.”

“I'm not saying I think that.”

Dutch pushed himself up on one elbow.

“Who?”

“Nobody specific. The people over there.”

Dutch looked past him toward the corridor.

“They don't decide where I go.”

“Exactly,” Brian said, relieved.

Clifford stood.

“You just said we should leave.”

“We should. When I say we should.”

He lay back, breathing through his nose, and touched the clean edge of his left cheek with two fingers.

“Bob always understood the difference.”

Brian glanced at the portrait.

This time, he managed not to discuss the workmanship.

Dutch painted the harbor green before breakfast.

He had taken a chart from the navigation table, asked a crewman for the paint used to mark the ship's equipment, and ignored the brush the man offered him. It was too coarse. Dutch found a smaller one in the same maintenance box and carried everything to the mess table himself.

By the time Brian came down, the eastern seawall had become a pleasant beach.

The paint smelled appalling in the enclosed compartment.

“That window doesn't open,” Brian said.

Dutch added a yellow curve to the beach.

“It should.”

Brian climbed onto a chair. Dutch's bandage was clean, though the swelling beneath it had not disappeared overnight. He held his head carefully when he reached across the paper.

“What are you doing?”

“Getting some perspective.”

Yesterday's stolen cases sat in the cargo compartment. Yesterday's borrowed launch remained broken against a gantry. Yesterday's sensible decision had apparently been left in it.

Dutch dabbed a little white along the painted shore.

“There.”

Brian looked from the chart to the open tin.

“Is that supposed to be surf?”

“What does it look like?”

“Surf.”

“Then why ask?”

Because the surf was covering the only clear depth markings, Brian. But don't let me interfere with a man creating a postcard for his own impending insurance claim.

Dr. Evil entered behind him and stopped at the smell.

“Oh, good. Breakfast has a finish.”

Dutch lifted the chart.

“We've been allowing other people to describe the situation.”

“That is usually the point of a map.”

“They describe limitations. I see possibilities.”

Dr. Evil looked at the painted green patch where the mud shoal had been.

“I see an island where a boat became a tax deduction.”

Dutch set the chart down.

“Bob understood that an ugly thing doesn't have to stay ugly.”

He touched his left cheek. The little portrait there stared into the mess compartment with all the navigational authority of a commemorative plate.

Brian slid a page from his pocket beneath the table. The heading about Dutch's loyalty still showed a tide mark. He read the first line, put it away, and smiled.

“What matters is that we're following your plan.”

Dutch looked at him.

“Why?”

Brian blinked.

“Well. Because you're the leader.”

“Is that a reason to follow a bad plan?”

“No.”

“Then tell me why it's good.”

Brian had prepared for the man who wanted faith. He had not prepared for the same man wanting a review of the faith application.

“The access route is good,” he said at last.

Dutch nodded toward the chart.

“Show me.”

Brian's finger hovered above wet green paint.

He withdrew it.

“We should get Merrit.”

Merrit arrived between two crewmen with half a piece of bread in his hand. He had slept in the utility compartment with someone outside the door. He took in Dutch, the chart, the paint, and the armed men before answering any question.

Dutch turned the picture toward him.

“Well?”

Merrit looked at Brian. Brian gave him no help.

“It's very fine.”

“Where does it put you in mind of?”

“A warm place.”

“Tahiti,” Dutch said.

“Yes. Just like it.”

“You've been?”

Merrit's mouth stayed open a fraction too long.

“No. But I imagine—”

“Exactly.”

Dutch tapped the table, pleased.

“You can see it without having been there.”

Merrit nodded. The bread had compressed into a damp lump in his fist.

Dutch pointed to the painted beach.

“We come in here.”

“No,” Merrit said before remembering his audience. “I mean, that's lovely in the picture. On the ground it's mud.”

“It won't be mud forever.”

“It'll be mud this morning.”

Dutch's expression hardened.

Brian opened his mouth with another sentence about leadership, then shut it. Dr. Evil had put a hand on the back of his chair.

“Let him describe the route,” the doctor said. “He has the unfair advantage of having walked it.”

Merrit pointed at an unpainted corner. The old slip ran behind the upper store. At low water there would be a narrow ledge beneath the retaining wall. Two men could walk it side by side. The fourth instrument case could come back the same way if they carried it lengthwise.

A watchman stood at the stair above the slip. Another worked the signal tower on the outer pier. From there, a flag or a bell could call the harbor patrol before anyone reached the service boat.

Dutch tilted the chart.

“That tower ruins it.”

“The approach?” Brian asked.

“The whole balance.”

He moved the brush through the sky and stopped at the little black mark that represented the tower.

“You've got all this room to breathe, and somebody sticks a damned finger through the middle.”

Merrit said nothing.

Dutch looked up.

“Well?”

“It would look better without it.”

There. A man trying to get out of a room had just volunteered a piece of municipal architecture for demolition.

Clifford came in carrying his dry coat. He looked at the painting, then at Dutch.

“I thought we were leaving.”

“We are. After we finish.”

“Yesterday you said finishing meant leaving.”

“And now I'm saying leaving means finishing.”

Clifford stared at him.

Dr. Evil took the paint tin away before the argument could spread to a second coastline.

“We get the fourth case. We do not replace the launch with another launch. We do not discover a new interpretation of how wide a boat is.”

Dutch reached for the tin. Dr. Evil moved it farther away.

“The service boat stays outside the wall. We go overland from the old slip. Merrit takes us in. Clifford covers the stair. Brian stays at the approach and watches the signal tower.”

Brian looked up.

“Me?”

“You noticed the handover yesterday.”

“That's different.”

“Yes. It was yesterday.”

Dr. Evil put the chart flat and asked Merrit to draw the route on its dry reverse. Dutch objected that it would show through. The doctor handed Merrit a separate piece of packing paper instead.

The resulting map had no beaches. It did have a way out.

Brian followed Dr. Evil into the passage as the others collected their equipment.

“The notes said he'd respond to loyalty.”

“People do.”

“He didn't.”

“He responded. You disliked the response.”

Brian stopped at the ladder.

“I thought I understood him.”

Dr. Evil came back down one step so he didn't have to speak over his shoulder.

“You understood a pattern. That is useful. It is not a remote control.”

Brian looked away.

“Everybody else seems to know when I'm about to be wrong.”

“Everybody else has the advantage of watching you announce that you cannot be.”

That one hurt. Dr. Evil let it sit, then continued without turning it into a performance for the crew.

“Yesterday you saw a man leave his post. You saw water getting into equipment. You made two correct observations. Start with what is there.”

Brian looked back at him.

“What if I don't see anything?”

“Then say so before we walk into it.”

The doctor held out a small pair of field glasses from the ship's navigation locker.

“Use these. Return them.”

Brian accepted them with both hands.

There was no punch line waiting behind the gift. Dr. Evil went up the ladder and left him with something to do.

Outside, the morning smelled of low tide and breakfast fires. The submarine still occupied its berth beside the export depot. Across the quay, men were arguing about yesterday's collision and the guards who had failed to recover three missing cases.

They had added a second patrol at the upper store.

Merrit saw it from the old slip and stopped.

“That's new.”

Dutch unfolded his painting.

“Where?”

“On the ground. There.”

Two men stood beside the store's lower door. One carried a long gun. The other held a cup and kept looking toward the harbor rather than the stair. Above them, the signal tower rose on four timber legs, its outside braces anchored to iron eyes along the pier.

Clifford crouched beside a stack of broken baskets. His saber hung at his belt, unlit. The revolver was in his hand.

“Can't talk our way through that.”

Dr. Evil looked at Merrit.

“Can you?”

“They know I went missing yesterday.”

“Could you become unmissing convincingly?”

“With you standing behind me?”

“A fair production note.”

Dutch folded the chart again and tucked it inside his coat.

“I can draw them away.”

Clifford turned.

“How?”

“By giving them something worth looking at.”

“You're already doing that.”

Dutch ignored him. He gave Merrit his hat, pushed the man's shoulder toward the bend in the ledge, and told him to call for help.

Merrit took one step and stopped.

“You'll shoot me if I run.”

Dr. Evil answered before Dutch could.

“If you do this and the case comes out, you walk away.”

Merrit searched his face.

“Without me coming along again?”

“Your consultancy will have concluded.”

“In words.”

“Yes. You leave.”

Merrit gave the hat back.

“They know my head.”

He went around the bend with his empty hands raised and shouted that the robbers were on the slip.

The man with the cup dropped it. Both guards moved toward the stair. Clifford waited until the rifle barrel appeared above the wall, then stepped close beneath it and struck upward with his own gun hand.

The first shot went into the sky.

Dutch fired past Clifford's shoulder. The rifleman fell against the rail and folded onto the steps. Clifford hit the second guard behind the ear with the revolver butt and caught his coat before he could tumble down onto Merrit.

The man sagged, breathing. Clifford lowered him against the wall.

So much for a silent theft. They had exchanged the complicated kind of alarm for the traditional gun-shaped one.

Dr. Evil caught Merrit by the sleeve and pointed him toward the store.

“Case.”

Merrit took a key ring from the unconscious guard's belt and led him through the open lower door. Dutch stayed at the stair. Clifford turned toward the pier.

Brian watched the tower through the field glasses from behind the baskets. He had to set one elbow on a broken crate to hold the view steady.

The signalman was looking toward the shot. He reached for a red flag.

“Tower,” Brian called.

Clifford saw him a moment later.

The flag went up.

Across the basin, a harbor boat began to pull away from its landing.

Brian lowered the glasses. There was a bell rope running outside the tower, down through a guide on the rail. He pointed.

“The rope. He can ring it from above.”

Clifford ran along the inside edge of the pier. The signalman drew a pistol, fired, and put a splinter through the rail beside his hand.

Clifford dropped behind a stack of net floats.

Dutch fired once toward the top of the tower. The signalman ducked behind its wooden parapet.

“Take it down!” Dutch shouted.

Clifford looked back.

“The bell?”

“The damned tower!”

Dutch pulled out the painting with his free hand, as if the timber structure might recognize the authority of its deletion.

“It was wrong from the beginning!”

Clifford had an armed man above him, a boat approaching across the basin, and enough exposed bell rope within reach to stop one of those problems. He ignited the saber low beside the floats, cut the rope, and kept the blade between his body and the rail as he moved.

The red flag remained up. Beneath the tower, the near timber foot had split around its bolts; water had worked into the exposed end until the wood looked almost black.

Dutch pointed at the nearest diagonal brace.

“Cut that. Then the other.”

Clifford glanced at the tower's feet. They stood on a separate pier spur. The braces ran down toward the approach used by the patrol boat. If the structure fell inward, it would crush the path back to Merrit.

“Move off the stair!” he shouted.

Dutch stayed where he was.

“Dutch!”

That name, without anything wrapped around it, finally got him moving.

Clifford sliced through the exposed iron eye holding the first brace. The released timber snapped sideways and struck the rail. A crack opened at the tower's near foot. The structure leaned, stopped, and leaned farther as the signalman scrambled toward its ladder.

Clifford backed away instead of waiting beneath it to admire the weapon.

The tower fell toward the basin.

Its upper platform smashed across the narrow entrance between the pier and the seawall. The red flag disappeared under a fan of dirty water. The signalman jumped from the ladder before it struck, caught a crosspiece, and clung there with both arms.

The patrol boat stopped short of the floating wreckage. Men on its bow shouted for him to let go and swim clear. One threw a line.

Dutch stood on the cleared stair, staring at the sudden gap in the skyline.

“There,” he said.

Clifford shut off the saber.

“You nearly stayed under it.”

“Look how much better that is.”

The obstruction had bought them a way out. The man who wanted to improve a painting had accidentally improved the getaway. Somewhere, a sensible idea just put its head in its hands.

Inside the store, Merrit unlocked a barred cage with the third key he tried. Dr. Evil checked the small case's polished fittings against Sella's description before lifting his end.

They emerged carrying the fourth case between them. It was longer than Brian was tall. They turned it lengthwise and hurried down the ledge while Clifford covered the rear with his revolver.

Brian grabbed the field glasses before they slid off the crate. He followed, slipping once on the wet stone, catching himself on the wall, and continuing without announcing that the route had always been part of his plan.

Dutch came last, moving less steadily than he had before the firing started. He pressed his fingers against the side of his head and lowered them when Clifford looked back.

The service boat waited outside the wall. Its crew took the case first, then the passengers. Merrit stayed on the ledge.

Dr. Evil looked at him.

“You'll want to get clear before somebody asks you to explain the painting.”

Merrit didn't wait for another invitation.

He ran up the old slip, climbed over the broken fence, and vanished behind the storehouses. He left knowing their faces, their ship, and exactly how much their promises were worth when somebody insisted on hearing them plainly.

Dutch opened the chart again on the ride back.

The wind tried to fold it. He held both corners.

“Now we have room.”

Brian looked at the newly empty stretch of pier behind them. Then he looked at the painting.

For once he let the difference remain a difference.

Sella inspected all four instrument cases in the submarine's cargo compartment that afternoon. She checked the brass fittings, counted the nested parts, and found no water in the covered cases.

She also found a crack in the corner of the fourth lid.

“That was there,” Dr. Evil said.

“Before what?”

“Your inspection.”

She ignored him, paid the remaining hundred and thirty silver after the agreed deduction for her ruined launch, and brought her own men to carry the cases away. The forty already advanced and the hundred and thirty now paid left the submarine's common purse at two hundred and thirty-six. The separate five stayed separate. Nobody discovered a second payment by changing what they called the first one.

Dutch tried to show Sella the painting.

She walked around it to reach the gangplank.

Dr. Evil watched the last case leave, then looked down at Brian.

“You called the signal.”

“After he saw us.”

“You called what was there.”

Brian turned the field glasses over in his hands.

“I didn't know which way the tower would fall.”

“Neither did the artist.”

Dutch was already carrying his work below. At the hatch, he paused beside the polished metal plate and turned his left cheek toward his reflection.

“There we are, Bob,” he said softly. “A place for everybody.”

He held the painting up beside his face. On its beach he had added four mango trees with trunks too short to support them. Their roots floated above the water.

Clifford reached past him for the ladder.

“Let the paint dry before you put it on your bed.”

Dutch lowered the paper.

“You have to imagine yourself there, son.”

“I'm trying to imagine getting down the stairs.”

Dutch moved aside, then looked back at the little portrait one more time. The metal gave him his own face and nothing else. The doctor waited until he was out of earshot.

“Tomorrow you can check the ship's announcement equipment. Half the crew pretend they cannot hear it.”

“Do they?”

“I would like a professional opinion before I accuse them.”

Brian looked up.

“A professional opinion?”

“About the equipment. Try to contain your portfolio.”

Brian handed back the glasses. Dr. Evil took them, then offered his empty hand. Brian shook one finger before realizing the invitation was to pass up the little carrying case too.

Neither man commented on it.

Across the quay, Billy sat with his back against the ropewalk embankment. Daynah was working a knot out of Mahnah's coat with his fingers. The dog lay on his side, one enormous hind foot twitching whenever Daynah found the right spot.

Arthur watched the harbor from the shade. Kaze stood beside him, Daniel's spear wrapped and secured at his back.

When the tower had fallen, Kaze had been halfway through describing how quietly he could cross the outer wall.

Now he pointed toward the broken skyline.

“That's a hell of a distraction.”

Arthur looked at him.

“For what?”

Kaze considered the closed stores, the rushing watchmen, and the fact that nobody here had actually chosen another target.

“Could've been for something.”

“Then it would've been planning.”

Kaze grinned.

“Mean old bastard.”

Arthur settled back against the wall.

“Premature young bastard.”

Billy's banjo sounded.

“I got a story about a man who celebrated early. This was before I was born, technically, but the guy telling it had an uncle—”

Clifford arrived before the uncle could acquire a surname.

He carried no saber. He had left it on the shelf in his submarine berth, beside the folded coat he didn't need for the short walk. The revolver stayed holstered at his hip.

Billy stopped talking.

“You ready?”

Clifford nodded.

Arthur glanced at him. Clifford kept looking at Billy.

Chris spoke through the banjo.

“Same river. I fixed the extra bullshit.”

“I want to see that first.”

“All right.”

No argument. Clifford looked briefly surprised by its absence.

Billy opened the way and deliberately took him inside. One moment Clifford stood beside the embankment; the next he stood in the interior space with Chris and the original console. Outside, Billy remained where he was.

The headset waited on the table.

Chris showed him the custom cliff trigger switched off, then loaded the fishing scene. He put the headset on himself long enough to demonstrate returning to the ordinary menu without a fall. The separate campaign save remained where Chris had left it.

Clifford watched every step.

“Again,” he said.

Chris did it again.

Chris moved a stool out from under the table. Then he offered the headset.

Clifford didn't take it immediately.

“Can I leave it there afterward?”

“That's where it is now.”

“I mean without somebody deciding I abandoned it.”

Chris set the headset on the table between them.

“You planning to abandon it?”

“No.”

“Then there you go.”

Clifford ran a finger along the edge of the strap.

“The other game. The longer one. That's yours?”

“My save is mine.”

“We'd work out when. You're not writing over my shit.”

“I wasn't asking to.”

“I know. I'm answering before we both get excited.”

Clifford almost smiled. Then he looked toward the place where he had come inside.

“I can ask to get out from here?”

Billy answered from within the space around them.

“Sure can.”

“What if he's playing?”

“Chris don't own the door.”

Chris looked up.

“I own enough other shit to keep me busy.”

Meatwad rolled closer to the stool and then back again, evidently trying to choose a distance that counted as watching without crowding.

Clifford saw him doing it.

“You can sit there.”

“Right here?”

“Yes.”

Meatwad settled with immense care, as though he had been given an assigned seat at an important public ceremony.

Clifford picked up the headset. He put it down again, unbuckled his gun belt, and laid it on the far side of the table where he could see it before the screen covered his eyes.

Chris watched the small arrangement and said nothing.

“I'm only doing the fishing today,” Clifford said.

“That's what I loaded.”

“Right.”

The word came out quieter than the rest. He drew the stool closer and tested its feet against the floor. It was steady. The return control had worked twice. His property lay where he could find it. He had asked to be there, and he had been answered.

For the moment, that was enough to pick the headset up.

Meatwad sat a short distance away, round and attentive.

“Can I watch the screen?”

Clifford looked at him.

“Sure.”

“I'm real good at not telling you where the fish is.”

Clifford sat, adjusted the headset, and took the controls.

The room disappeared.

He stood beside a river in the game, smaller in the familiar childhood body the fishing scene gave him. Water moved around a stone near the bank. Leaves shook above him. The virtual Arthur stood nearby with a rod in one hand.

There was no cliff at his back.

Jack looked anyway.

Only grass, the path, and trees.

The man beside him waited.

Jack picked up his rod.

His first cast landed too close to the bank. He drew it back too quickly, caught the line in weeds, and went still.

The virtual Arthur crouched beside the snag.

“We can get that loose.”

He worked it free without calling anybody over to watch.

Jack swallowed.

On the little screen inside Billy, Chris watched the line come clear. He glanced at Meatwad, who had opened his mouth, then closed it again with visible effort.

Jack cast a second time.

Better.

The float settled beyond the stone.

He waited, listening to the water. Nothing struck him from behind. Nobody turned his mistake into the reason he should never have tried.

After a while, the virtual Arthur sat on the bank beside him.

Jack let his shoulders lower.

Outside, the real Arthur got up to find something to eat. He did not know what was happening inside the headset. Jack had not forgiven him, and a kind voice beside a river had not asked him to.

The float moved.

Jack leaned forward.

This time he remembered to wait.

When he finally took the headset off, his face was damp and his first fish was still visible on the screen.

Meatwad raised one little arm.

“I didn't tell you.”

“No,” Jack said. His voice caught, and he cleared it. “You didn't.”

Chris took the headset by its strap. Jack stood and buckled his gun belt back on.

Jack nodded.

“Yeah.”

Billy let him out when he asked. Jack walked to the submarine in the fading light, passed the crew hauling in the last empty cargo straps, and returned to his berth.

He sat on the edge of the bed with it beside him. For a long time he looked at the opposite wall without seeing a cliff.
