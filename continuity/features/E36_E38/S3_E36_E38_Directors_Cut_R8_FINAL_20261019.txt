“What’s bigger than a planet?”

“The Sun.”

Chris put the empty jar down.

“The Sun pissed on me.”

Arthur stared at him, then let out a slow breath.

“Course it did.”

“Accidentally.”

That word changed the sentence.

“It wasn’t part of it,” Chris said. “Wasn’t aiming. Wasn’t teaching me anything. Just happened to do it where I was.”

He looked up too.

“And it washed everything off.”

The courtyard had become quieter. Bells still sounded in the town, but farther away now, where the damage had pulled people from their houses and given the watch several different emergencies.

“Mercury’s little pellets. Venus. All the rings, the dust, the stuff in my eyes. Tadmor’s fucking geometry. Arion’s heat. Gone. Skin clean. Clothes clean. Like somebody had finally put the world under running water.”

He rubbed his thumb against the side of his finger.

“I was grateful to something that didn’t even know I was there.”

Mahnuh stood and went to Daynuh. The man rested a hand on his broad head.

Chris lowered his eyes.

“My body felt clean. I didn’t.”

He did not try to improve that sentence.

After a while, Arthur asked what happened next.

“I started learning food.”

“All that made you cook?”

“Not overnight. It made me ask questions. What’s in this? What got taken out? Why does it feel different when you heat it? Why does a sauce split? Why does bread rise?”

Chris touched the overturned menu with one finger.

“The answers worked. That was the beautiful part. Eggs behaved like eggs. You treated cream one way, it did one thing. Treated it wrong, you could see where you’d fucked up. Somebody could explain it, and then I could do it myself.”

Kay Kay shifted beside the wall.

“You liked knowing.”

“Yeah. And I liked feeding people. That matters too. You make something right, somebody tastes it, and for a second they’re just happy it exists.”

His mouth tightened.

“At first, every new thing felt rude. Butter. Cheese. Milk could stand up. Nobody had warned me the beverage was capable of becoming furniture.”

Daniel laughed. “Cheese ain’t furniture.”

“Put a big enough block in a small enough apartment and watch what a broke man does with it.”

“Wouldn’t last.”

“Neither did my first couch.” Chris pointed at him. “That’s a comparison. Before anybody asks which fucking planet upholstered it.”

Kay Kay shut his mouth.

“Then mother sauces,” Chris continued. “I hear that and think, great, the gravy’s got a parent. At least somebody in this kitchen’s being supervised. Then you learn what it means. You learn the families, the techniques, why one thing becomes another. The weirdness stops being a threat and starts being something you can do correctly.”

He rubbed his thumb over a nick in the edge of the menu.

“I kept learning. Knife work. Stock. Sauces. Meat. Food safety. Watching good cooks. Asking questions until I could tell when the answer was bullshit. Eventually I got serious about the education. Learned how to run a kitchen instead of just being the loudest bastard near a stove.”

He looked through the ruined opening toward the place Ren had stood.

“That man knew what he was doing.”

Nobody had to ask whom he meant.

Chris left his hand on the menu for another moment, then took it away.

“That’s where the cooking started.”

Arthur stood.

“We need to move.”

Chris looked at him, then at the road.

“Yeah.”

Daynuh had already found a way along the retaining wall. Mahnuh tested it first, stopped where the earth had slipped and chose the firmer stones instead. Kay Kay picked up his stick. The cloth on his forearm remained stained; fresh scrapes marked the fingers that had caught the awning frame.

Kaze watched him look back at the restaurant.

“What?”

“I reached for her,” Kay Kay said.

“I saw.”

“You kept going.”

“Yeah.”

Kay Kay’s fingers tightened around the stick.

“I don’t like that you saw.”

Kaze held his gaze. The answer had reached somewhere a generic accusation would not have.

He did not apologize.

But he did not laugh either.

They took the rear passage, single file where the walls narrowed. Arthur moved ahead. He drew both UNSHAKEN revolvers, glanced down at them and pressed the heavy guns together.

The joined mechanisms moved under his hands.

Their dark connective structure drew tight. Metal turned, receivers locked and the two separate revolvers became one short, brutal firearm. HIGH NOON rested across his hands, oversized barrels above a compact, battered body. A sawed-off shotgun’s ugly close-range promise, made from the same weapons he had just held separately.

Chris looked up despite himself.

“Now that’s a useful fucking argument.”

Arthur checked the alley mouth rather than answer.

A shot struck the wall beyond his shoulder.

He dropped behind the corner. Another crack came from uphill, across the broken canal. Through the opening he saw the road-watch position: a man with a long gun behind a timber shield on a stone terrace. The man had watched the armed party emerge from the wrecked restaurant. Now he was reloading.

Arthur’s artificial red eye narrowed and opened around its central lens. He found the barrel, the hands, the angle through the timber, and the clear stone beyond the shooter.

HIGH NOON changed in his grip.

The short barrels and squat receiver reorganized. A longer barrel extended; the stock settled against the rebuilt right shoulder. The same joined weapon became THE COUNT, a formidable marksman rifle, not another gun pulled from nowhere.

Arthur leaned into the supported stance.

No prolonged whine. No waiting for a charge.

He aimed and fired.

The physical round punched through the timber shield and the man behind it, striking the stone beyond hard enough to blow a pale burst of fragments from its face. The guard’s rifle fell over the terrace edge.

The recoil drove into Arthur’s rebuilt shoulder. His boots held. He worked the action, caught the spent casing and checked the far position again.

Nothing moved there now.

“Go,” he said.

They crossed the exposed gap.

Once they were under the next stretch of wall, Arthur took the long weapon in both hands and pulled it apart.

The split was physical and ugly, metal opening through its living connective seams. Each half turned and closed into one of the original UNSHAKEN revolvers. He checked them before returning them to their places. One shot had been spent. Changing the shape had not given it back.

Chris looked at the casing in Arthur’s hand.

“You keeping that?”

Arthur put it in his own pocket.

“Yes.”

“Was just asking.”

“I know. That’s how you start.”

Daniel came through last, his bottle secured, his footing loose until it needed to be exact.

“Didn’t even get my dumplings,” he said.

Daynuh looked back at him.

Daniel shrugged.

“I can be upset about more than one thing.”

They left the Golden Heron with its bill unpaid and its kitchen silent.

On the intact shelf beside the courtyard door, Madam Lin’s order book lay beneath a fallen panel. The dust had covered the prices. Chris’s signature remained visible where the panel had stopped short.

Beyond the town, the road no longer led where it had led that afternoon.

At the harbor, Dutch finally opened the small bond box. He counted the silver while Tracy and Clifford kept the launch from settling too low in the water.

One hundred and seventeen.

Added to the eighty-two they had carried out of their ruined show, it made a sum large enough for Dutch to start looking hopeful again.

Jimmy watched him count.

“People were cheering us.”

“They saw what we did,” Dutch said.

“Some of what we did.”

Dutch looked up.

Jimmy did not lower his eyes.

Timmy tapped his wheel, pointed back toward the harbor, then toward the bucket in Clifford’s hands. He wanted them to stop admiring themselves and help keep the boat afloat.

Dutch put the silver away and took the bucket.

It was a better answer than another speech.

Brian examined the box’s customs mark.

“We should cover that.”

“With what?” Tracy asked.

“Our rescue credentials.”

“We don’t have rescue credentials.”

“We have eyewitnesses.”

“We also got theft eyewitnesses. You planning to stack one on top of the other?”

Brian considered this as an actual possible technique.

Tracy saw him considering it.

“Don’t answer.”

Cartman had taken off his mask because the people behind them had begun describing it to the watch. He still wore the cape, folded into his lap where it could no longer announce him from a distance.

“The Coon operates anonymously,” he said.

Clifford glanced at him.

“You shouted your name for twenty minutes.”

“Brand recognition and secrecy can coexist.”

“Not in your mouth.”

Tracy laughed so loudly Brian had to brace himself.

The current took the stolen launch deeper into the service channel. Behind them remained a wounded guard, a robbed harbor, rescued sailors, a broken quay and several incompatible stories, all of them containing something true.

Ahead, the water ran through a city whose foundations had begun to change.

They had no headquarters. They did have money, a boat, and an audience that might either buy them dinner or identify them to the authorities.

For TPP, that was practically a renaissance.

At the Immortal Bastion, Swain stood above the newly opened lower passage while Orra reported what she had seen.

The Grand General listened without interrupting. Dust had settled on his dark cloak. Beneath it, his unnatural left hand moved once, red light touching the edge of his sleeve.

Orra described the broken walkways, the water, the old door and the people they had brought out.

“How many still below?” he asked.

“We don’t know.”

“Then keep looking.”

“The old passage needs shoring before anyone goes farther.”

“What do you need?”

She told him. He gave the nearest officer the order.

Only then did he turn toward the exposed arch.

The Bastion had endured conquest, rebuilding and generations of men who considered themselves permanent. A portion of its buried history now stood open to the air, with fresh damage crossing masonry the modern city had never meant to show anyone.

No one there knew about the milk.

No one there knew about a cookie.

Swain looked at the direction of the fractured stone.

“Find where it began.”

Outside the market town, Kay Kay stopped on a rise and looked back along the altered land.

“My tree,” he said.

Daynuh followed his gaze toward the stream they could no longer see.

“We don’t know yet.”

Kay Kay nodded. He preferred the honest uncertainty to being comforted about something no one had checked.

Kaze stood beside him, one hand in the pocket of his samurai pants. His black shoes were powdered with the restaurant’s pale stone. The skin of his sword hand had closed, leaving dried black blood at the crease of his palm.

He took out the folded fortune.

For a moment, the whole group watched him read it again.

Chris looked at the little piece of paper.

“You gonna kill that too?”

Kaze’s mouth moved toward a smile.

“Thinking about it.”

Daniel leaned against his spear.

“Take five minutes.”

Arthur laughed first.

Then Chris did. Even Daynuh let out a short, unwilling breath through his nose. Kay Kay looked between them, learning that laughter did not always mean forgiveness, or agreement, or that a thing had stopped hurting.

Kaze folded the fortune carefully and put it back in his pocket.

The paper had survived the restaurant.

Noxus would have to be considerably more inventive.
