The first mistake was assuming the tree would follow them for dinner.

Not Kay Kay. The other tree.

The one he'd planted above the stream, where its roots were getting their first uninterrupted afternoon in honest ground. The empty tub stood upside down beside his ordinary walking stick, and the huge, newly living hybrid regarded the little pear tree with the patience of a person who had only recently discovered that something could belong to you without needing to travel wherever you went.

Kaze watched him from a flat stone, finishing the last piece of bread Chris had sent outside with supper.

“You coming or what?”

Kay Kay looked up. “Where?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Then I'm not sure what I'm agreeing to.”

Arthur snorted. “He's already got more sense than the rest of us.”

Daniel was hanging by one bare foot from a low branch, drinking from his frosted bottle with his other foot hooked around the trunk. He was spectacularly drunk and, as usual, entirely capable of discussing it.

“We could eat again,” he said. “It's called a second helping. Unlike the first helping, which, in my experience, never helps.”

Daynuh stood over Mahnuh, checking that the dog's drinking water came from upstream of the place Kay Kay had washed the blood off his hand. He examined the streambank without touching the bandage wrapped around Kay Kay's forearm. The cloth still concealed a real wound. Nobody's conversation had healed it.

“Not gonna keep a tree alive with a speech,” Dana said.

“I did water it.”

“Then leave it alone long enough for that to matter.”

Kay Kay considered the instruction. His roots remained where they belonged, inside his own feet. He picked up the stick and the empty tub. Mahnuh sniffed the tub, found absolutely nothing edible, and lost interest with a degree of personal contempt most senators would charge for.

In the living kitchen inside Catfish Billy, a completely different assembly of idiots had just finished arguing about whether pretending Meatwad was Chris's newly born son counted as fraud or research.

Chris had an actual meal to clean up, a real mixing bowl to wash, and Master Shake attempting to charge admission to view the bowl.

“You own a fat strip that hit you in the cheek,” Chris answered.

“That's intellectual property.”

“It's sheep intestine, man. It's got less intellectual property than the couch you already tried to sell.”

Frylock hovered above the kitchen table, inspecting the last scraps he'd been permitted to use for his experiment. He had cleaned his equipment. This did not stop Shake from calling him a war criminal for refusing to build a second fake person who could carry Shake's drinks.

Meatwad sat on Chris's newly washed plate and looked at the space where the puppet had been.

“Can I just be myself tonight?”

“That's the first goddamn idea I heard in here all day,” Chris said.

Charles Manson, back from consigning the puppet's inanimate remains to the surrounding stomach acid, lifted one finger.

“Identity is a burden.”

“Then why the fuck are you trying to collect everybody else's?”

A pause.

And there it was: a household operating inside an immortal demon-catfish, with the governing maturity of a shopping cart that has one wheel screaming sideways. Nobody could leave without the sovereign owner of the digestive tract making an actual decision, yet Shake had somehow concluded that the room's most pressing issue was licensing. If you're looking for proof civilization is fragile, stop studying falling empires. Put a milkshake in charge of a kitchen sponge.

On the first monitor, Arthur Morgan's old digital frontier waited exactly where Chris had left his RDR2 character.

“Billy. You hear me?”

The catfish, lying against the streambank outside, lowered his enormous head with the slow pleasure of a creature who had eaten kings before the first menu was written.

The change happened where Billy lay. Broad whiskered flesh drew inward; the vast head and tail surrendered the space to Chris's human frame. Chris stood in the very place where the ancient catfish had been. Inside BELLY, a full-sized Billy settled among Charles, Meatwad, Shake and Frylock, bringing the roommates a predator whose opinions about dessert were considerably more intense.

He took a small step away, turned back once, and then committed to following the others. He had not promised to return by a particular hour. The plant had not promised to wait.

Chris started walking toward the inland road. Kaze fell in beside him. Arthur, Dana and Mahnuh followed, with Daniel eventually dropping from the branch and landing beside them without spilling his bottle. Kay Kay took the edge of the track where his size would not crush the wagon ruts.

They found the restaurant in a market town where the inland road opened onto a stone square beside an irrigation canal. It had lanterns behind carved wooden panels, polished stone under the entrance, a low garden wall and a broad courtyard where tall guests could stand beneath an awning without placing their heads inside somebody else's dining room. The sign identified it as the Golden Heron. A painted heron looked sternly into a bowl, as though the bird had found a noodle that owed it money.

The owner, a woman with silver at her temples and flour on her sleeve, emerged from the doorway when she saw Kay Kay approaching. She did not confuse him with street furniture. She checked the height of the garden awning, measured the open yard with her eyes and asked whether he preferred to sit on the earth or stand near the wall. Kay Kay said standing would suit him.

“Your dog?” she asked Dana.

“Mine, yeah. He'll behave unless the food starts it.”

Mahnuh sat before anyone asked, an agreement he had made with the smell of roasting poultry rather than the owner.

Chris asked to see the menu. His face changed when the owner described her kitchen's preparation of stock, pickled greens and hand-pulled noodles. The interest was genuine. He asked about the amount of time the broth had been kept moving, listened to her answer, and nodded with professional respect.

A good chef recognizing another good kitchen. Beautiful. The species can apparently recognize craftsmanship without filing a declaration of war. At this point the evening could have gone beautifully, which ought to make the next events feel like a tragedy caused by decisions. Because that's precisely what they will be. Hold that thought. I'm going to hate being right.

Chris said he would take the whole party's bill. The owner asked for confirmation twice, once while looking up at Kay Kay. Chris agreed to the price of the menu and the courtyard surcharge as a charge against his own name, to be settled with the owner when service finished; his personal ready silver was not counted aloud and no communal coin changed hands. She marked the account in her order book and didn't confuse a signed obligation with payment already received. Chris accepted the terms.

Arthur ordered pork and greens with noodles. Daniel wanted dumplings, hot broth and something sour enough to make him admit he had a tongue. Dana chose duck, rice and a large dish of vegetables to share with Mahnuh only after checking what was safe to give the dog. Kaze asked what was strongest. The owner named a chili dish, and he chose that, together with enough rice to rescue him if he decided the challenge had become boring.

Arthur took off his hat. “You got a story for every item?”

“Some of 'em got stories for me.”

Outside the courtyard, ordinary townspeople walked home beneath lanterns. A wagon arrived with baskets of greens. Behind the kitchen wall, a cook was doing the unromantic work that makes a restaurant worth entering: maintaining heat, chopping accurately, tasting his stock and preparing courses for people whose lives did not revolve around being loudly interesting.

The first fortune cookie arrived as a complimentary amuse-bouche before the hot dishes. Crisply folded, placed on a clean plate, it contained a paper message.

Chris cracked his open. He read the writing once and then again, as a man examining an unexpected test result.

“boy you play kickball with russians”

“See? Even when you help, you make the investigation worse.”

“can i scrape your pipe?”

“Old problem with a game save. That motherfucker once made an Assassin's Creed Black Flag file disappear after I had damn near finished it. “Does he work here?” Dana asked.

“I got absolutely no evidence he does.” Chris laid both slips on the table. “But tell me that ain't the exact sort of thing he'd put in a cookie just to watch me discover punctuation as a weapon.”

The owner looked briefly at the papers and frowned. The messages were genuinely strange. Her kitchen hadn't become a hidden conspiracy because of them. She offered to replace the cookies. Chris refused, rolled up the first message and said he wanted it kept for the record of how weird dinner could become without anybody cooking badly.

In the harbor, the people calling themselves TPP had no clean table, no guaranteed next address, and eighty-two tracked silver in a pouch Dutch had begun treating as the last surviving institution of civilization.

Clifford's wheelchair bumped over an uneven plank as they turned away from their recently exposed headquarters. The dark helmet sat across his knees, the cape folded behind it. He could stand and walk with actual support after his surgery. He could not make a crooked neck or the long cost of that recovery disappear by deciding it would be impressive.

Dutch carried the stolen ledger beneath his coat. It had already made him sixty silver at Pell's brokerage before the broker tried to coerce him and Dutch reclaimed it. Pell now possessed reason to want the thing back, and the ledger's continued existence made the whole group more than a carnival fraud with a single stolen purse.

Tracy Morgan shoved a handcart with one arm. The other kept Brian, his miniature conjoined adult partner, from banging into a shutter when the street narrowed.

Timmy rolled beside them in his chair and pointed to an open-sided produce wagon moving away from the harbor. “Timmy!”

At the end of the street, Dutch stopped where the harbor opened below a row of warehouses. He didn't consult a prophecy. He opened the stolen ledger to a line he'd seen while Pell was bargaining. A customs transfer was scheduled for that evening: two sealed lockboxes from a confiscated shipping bond, brought from the counting house to the auction platform beside the old signal tower. A shipowner's forfeited bond, not a shipment of harmless rice. That distinction mattered to Dutch.

A better man would have examined the ledger and decided the money had already been stolen once. Dutch examined it and concluded the universe had done part of the stealing for him.

He saw the transfer take place and watched the clerk check a wax seal. The ledger was useful because it directed attention, not because it conferred command over the contents.

Brian perked up. “We could become the official public face of maritime safety. I can speak in the head-voice of a visionary without moving the rest of the body.”

Dutch considered him. This was one of those rare occasions when a dangerous man correctly identified both his own limits and his usefulness. Clifford looked like the aftermath of a medical argument; he could draw attention without pretending he had grown a second body.

Jimmy folded his arms. “I will do a performance. I won't tell people we're officials.”

Timmy pointed toward the ramp leading up to the auction platform. It was narrow and uneven, ending in a tall lip that neither his own chair nor Clifford's would clear comfortably without help. He slapped his wheel and glared at Cartman until Cartman finally looked.

Dutch watched two stevedores place a movable boarding plank against the platform. He had been about to tell Timmy to wait ashore. Instead he stopped and made the group approach the opening like ordinary customers. The plank would carry both chairs if properly positioned and not overloaded. Timmy had forced them to think about an entrance rather than a speech.

The harbor auction began with a sale of spoiled rope.

Jimmy stepped into the open space beside it, where a man was clearing wet straw from the platform, and began a short comedy act about drowning in shallow water while the wealthy drowned in deeper debt. The sailor laughed first. Then the clerk. Then a small crowd leaned nearer. The jokes bought attention, not legal permission. Dutch made no grand entrance. He walked through the crowd with the ledger concealed and listened to the auctioneer's description of the bond boxes.

Tracy stayed near the gangplank, giving Brian a terrible look every time the smaller man tried to announce that the actual Tracy Morgan was an optical illusion.

Above them, the harbor signal bell rang once.

The sound carried over the water. Two distant vessels adjusted their approaches to the moorings, responding to a normal local harbor indication. That was a real system, not decorative nautical music.

Dutch looked up at the bell, then at the lockboxes.

The first idea in his head had involved taking money.

The second involved the thing that told everyone where not to go.

And this is why I don't trust men who pause before calling a plan brilliant. They're not weighing ethics. They're checking whether the idea will fit inside a speech. Dutch can make a wolf sound like a moderately misunderstood shepherd, and—credit where it's due—occasionally the wolf really does know where the sheep are. Doesn't mean you should let him choose lunch.

Back at the Golden Heron, Chris took a slow breath of kitchen air and smiled.

He could smell ginger hitting hot fat at the correct moment. Aromatics, neither scorched nor drowned, and stock being reduced without being bullied into a paste. His chef's attention stayed sharp. Nobody had put suspicious human flesh into his mushroom dish. Nothing smelled wrong.

Which meant he had no culinary reason whatsoever to do what he did next.

“I wanna see that wok,” Chris said.

The owner glanced toward the kitchen. “The cooks are working.”

“Yeah, I know. That's the attraction.”

She told him guests couldn't enter. Chris waited until she turned to speak with a server, then slid past the swinging partition as though a sign that said STAFF ONLY were an invitation to a man who considered himself temporary management of the alphabet.

The chef at the stove was stirring the cream-and-mushroom sauce for Chris's order. The heat was steady. The wrist action was exactly right for preserving the mushrooms' texture; the chef hadn't scorched the dairy. He didn't need instruction. He gave Chris a startled look and then returned to the pan because a professional does not abandon the food whenever a customer mistakes confidence for access.

Chris leaned toward the huge wok beside the saucepan.

“NO. YOU NEED MO’ POWAH. USE MO POWAH, MO POWAH.”

The chef blinked.

He did not raise the flame. He did not alter his grip, add oil, change the angle, adjust the burner or revise the dish. He kept cooking precisely as he had been cooking before Chris came in. A mushroom turned in the cream. The stock continued to reduce as intended. Chris could see perfectly well that nothing changed.

“yeah yeah yeah, good. very good,” Chris said, pointing as though he'd just corrected a catastrophic mistake. “that wok is big as hell, dont put that joint on the great wall of china, that wok gon break that wall, cause it got too much powah, thats a Mongolian powah wok.”

The chef looked at the wok, then at Chris. “The dish is already correct.”

“See? That's the spirit. Took my advice without following a damn word of it. That's a gift, man. Like a mule that refuses to jump and still wins the high-jump competition because the judge gets scared of its face.”

“You are not supposed to be here.”

“I know. That's the part that gives the visit flavor.”

The owner appeared at the swinging door, took one look at Chris and pointed outside. “Out. Now. Leave the cooks alone.”

Chris held his palms up and backed out, grinning at the chef's utter refusal to humor him.

The chef continued exactly the same motion.

Not a degree more heat. Not a different ingredient. Not one meaningful alteration.

Chris had accomplished nothing except aggravating a perfectly competent professional, which was the complete purpose of his visit. He returned to the courtyard pleased with himself.

“Good place,” he announced. “Chef knows what he's doing.”

“Then why'd you go yell at him?” Arthur asked.

“Because I felt like being an asshole.”

Arthur considered him for a beat. “At least you ain't pretending it's philosophy.”

“I can make it philosophy if you need a longer dinner.”

“Please don't.”

Chris settled at the table. A server approached to say their courses would be coming out shortly.

Before any of them arrived, Chris said, “Get me a cold glass of milk. Real cold. Good, whole milk. That's what I want with this.”

The server repeated “milk” and took the order. Chris heard what he expected to hear. He was expecting WHOLE milk, the normal full-bodied thing he considered proper milk. He watched the server disappear through the door without checking the dairy stock personally.

A few minutes later, a glass appeared beside his folded napkin.

Chris lifted it. The milk was pale against the lantern. He took one sip.

The expression left his face.

He spat the mouthful onto the stone beside his chair.

“That's skim.”

Not a guess. Not an assumption based on a suspicious carton. An expert's immediate correct recognition of the genuine low-fat liquid in his mouth: thin body, missing richness, that unmistakable emptied texture. The Golden Heron had served actual skim milk. Nothing secretly replaced it between table and lips. The server hadn't done anything more sinister than carry the product the kitchen possessed.

Chris set down the glass very carefully.

“Who decided this was milk?”

The server looked confused. “It's milk, sir.”

“That's SKIM.”

“Yes.”

Chris's eyes moved from the glass to the kitchen door.

Kaze tilted his head. “What's the issue?”

Chris stood.

And a lovely dinner began to die.

The server tried again. “Would you prefer another glass?”

“Another glass of what?” Chris asked. His voice had gone low and exact. “If I order a horse and you bring me a skeleton with reins, don't ask whether I want a second skeleton.”

“I can speak to the owner—”

“You just told me it was skim. You knew.”

“I brought what the kitchen had.”

She had. It was a perfectly ordinary explanation, and it did nothing to reduce the thing rising behind Chris's eyes.

Arthur put one hand on the table, away from his guns. “Chris. It's a drink.”

Chris turned to him. “I know exactly what it is.”

“Then act like it.”

“I am.”

And now, dear viewers, we arrive at the world's least defensible syllogism. Premise one: the milk is skim. Premise two: Chris correctly knows that. Conclusion: everybody in the building deserves a ballistic obituary. That does not follow. That's not how logic works. I've seen a man in a red suit mistake personal inconvenience for cosmic purpose, and I can say with uncomfortable authority that being self-aware during a stupid decision doesn't automatically make it less stupid.

Chris pulled THE CRITIC into firing position.

The rifle was heavy. He brought the stock into his shoulder, moved his feet into a braced stance and turned toward the open kitchen partition. Nothing in the weapon made recoil optional. His practiced grip and the reinforced firing assembly gave him control; they did not erase the table, the people, or the distance between them.

The server saw the muzzle before anybody could persuade herself he'd only come to complain. She moved sideways. Chris followed her with it.

“Chris!” Dana barked.

The first shot took the serving counter apart and struck the male worker who'd stepped behind it to collect plates. Wood and crockery burst outward. The impact flung him backward into the wall, and he did not rise. Chris had chosen a line where a person was standing. He knew he had.

The boom was too large for the polite courtyard. The window above the counter split. A nearby lantern broke and hit the paving stones with its fire already dying in spilled broth.

A customer screamed.

The server who'd brought the glass ran for the side gate. Chris swung, trying to clear the low roof beam that blocked his barrel. Even the sharpened focus of Dead Eye couldn't give him a clean shot through solid wood. He lowered the muzzle, adjusted his stance and fired through the now-open partition. The round smashed into the back cooking wall; the fleeing woman had already moved behind a stone pillar. Plaster and shelving exploded across the kitchen.

Arthur was on his feet. He seized the chair nearest the gate and shoved it out of the running server's way.

“Go!”

Mahnuh's hackles rose. Dana moved between Chris and the patrons at the garden wall, creating a route around the muzzle for the people nearest the exit.

“Anybody not fighting, get out!” Dana shouted.

Chris fired a third time. This one struck an adult assistant cook who had bent to pull his colleague off the floor. The man fell against the stove and stopped moving. Hot stock spilled over his shoes. The cook whose mushroom dish Chris had just praised dragged the burner cart aside to keep the falling body from overturning the fire onto his fleeing staff. He was still doing his job while the kitchen came apart around him.

Kaze watched all of this with the half smile of a man discovering an argument that wasn't his and deciding he liked the direction it had taken.

“Hold up,” he said. “You going crazy over the milk?”

“It's SKIM!” Chris roared.

“I don't know what the fuck that means.”

Kaze looked toward the courtyard exit. The owner had returned to shepherd two terrified customers behind the garden wall. A younger server hesitated in the doorway, trying to get back to the wounded cook.

Kaze considered the open doorway, the man's exposed position, the disgusting ease with which the evening could still become ordinary if he simply walked away. He wasn't being threatened. He wasn't confused about that. His friends weren't requesting his help. He chose the violence anyway.

“Fuck it,” he said. “I'm joining in.”

Black Marrow formed from the flesh of his right arm, one dark living sword settling into his hand with the terrible ease of an old habit.

He crossed the courtyard in one motion. The server near the doorway turned too late. Kaze's blade entered beneath his ribs and came through his back; the force drove him into the doorframe hard enough to break it. Kaze withdrew the sword as blood ran onto the clean stone. The man folded beside a row of untouched serving trays.

The owner saw his face. Kaze saw her see it. He could have followed her, but the impulse had already spent itself on the first reachable victim. He turned back toward Chris, no remorse and no invented claim that he was saving anybody from a criminal scheme.

“There,” Kaze said. “You satisfied?”

Chris stood in the blown-open service lane, shoulders working against the rifle's real weight. “Don't know yet.”

“Yeah? Well, I still don't know why.”

Kay Kay lowered one enormous hand toward the awning, not into Kaze's path but over the frightened patrons now trying to climb through the narrow garden gap. His arm made a covered corridor beside the stone wall. The bandage on his other forearm pulled tight. He looked directly at Kaze.

“I am not doing that.”

Kaze shrugged. “Then don't.”

There was no unanimous cheer. That was the point of being a chosen family full of people who could choose wrong independently: agreement wasn't automatic, and the ordinary word family did not turn everybody's decision into a shared nervous system.

Daniel swung down from the awning support. His icy forearms caught a sagging crossbeam where the third rifle shot had loosened its timber joint. The beam was actually heavy, and the cracked joint continued giving way under its own weight. He wedged it against the intact stone instead of pretending cold air could repeal gravity.

“Great,” he said drunkenly. “A milkshake brings down the roof, and we don't even have a milkshake outside. That's false advertising.”

He laughed at his own line, then looked at the injured cook struggling under the shelf.

Arthur was already moving toward the kitchen. His red cyber-eye caught the collapse angle; his kinetic systems were ready, but he didn't need to fire either UNSHAKEN to pick up the edge of a cabinet and drag the wounded man free. He carried him across the lane while Chris rechecked the rifle's feed. Arthur's own hat came off his head and hit a puddle of broth.

Chris saw the man Arthur was carrying. He did not shoot at him. That was a choice too, not a revelation that the first people had been secretly enemies.

Dana caught the falling lantern frame before it dropped among the patrons. The protection traveled through his actual braced body and feet. He didn't make the shattered glass or human blood vanish. Mahnuh nudged a terrified customer out through Kay Kay's covered gap, then wheeled back toward Dana with his ears flat.

Billy spoke from within BELLY, his words carrying out through Chris with the banjo's familiar timbre.

“Christopher. I do believe the establishment has become inhospitable.”

“That's because they got SKIM MILK!”

“Then the cow has offended you, not the furniture.”

“Don't you start.”

Arthur laid the wounded cook down in the garden. The owner knelt beside him and pressed a cloth to the injury. She had heard Chris's accusation, watched him kill two workers, and watched Kaze kill a third. She would not leave this encounter believing the customers were harmless men who'd accidentally broken a plate.

A fourth rifle report rattled the courtyard. Chris fired into the serving counter again, destroying the remaining upright section. The table holding his fortune cookies overturned. Both slips of paper vanished under hot broth, although the people present had heard the words. He worked the action and stopped, the sharp mechanical sound loud in the settling dust.

“M.S.G.,” Chris said, looking at the ruin. “Make shit good. Not milked skim gaylords. That's what that means to me right now.”

Nobody applauded.

Daniel glanced down at a splinter lodged in the stone beside his foot. “You're gonna need a stronger slogan if you're planning to franchise this disaster.”

The owner raised her head. “Get out of my restaurant.”

Chris stared at her. He had enough ammunition, in principle, to continue hurting people if he could legitimately feed the weapon. But the kitchen was already in pieces, the people he had wanted to punish were either dead, wounded or gone, and his fury had found no satisfying object in the wreckage.

“Why did you have skim?” he asked.

She looked at the bodies, then back at him. “Because we had skim.”

It was the clearest answer anyone could give, and the one he seemed least prepared to hear.

Outside the garden wall a bell sounded from the town watch post. Someone who had fled was calling for help. The people who escaped had mouths, a road, and a story worth telling. The sound was already moving farther than the courtyard.

Arthur looked at Chris. Then at Kaze.

“We're not finishing this by sitting here and pretending those folks were armed.”

Kaze rested Black Marrow back against his living arm; it returned to his body. “I wasn't pretending.”

“I know you weren't.”

Chris's jaw worked.

From the side gate, Kay Kay said, “A person can recognize a thing correctly and still respond to it wrongly.”

Kaze looked up at him. “You lecturing?”

Venus. The pebbles. The rings. All of it. Even Hypatia out of my damn eye sockets. The heat from Arion, the feathery things from Tadmor. My clothes got clean. My skin got clean.”

He looked at his hands again.

“And I still felt filthy. Couldn't wash the part that remembered.”

There was no punchline. Chris knew it and left the space alone.

The open restaurant had been a good kitchen before he made it a murder site. The chef's competent hands had not changed a single thing when Chris yelled. The people he'd killed had possessed their own wishes and histories. Chris understood all of that, even if he had decided not to let it govern his actions. The story was an explanation of the depth of an old hurt, not a transfer of guilt from the men with guns to the dead.

Dana finally spoke. “So what'd you do when you were a kid, after all that?”

Chris answered slowly.

“I started asking what's actually in food. That's the first thing. Not because I thought every bowl was lying. Because I didn't want to live my life not knowing what something was. You put a glass in front of me, I wanted to know what gave it body, what got removed, how it changed when it got hot. Those aren't crazy questions. Those are cooking questions.”

He smiled faintly at something well beyond the wrecked restaurant.

“I found out that when you warm cream slow, it behaves one way. You scorch it, it does another. You break a sauce and you can see it break. Nothing mystical about it. A cook told me once you can't argue an emulsion into staying together. You've gotta understand the ingredients and what you're asking them to do. That was the first time a grown man explained a rule to me that actually worked every time I tested it.”

Kay Kay lowered his large head. “Was that comforting?”

“Yeah. Weirdly. Made me feel like the world could have rules without trying to trick me.”

Chris glanced toward the dead workers, then away.

“I started with little things. Eggs. Stock. Why bread rises. Why mushrooms release water before they take color. I loved when something came out exactly right. Not just because I controlled it. Because it tasted good and I knew why. I got greedy for that feeling. Asked cooks questions, watched good ones, made my own mistakes, learned how to fix 'em without lying about what the mistake was. Eventually I got serious about the education. Knife work, sauces, meat, food safety, how to run a kitchen where ten things need attention and none of them wait because the chef has a personality problem.”

Arthur looked pointedly at the smashed stove.

“Yeah,” Chris said. “I see the irony.”

He didn't smile at it.

“I learned the discipline. The reason behind it. The responsibility of sending a plate to somebody who's trusting you. That's the part of being an executive chef I actually love. You do the work right, somebody tastes it, and for that one minute the food is honest. I wanted to be the man who could do that. So I studied until I had the training to run a real kitchen. That's where the cooking part of my life began.”

He stopped.

Not because the past had become less strange, but because the account he'd chosen to give them had reached the point where a child decided to learn a craft. The rest did not need to be introduced.

Kaze shook his head slowly. “You went through all that shit, became a chef, then shot some people who were making your food right.”

Chris met his gaze. “Yes.”

“And I joined you because I wanted to.”

“Yes.”

“That's fucked up.”

“Also yes.”

Arthur picked up his hat from the puddle. He shook off the broth; the stain remained. “Well, at least nobody's gonna pretend we were the heroes in that.”

“Don't look at me,” Daniel said. “I was just here for the dumplings. And apparently the dumping.”

Mahnuh got to his feet. He went to Dana, waited for him to move, and then chose the open alley rather than the direct route past the accumulating watchmen. Dana followed his lead after checking the street. The dog didn't need a human translation to contribute a practical route.

Kay Kay stood upright again. He had been carrying the little tree's empty tub before setting it down at the stream, and he had left it there by choice. He had come to dinner with nothing in his hand except his walking stick. The bandage on his forearm was still damp with use. He looked from Chris to the broken kitchen and then toward the people who'd gathered beyond the wall.

“Yes,” Arthur said. “It's her place.”

Chris looked at the windowless opening where a cook had died. “Yeah.”

The group left by the narrow alley. Arthur stayed far enough behind to make sure the wounded cook was no longer within their way, then followed. The watchmen saw them go. One shouted for them to stop; when Kaze glanced back, the man looked at the broken building, the giant at the alley's mouth and the number of civilians behind him, and did not throw himself at an impossible fight. In the harbor, the stolen launch finally came past the outer pilings. It was not fast enough to outrun every vessel or strong enough to ram through the city. Dutch had chosen a narrower service channel because the south mouth's traffic had already reacted to Cartman's false bell. The launch squeezed through under the pilings while a pursuing boat was forced to turn away from shallower water. That small geographic advantage was real. It did not make the pursuit disappear from the world.

Clifford sat again, breathing hard, his wheelchair secured beside him. Blood had soaked the cuff he'd scraped against the rail. His crooked neck hurt; his shooting hand still worked. He hadn't become well simply because he'd won one exchange.

Tracy sat beside a crate, with Brian conjoined as ever and tiny arms folded in self-satisfaction.

“The public response was exceptional,” Brian announced.

“You almost got us killed at a boat auction,” Tracy said.

Jimmy sat on the deck beside Timmy, watching the black water through a crack in the boards. “We helped those people. Then we stole a box.”

Dutch didn't answer immediately. He was levering the lid free with an ordinary metal bar they already had aboard the launch. The seal resisted; the chest was real, with a real lock and a real delay, not a container that opened because a speech had reached its final line.

Cartman stood beside the engine. “I saved the harbor. You should all be thanking me for making the bell work.”

“You sent two boats at each other,” Jimmy said.

“Timmy!” Timmy called, pointing toward the deck where the sailors had escaped, then tapping his own wheel.

Cartman glared. “You know what? You're all welcome.”

The lid finally gave. Inside lay wrapped stacks of silver and two stamped documents identifying the confiscated bond. The documents bore the customs seal. They would make the silver much harder to deny when somebody found the stolen box.

Dutch counted the coins twice in the small lantern light. One hundred and seventeen silver in the seized box. Together with the eighty-two tracked silver they had carried from the show and the purse, that made one hundred and ninety-nine tracked silver available before any future spending. The older separate funds remained separate, and their rent and medical obligations had not evaporated because they'd stolen from somebody else.

Dutch set the last coin down, smiled, and held out his hands to the little crew that was beginning to hate his speeches.

“Not a perfect evening,” Dutch admitted.

Tracy stared at him. “It's a start.”

The old signal tower vanished behind a line of pilings. The launch kept moving on current and the efforts of the people aboard, still stolen, not yet a new headquarters or an unchallenged harbor empire. A wounded guard, an injured captain, a rescued boatman, a public crowd and a boy in a recognizably stupid cape had all left evidence behind. The gang had acquired money, transport and a wider class of enemies. Jimmy and Timmy had proved they could refuse a lie without stopping every violent adult in the group. Clifford had defended the theft and paid for the effort in pain. Tracy had saved strangers while helping the same thieves escape. Brian believed he had invented a profession. The outrage in his voice reached Brian before Dutch could answer.

I know. There's our distinction. One group destroyed an excellent dinner because a chef correctly recognized bad milk, and another engineered a maritime disaster to commit a fairly successful theft while pretending to promote public safety. Two different philosophies of human failure, each stupid in its own technically impressive way. Neither needs a greater cosmic mastermind. On the inland road, Chris walked beside Kaze without speaking. The others followed at their own pace. No feast had been eaten; nobody had secretly paid the bill. He had finished telling his story where he meant to finish it: at the first honest lesson he'd learned in a kitchen.
