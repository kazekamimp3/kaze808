“Maul. Before they went up the steps.”

“And you left when?”

“Your friend told me to. I saw the red thing come on. Then the first hit. After that I ran.”

He looked past Arthur toward the furnace.

“I should've stayed.”

“Where?” Chris asked.

The man pointed helplessly at the terrace.

Chris looked at the broken brick, the iron driven through the wall, the deep cuts across the stone.

“Which fucking bit?”

The worker's hand dropped.

Billy's voice came through the banjo.

“Did he have the stick when you last saw him?”

“I didn't see. It was there when I came back.”

Billy asked Chris to turn toward the wall. Through the headlamp he examined the little clear space beside it, protected from the cart's first broken timbers.

Then he asked to see the rest of the terrace.

Chris gave him the view slowly. The fish took in the damage without pretending he could watch backward through it.

Arthur wrote Maul's name on the back of a loose scrap. Beside it he put the worker's account, stopping at the point where the man had stopped seeing.

Daynuh put a hand on Mahnuh's shoulder. The dog had reached the scattered branches and was smelling along them, following the familiar scent into something he couldn't complete.

“Here,” Daynuh said.

Mahnuh came back to him.

Arthur stood beside Kaze.

“You see all of it?”

Kaze shook his head.

“Saw enough.”

Arthur looked toward the upper road again.

“We should get that weapon off the road.”

Kaze's fingers tightened around the ordinary stick.

“Then get it.”

Arthur went up the switchback. At the broken section, he found the body, the scorched stone beside the still-active blade, and the long hilt lying clear of Maul's hand.

He approached from the side, keeping out of both red ends. He'd just watched Clifford use a similar kind of weapon. That didn't make every control identical. He studied the hilt before touching the recessed switch with his gloved hand.

The blades went out.

He wrapped the hilt in a spare piece of cloth from his satchel and brought it back, keeping the control beneath a fold where a careless grip wouldn't press it.

The survey map lay torn beside the body. Arthur recovered that too.

Down at the works, Chris had found the workers' long iron tongs. He waited until they could safely reach what remained in the mouth of the furnace, then lifted the larger pieces into an empty stone trough away from the heat.

Nobody pretended the pieces would start talking if they were arranged correctly.

Billy asked to see the stick.

Chris turned toward it so the headlamp gave him the view.

Kaze held it up.

For a while the fish said nothing.

Then, “He put that aside.”

The worker looked at it.

“Had it when he came here.”

Kaze looked at the unburned timber in his hands.

He had taken care of the thing he was carrying.

That detail hurt in a place the blood on Kaze's chest couldn't reach.

Far below them, the cart from Tampon Pandemic Productions continued toward the port.

Clifford sat with the five silver secured separately, Arthur's folded reply inside his coat, and the complimentary roast protected from the little man beside it.

Brian had been rehearsing.

“A vulnerability test.”

Clifford kept his eyes on the road.

“An opportunity assessment.”

No answer.

“You could contribute something.”

Clifford looked at him.

“You opened the dish and called the dog.”

“That's not a contribution. That's a description.”

“It's what I'll say.”

Brian sat back against the empty containers.

The road seemed longer than it had on the way out.

They arrived the following morning.

Dutch met them at the surviving berth. Clifford handed over the five silver first, then Arthur's letter.

Dutch read it once.

His shoulders changed by a small amount.

He folded the paper and put it inside his waistcoat.

“He's well?” Clifford asked.

“He says he is.”

“Good.”

Dutch looked at the young man, then at Brian.

“How did the delivery go?”

“Worked.”

“And?”

Clifford nodded toward the covered dish.

“He'll explain.”

Brian opened his mouth.

Dutch held up one finger.

“Inside.”

The roast reached Dr. Evil's table before the explanation did.

That was unfortunate for Brian. His employer had already tasted the gravy by the time he learned somebody had tried to feed it to an animal.

Dr. Evil looked at him over the raised spoon.

“A dog.”

“A controlled—”

“My dinner.”

“The security arrangement required testing under realistic conditions.”

Dr. Evil put the spoon down.

“I see. And the realistic condition was that you wanted a dog to eat my dinner.”

Brian glanced at Dutch.

Dutch was placing the five silver on the table, where everybody could see what the job had actually produced.

“The client paid,” he said. “No stock lost. Clifford reports the access precautions worked.”

“My precautions,” Brian said.

Dr. Evil nodded.

“And then you tested them by becoming the problem.”

“That's not what happened.”

“Clifford?”

“He opened the dish and called the dog.”

Dr. Evil considered Brian for several seconds.

“Were you hoping it would found another organization?”

Brian's hands closed.

“I made a useful recommendation. Nobody acknowledged it. Then your meal gets prepared, and somehow you're the person everyone wants to thank.”

Dr. Evil looked down at the roast.

“It is very good.”

“That's not the point.”

“It seems to have been a significant part of your planning.”

Brian stepped closer to the table.

“I named the company. I told them how to secure the transfer. I've been trying to make this work while everybody treats me like something they found under the fucking furniture.”

Dr. Evil leaned back.

“And this was your response to being overlooked.”

“You keep saying it like that.”

“I'm looking for a version you find less embarrassing.”

Dutch came in before Brian could spend another hour buying the same humiliation in smaller installments.

“His recommendation was useful,” he said. “It should be credited. His attempt to spoil the result was deliberate. Those remain separate facts.”

Dr. Evil's eyes moved toward him.

Dutch continued, “I can use a man who sees a weakness. I can't send him to create one whenever somebody else receives a compliment.”

Brian looked down.

For a moment his voice lost the performance.

“I wanted it to be mine again.”

Dr. Evil let the silence sit.

Then he pushed the empty small plate toward Brian.

“You may have some chicken.”

Brian looked at it.

“That's your solution?”

“No. That is lunch. I thought we might distinguish the two before you involve another species.”

Clifford looked toward the door.

Something inside his mask might have been a breath. It might have been the day's only laugh.

Dr. Evil turned back to Dutch.

“Brian will not attend the next client delivery.”

Brian looked up.

“You can't—”

“You will review the arrangements here. Useful work. Your observations will be attributed to you. You will not be near the food.”

“That's not creative direction.”

“At present your creative direction appears to be toward a dog's mouth.”

Dr. Evil picked up his spoon again.

“And we shall discuss the name separately, as agreed.”

Brian stared at him.

The agreement hadn't vanished. Neither had the ship.

He took the plate.

Dutch drew a clean sheet across the table.

“Then let's put the next arrangement where everybody can read it.”

Brian looked up from his chicken.

“There is no next arrangement.”

“There won't be if we spend the first payment arguing over who invented a latch.”

Dutch dipped the pen.

“What did the seller actually say after the transfer?”

Clifford considered it.

“Asked if we could do it again.”

“Same price?”

“Didn't agree one.”

Dutch made a short mark on the paper.

“Good. We have an inquiry.”

Dr. Evil leaned toward the sheet.

“A repeat client.”

“A possible repeat client.”

“Must you keep taking the achievement out of the sentence?”

“I'd like to keep the money in it.”

Brian put his plate down.

“The receiving gate needs to stay closed until the cart has been secured. They opened it to let people look.”

Dutch wrote that down.

Brian watched the pen.

“And the covered dishes should travel away from the empty returns. A cover isn't a fastening.”

The pen moved again.

Dr. Evil looked at the dish in front of him.

“How alarming that somebody might remove it.”

Brian stared at him.

Dutch kept writing.

“Anything else?”

Brian hesitated, then pointed at the five silver.

“If you're going to offer another run, find out what you're carrying before you give them the same number. A cart of feed and a cart of live birds aren't the same job.”

Dutch finished the line.

Under the three recommendations, he wrote Brian's name.

Brian could see it from where he stood.

For several seconds he said nothing at all.

Then he reached for the sheet.

Dutch moved it out of reach.

“I'll need it.”

“I was checking the wording.”

“You may read it.”

Brian did. He corrected one word. Dutch changed it without ceremony.

At the other end of the table, Dr. Evil had found the edge of his covered trademark drawing beneath a stack of papers. He pulled it out halfway.

Brian saw the exposed letters.

The plate stopped moving toward his mouth.

Dr. Evil noticed.

He stopped pulling.

“Yes. That.”

“You said we'd discuss it.”

“We are discussing it.”

“You've drawn the whole fucking thing already.”

“I find pictures useful.”

Brian planted both hands on the table.

“If my contribution is valuable when it gets somebody through a gate, it's valuable when you put it above one.”

Dutch's pen paused.

Clifford looked at Brian.

There it was. A sentence he'd managed to reach without releasing poultry into it.

Dr. Evil studied the drawing. Then Brian.

“The name was your suggestion.”

“Yes.”

“The submarine was mine.”

“Nobody's trying to name your submarine Brian.”

Clifford made the noise inside his mask again.

This time it was definitely a laugh.

Dr. Evil looked at him.

Clifford looked back.

The doctor covered the drawing with the blank side of another sheet.

“You will bring your proposed terms. I will bring mine. Dutch will attempt to make them shorter than this conversation.”

“I want the suggestion credited,” Brian said.

“Write it down.”

Brian looked toward Dutch's pen.

Dutch gave him another one.

The small man took it, then dragged a blank sheet close enough to work on. Dr. Evil returned to his chicken while Brian began a demand that would fit on the paper if he resisted writing his own name quite so large.

Clifford stood to leave.

Dutch caught his eye.

“Thank you for bringing Arthur's reply.”

“Said I would.”

“Yes.”

Dutch touched the folded paper inside his waistcoat.

Clifford went back toward the berth. His lightsaber remained fastened to the chair, the clipped hilt knocking once against its frame as he crossed the threshold.

Behind him, Brian asked how to spell exclusive.

Dutch looked at the ceiling.

Dr. Evil said, “Oh, this should be good.”

Onshore, five silver from a successful job joined the company's funds. Dutch kept the old fifty-four distinct while they accounted for the new operation. Nothing about the meal settled the ownership dispute.

Upriver, on the evening the cart had left town, the workers had returned to the upper works one at a time after the noise stopped.

Arthur told them which stretch of road was broken now. He didn't tell them they had witnessed justice or try to sell Kaze as the man who made the country safer.

The foreman looked toward the furnace, then at the red blood drying on Kaze's skin.

“That one did all this?”

“Some,” Arthur said.

The man understood enough to stop asking that question.

He showed them an intact path back downriver. It went farther around the hill than the damaged road, but nobody needed to risk the new crater to reach the stream.

Arthur asked him to keep travelers off the upper bend until somebody had marked the damage.

“There's a body there too.”

The foreman's eyes moved toward Kaze.

“I gathered.”

“Weapon's with me. Don't let somebody go digging through the wreckage in the dark.”

The man called one of the returning workers over. Together they dragged an unbroken beam across the upper approach, well short of the cracked section. Arthur waited until it was in place before coming down the steps.

Before they left, the worker from the crossing approached Kaze.

“He didn't charge us.”

Kaze looked at him.

“For the bridge.”

“He wanted to look.”

The worker nodded.

“I said he could.”

Kaze stood.

For a second the man thought he'd done something wrong.

Then Kaze handed him the walking stick.

The worker took it automatically.

Kaze looked at the hand holding it.

He took it back.

“No.”

The worker let go immediately.

Kaze stared at the stick as though it had asked him a question in somebody else's voice.

Then he went down the steps.

The others followed.

Chris carried the cooled remains they could recover, wrapped in a work cloth the foreman had given him. He held the bundle carefully without pretending it was a patient.

Billy stayed quiet through the banjo.

That evening, they placed the remains in the ground near the pear tree K.K. had bought.

Chris lowered the bundle himself. He waited beside the open earth until Arthur asked him for room to finish.

Daynuh settled Mahnuh away from the open earth while Arthur worked. Chris stepped back. Kaze put the ordinary stick across the fresh ground.

The place where their guest had stood was empty.

Chris stood looking at the pear tree.

“He was gonna tell me what he found.”

Arthur leaned on the tool he'd borrowed from the works.

“I know.”

Chris rubbed his mouth.

“I planted a fucking vegetable.”

From the banjo came Billy's voice.

“And met somebody.”

Chris nodded once.

Nothing clever followed it.

Later, Kaze sat beside the stream washing Maul's blood from his hands. The water took what it could. Red remained beneath his nails and in the creases around his knuckles. His pants were still stained.

Arthur put the wrapped double-ended hilt on a dry stone beside him.

“Keeping it?”

Kaze looked at the shape beneath the cloth.

“You found it.”

“Didn't ask who found it.”

The water moved around Kaze's fingers.

“Not tonight.”

Arthur picked it up again.

Across the camp, Chris had put a little of the evening's food aside without looking at the empty space where K.K. usually stood.

Then he'd noticed what he'd done.

He left it there.

Daynuh sat with Mahnuh's head against his leg. Billy watched through the headlamp when Chris turned toward the tree, and did not supply a history lesson.

Kaze dried his hands on the grass and went back to the fire.

He stopped beside Arthur.

“That map.”

Arthur looked up.

“Got it.”

“Tomorrow.”

Arthur nodded.

Kaze looked toward the upper hills, where the damaged roads ran deeper into Noxus.

Then he sat down with the people who were still there.
