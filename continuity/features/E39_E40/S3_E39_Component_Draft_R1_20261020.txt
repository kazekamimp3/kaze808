Season 3, Episodes 39–40: “The Ground Has Witnesses”

The first thing K.K did after the restaurant was pull the cloth off his arm.

He examined the old wrap between two thin wooden fingers. The fibers had gone gray with dust. Beneath them, his forearm was a lattice of bark and dark heartwood closing around an old break. Tender new grain had formed in the gap, rising in careful ridges. It was not finished growing.

There was no blood. There was nothing there that needed a dressing.

“Lovely,” he said. “Someone made a little shirt for my injury. A shame the injury has outgrown it.”

Chris looked over. “That thing was still on you?”

“It was a thoughtful gesture. The fabric was beginning to believe it belonged to me.”

K.K folded it once. Then again. He offered it to Kaze.

“For your pocket. It has been near a terrible decision and would feel at home with your fortune.”

Kaze glanced down at his blackened palm, where the cut made by his own sword's recoil had healed. “You still pissed about the restaurant?”

“Oh, not continuously.” K.K smiled with narrow grooves in the wood around his mouth. “I take little holidays from it. Sometimes I remember you killing those people. Sometimes I remember you watching me try to save one. The second memory is rather more personal. The first one has better acoustics.”

Daynuh stopped working a pebble loose from Mahnuh's paw.

“That ain't much of a holiday.”

“I didn't say it was restful.”

Kaze looked at the strip of cloth and laughed. “Keep it.”

K.K tucked the wrap beneath a stone beside the road, neat as laying a guest's coat down for collection. He tested the regenerating arm. Fibers tightened with a dry series of small clicks. His long fingers made a fist. The last finger closed a little late.

“Still growing,” he said. “The young wood is terribly enthusiastic and has no manners.”

Mahnuh sniffed it.

K.K lowered his face nearly to the dog. “Please don't lick the crack. It's private.”

Mahnuh considered the strange wooden face, sneezed into it, and returned to Daynuh.

Daniel leaned on his knees laughing. He was still drunk, although his bare feet had kept finding the good stones while the rest of them crossed the torn ground.

Arthur checked the road ahead. The usual trading way had become three stretches of dirt with a long dark opening between them. Somewhere below, water moved in a direction water had not used yesterday.

“Can't keep walking that line,” he said. “Road's split.”

“No shit,” Chris said.

Arthur pointed at the black cut extending down the slope. “That is what I am talking about.”

Chris studied it.

“All right. Fair.”

I regret to inform you that a man who recently helped turn an ordinary dinner into an international geological incident has correctly identified a crack.

Give him a sticker. An enormous little gold star reading, CONGRATULATIONS ON NOTICING THE CONTINENT IS FUCKED.

A narrow rattle came out of Chris's chest.

Not his stomach. Banjo strings.

Billy had been watching through the little exterior feed and had apparently discovered an audience by hearing a road settle.

“Now, this takes me back,” the old catfish announced without the slightest invitation, “to my mother's account of a river that believed itself entitled to cut through our family's hunting ground.”

Chris closed his eyes. “Billy.”

“Eulalie Devadander Abercrombie could play an argument into a banjo, son. A whole river's worth. You'd hear her touch one string and understand that the water had behaved disrespectfully.”

Arthur stopped. “Is this gonna help us cross?”

“Young Arthur, I have been crossing things since the earth was a disorganized child. Your modern road is a pitiful little ribbon laid down by men afraid to get their boots wet.”

“That's a no,” Arthur said.

“Once, before your breed of horse was a notion in the Lord's unattended notebook, I found myself looking upon a whole basin of water that had changed its route. I was larger in those days.”

“You're ten and a half feet now,” Daniel said.

“A man can be larger in accomplishments.”

“Sure can.”

“And I said to that water, now, you have crossed a line.”

Chris rubbed his face. “You threatened a fucking river?”

“Threatened? Christopher, I offered it hospitality and then disputed the terms of its arrival.”

Inside BELLY, Shake was occupying the chair he'd insisted no one else could use. He had set his cup against the exterior monitor, concealing a strip of image.

“Ask him who won,” Shake said.

“The river?” Frylock asked.

“I want to know the winner before I listen to the rest.”

Billy ignored him.

“The water had swallowed a bank that belonged to no single creature, and my mother told me two things. One was that a river which moves its bed will claim its new place as if it had always slept there. The other was that any fool who builds the same bridge twice in the same torrent deserves a third lesson.”

There was the tiniest pause.

“Of course, I improved upon the advice.”

“How?” Daynuh asked despite himself.

“I did not build a bridge. I became the crossing.”

Chris turned toward the sound from within him. “You were the bridge?”

“In the telling, certainly.”

“In life?”

“Young man, do not examine my story as if it were a taxidermy specimen. My point is that I offered my back, and the creatures who dared use it had better remember whose back they had been on.”

“That's not a point,” Arthur said. “That's a toll road with whiskers.”

Billy sounded delighted. “At last, somebody appreciates the commercial genius.”

The story had gotten louder because Shake was talking over it.

“I own the rights,” Shake announced. “The bridge-fish story has a licensing agreement. Nobody cross the fish unless you buy a ticket.”

Frylock took his cup away from the monitor. The cup had obscured the left side of Chris's view, and Shake had been watching the world through the other half.

“Stop putting things on that screen.”

“Censorship,” Shake said. “An old man works his whole life to become infrastructure, and you won't let me monetize him.”

Meatwad looked at Billy. “Could I cross you for free?”

The catfish put a broad hand beside him.

“Of course, little gentleman.”

“That's favoritism!” Shake shouted.

“Yes,” Billy said. “I am enormously good at it.”

Outside, K.K had been listening to the river story with a leaf turning slowly toward the vibration.

“Your mother sounds delightful,” he said to the voice inside Chris.

“She was formidable.”

“Would she have eaten me?”

Billy considered that with suspicious appreciation.

“Why, sir, I believe she would first have asked where you were grown.”

K.K nodded thoughtfully. “A discerning lady. I might have liked to meet her somewhere with no cutlery.”

The ground shifted again.

Arthur stopped smiling.

Far below, somebody shouted.

The party moved toward the sound, not because anyone had voted on a rescue, but because the broken road had run out of useful options.

A freight wagon stood with its front wheels on firm ground and its rear axle almost over the new cut. One draft horse had pulled free. The other was still in harness, hind legs sliding on loose earth. An adult wagoner was trying to hold it from above. A woman and her grown son stood on the opposite bank. A smaller boy clung to the woman's skirt.

No one looked ready to die for a gang of strangers, but the wagon had already begun doing it without asking.

Daynuh put a hand on Arthur's shoulder. “We can get them across.”

Arthur judged the width.

“Not with a rope tied to that axle. Whole thing's going.”

Chris spotted the overturned bucket of grain beside the wagon.

“I could use some of that,” he said.

Daynuh rounded on him.

“Not right now.”

“What? I didn't say I was taking it. I said I could use it. That's a perfectly legal fucking thought.”

“Very generous of you to keep it in your head.”

Kaze stepped close enough to see the horse's eye, rolling white.

“You wanna save the wagon or the people?”

“The fucking horse,” Daynuh said. “And the people.”

“Ambitious.”

K.K touched the dirt with three fingertips.

For a few seconds, he was perfectly still. The leaf at the back of his neck trembled as vibrations crossed the soil.

“That ground has teeth,” he said, softly enough to make the wagoner stop shouting.

Daniel crouched beside him, Byakugan tracing the open spaces below the road. The crack's shallow edge led down into a deeper broken cavity.

“There's a hollow under the axle,” Daniel said. “We're standing on the wrong lip.”

He drew a pale line in the air with a frost-covered finger.

“Back two steps, ugly wagon man.”

“Who the hell are you?”

“A drunk with better eyes than your whole fucking carriage.”

The wagoner stepped back.

K.K put down his walking stick, drove roots into the firm bank and reached three long wooden limbs across the break. His body unfolded, gaining reach by genuine growth; wood pulled at the living seams around his ribs as he formed a low support beneath the wagon shaft.

Arthur cut the harness where the panicked horse couldn't clear itself.

Daynuh caught the animal by its chest and pushed it onto the safer shelf. His shoulder took the weight. The horse scrambled and found solid dirt.

K.K's roots strained. Fine cracks raced over fresh grain in his extended arm.

He watched them with the interested expression of a man examining a pretty piece of lace.

“Oh, this is becoming expensive.”

“Let the wagon go!” Arthur yelled.

“The wagon contains their winter,” K.K answered.

“How the hell you know?”

“I heard the woman say it, Arthur. I'm peculiar, not deaf.”

Kaze was looking at the opposite bank, where the woman's grown son had dropped to his knees with a length of rope.

“That's not gonna hold,” Kaze said.

“No,” K.K agreed. “But I admire the optimism. A little rope trying to have the responsibilities of a bridge.”

He anchored another root into the far bank. The strain shifted from the failing road to his own grown structure. Daynuh shoved the wagon forward. Arthur caught the high side and kept its heavy axle from dragging down.

Daniel stepped onto an iced patch he formed against the safe rock and used the angle to push.

The wagon came free with a noise like a house changing its mind.

K.K severed his sacrificial root brace as soon as the wheels reached stable ground. The growth tore away from his body, dropping into the crack. He inspected the broken stump, flexed it once and began slowly tightening new fibers over the exposed end.

The wagoner stared at him.

“Thank you.”

“Oh, don't do that yet,” K.K said. “You may have to regret my assistance. We hardly know one another.”

The little boy took one step forward.

“You're a tree.”

K.K bent toward him.

“Yes. You've discovered the problem with introducing ourselves by professions.”

“Are you a bad tree?”

K.K's face grew very still.

“I have been told I keep terrible company. The company has been told I am a terrible tree. We are all hoping to find out which description gets us a discount.”

The boy stared.

“What's a discount?”

“A wonderful little lie shopkeepers tell you while keeping the scissors under the counter.”

The mother pulled her son back.

K.K straightened. His voice became perfectly ordinary.

“Please move your wagon away from the rim. My roots won't hold the road forever.”

The wagoner thanked Daynuh, then stared at Chris's rifle, Kaze's tattoos and the broken landscape behind them.

“Did you come from the town?”

Kaze started to answer.

K.K spoke first.

“We did.”

The wagoner looked at the enormous living tree.

“Is it still standing?”

K.K gave him a polite, thoughtful look.

“Enough of it to remember what it used to be.”

Kaze's smile faded a fraction.

Nobody had asked K.K to lie.

Nobody had given him a story to tell. He had picked his own.

At the Immortal Bastion, the first thing Darius heard was somebody calling the building a tomb.

He crossed a shattered square with his enormous axe balanced in one hand and stopped beside the guard Orra had pulled out of the crack. The guard was alive. He had refused to leave until the men underneath him were found.

“Who's trapped?” Darius asked.

Orra pointed to the old arch. “Two builders beyond the lower stair. More in the occupied rooms farther down. Can't count them while the weight's shifting.”

Darius looked at her, then at the arch.

“You're in charge of the structure?”

“Until somebody better appears.”

“Then you're in charge. Tell me what moves.”

She led him to the door that had been sealed inside the Bastion's layered foundations.

Up close it did not resemble a treasure vault. Iron reinforced an ancient slab set against a low channel. The door was caught in its frame by pressure from the shifted stone around it. Water was seeping through one lower seam.

One soldier suggested cutting the hinges.

Orra caught his sleeve.

“Cut the hinges and the weight comes into the passage. Then your funeral's part of the repair.”

Darius watched the water.

“Where's the pressure coming from?”

“Old channel. The newer one broke when the ground opened. This door is taking the load.”

“Then it stays shut.”

“Until we can move the water another way.”

He nodded. He did not pretend to know stone better than she did.

Above them, a section of wall cracked with the heavy, measured report of a tree being felled. Dust passed over the arch.

From the stairs came Swain, his dark coat marked with the same pale rubble covering every other person in Noxus. The Grand General had stayed long enough to hear Orra's report; now he returned to see what the builders were actually facing.

“How long?” Swain asked.

“Until the old channel gives way?” Orra said. “Couldn't tell you. Maybe it waits. Maybe we run out of questions.”

“Can you relieve it?”

“Need space above this brace and men who listen when I tell them not to be heroes.”

Darius looked over his shoulder at the nearest soldiers.

“Pick the second kind.”

Swain watched the water.

“Whatever caused this had enough force to compromise foundations built before the empire. I want the direction of each failure recorded.”

Darius frowned.

“You want records before we get the people out?”

“I want the men in the street alive. And I want to know if this is the first strike of something that intends a second.”

There was no argument in that.

It was a perfectly Noxian problem. A city that respected strength had discovered that strength did not stop a floor from deciding to become a ceiling. The army could fight an invader. It could not put a chain around the ground and drag it into an interrogation room.

Don't worry, they'll probably try.

A gray-haired woman in a plain work cloak appeared behind the soldiers, carrying a rolled measurement cord.

“I was asked to inspect the old brace,” she told Orra.

Orra looked at her hands.

No fresh dust at the cuffs. Clean nails. A woman's hands that had supposedly been working below street level while stone fell for the last hour.

“Who sent you?”

“The upper ward.”

“Name?”

“Does it matter?”

Orra held out her own dirty palm.

“Yes.”

The woman smiled politely.

Darius turned toward her. “If you aren't here to help carry something, get back behind the line.”

For a moment, the stranger's face was difficult to place. Not changing, not exactly. More like Orra's attention had slid off it and returned without catching the same features twice.

The woman looked beyond the door, past the water stain, to a small patch of ancient writing newly exposed above the lintel.

“I should leave this one sealed,” she said gently.

Orra answered before Swain could.

“That's exactly what it's doing. I'm trying to stop it from killing people while it does.”

The woman glanced at the beam.

“Then don't open what you cannot put back.”

“That is excellent advice,” Orra said. “For somebody planning to go home.”

The stranger moved away.

Swain watched her go, but he did not summon ravens to announce what the woman had eaten for breakfast. He had seen a face and heard an unhelpful answer. That was all.

“Who was she?” Darius asked.

“An interest,” Swain said. “Not yet a fact.”

Orra's apprentice called from the lower stair.

The sound had changed.

The ancient iron slab groaned against its frame, and somewhere underneath it a second channel began to sing.

At the harbor, Clifford Marston had discovered that Dutch intended to keep their boat afloat by optimism.

Dutch stood at the bow with one pole. Tracy had the other. Water reached Clifford's boot soles where his chair was parked, and Brian was attempting to take charge of the emergency because he happened to be near the tallest available person's shoulder.

“Everybody remain professional,” Brian said. “Our rescue reputation depends upon it.”

Clifford looked down at the wet floor.

“Funny word, rescue.”

No one had asked.

Dutch knew from the tone he was about to lose the next several minutes.

Clifford leaned forward, adjusted the support around his damaged leg and continued.

“When I was little, my father wanted me to stay clear of the life he lived. John Marston, for those of you who've somehow spent this much time with me and missed the fucking name. He taught me to ride, to read a man's face, to keep my head down when I had to. Not because he wanted me to turn into him. Because he didn't.”

“Clifford,” Dutch said.

“No. You wanted a story about saving people? Have one. My father died with the government at the gate. They sent enough armed men to make sure nobody could mistake it for a conversation. And later my mother died. I buried the last part of the home he'd been trying to give me.”

Tracy stopped poling.

His expression was suddenly, inconveniently serious.

Clifford looked straight ahead.

“I found the man who arranged my father's death. I killed Edgar Ross. Stood there afterward thinking revenge would change the fucking air. It didn't. Just left me with a man's body on the ground and the same family missing from every room.”

Cartman opened his mouth.

Jimmy struck his foot lightly with a crutch.

“Don't.”

Clifford's gaze moved to Dutch.

“Then I came here. Chris and Arthur shoved me off cliffs. Not once, not by accident. They found it funny. Cut stupid letters into me because they could. My neck healed wrong. Men put metal in my back so I could stand again, and somebody put a helmet on me that made me look like a goddamn discount executioner.”

He tapped his crooked helmet, resting on the deck beside the chair.

“Now I'm sitting in stolen water with the world falling open and you want to tell me this is an inspiring rescue operation?”

Dutch held the pole steady.

For once, he had no grand reply that could improve what he had heard.

Brian raised a tiny finger.

“We still need a name for the operation.”

Clifford slowly turned.

“Brian. If you attach one more slogan to my fucking spine, I'm going to write the full story on your forehead and make you wear it at the next auction.”

Tracy laughed once, involuntarily.

Then he looked ashamed of it.

Clifford looked back at the dark water.

“I ain't telling you all that because I want a hug. I'm telling you because Dutch here keeps calling us a family when it's useful, and I'd like one fucking person to understand what that word cost me.”

Dutch had been waiting for his turn to speak.

That sentence took it away.

The little launch struck a piling.

Timmy hit the side of his chair and pointed.

A patrol skiff had entered the service channel behind them.

Jimmy looked back. “They've found us.”

The watchmen shouted for the launch to stop.

Dutch took the pole from Tracy and shoved hard.

Clifford reached for his revolver.

“Don't,” Tracy said.

“They're armed.”

“So are we. You start shooting and the next fuckup ain't a rescue. It's a fucking massacre.”

The words landed harder than Tracy intended.

Clifford looked at him for a moment.

Then he put the revolver away.

The boat passed under the low beam Timmy had warned them about earlier. Dutch steered for the narrower drainage channel ahead. The skiff behind them tried to follow, then stopped when its taller stern rail struck the timber frame.

Cartman stood up.

“Behold! The Coon has selected a secret escape tunnel!”

Timmy shoved him back down before he cracked his head against the next beam.

“TIMMY!”

They escaped the first pursuers through a channel neither Dutch nor Cartman had known was open until Timmy found it.

It was a victory. A dangerous one. And this time nobody could confidently assign the credit to a man with a hat.

Back beneath the Bastion, the second channel burst.

Water hit the old door hard enough to bow its lower hinge plate. The iron bar Orra had been studying began to peel out of the wall.

Darius planted his axe across the gap as a temporary brace, then shouted for timber. Soldiers dragged a heavy support into position while Orra told them exactly where to place it.

A voice came from the corridor beyond the door.

Not ancient magic.

A man coughing.

Then a woman's shout.

Orra pressed her ear to the stone beside the channel.

The lower passage connected around the old room. People from an inhabited underground street had fled from the collapse and found the only surviving stair blocked by the weight of the shifted door.

“There are people behind it,” she told Darius.

The soldier nearest the bar stared at the water.

“Then opening it will drown them.”

“Leaving it will crush them.”

He looked at her.

“Which is worse?”

She put the measurement cord in his hand.

“Hold this end. Find out with me.”

The bar tore free another inch.

The Grand General's red left hand opened in the half-light.

And the oldest stones in Noxus began to move again.
