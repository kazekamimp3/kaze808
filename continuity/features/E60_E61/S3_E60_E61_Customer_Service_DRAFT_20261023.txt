Daynah waited beside the drainage ditch below them. Mahnah stayed behind his leg, nose moving over the damp grass. The forepaw that had been scraped on the road bore his weight without a visible limp this morning; Daynah still kept him off the sharp broken wall stones.

The plan was straightforward. Let the valuable wagons enter the constriction. Stop the front. Close the rear. Take what they wanted.

Several highly qualified murderers had assembled to perform the work of two traffic cones.

A horn sounded inside the yard.

“They've heard something.”

“Pell?” Chris asked.

“Maybe. Keep down.”

The front wheels were already passing Daynah's position.

Kaze rose.

“We wait, we lose those too.”

Arthur's eye followed the escort's hand toward his weapon.

“Then take the front two.”

Kaze went over the wall.

He landed ahead of the horses with enough force to punch two heel-shaped holes into the packed road. They shied. He caught the wooden tongue between the team and the wagon, shoved it sideways, and forced the driver to haul back on his reins.

The vehicle lurched to a stop across the narrow lane.

One walking guard brought his crossbow up. Arthur's first shot struck him beneath the cheekbone before the stock reached his shoulder. The bolt went into the retaining wall.

The second guard ran for the ditch.

Daynah stepped into his path.

The guard looked at the chest in front of him, then tilted his head up as though the rest of the man might be less of a problem.

He thrust his short spear.

Daynah knocked the shaft aside, grabbed his coat, and drove him backward into the barrel wagon's iron wheel. The rim caught the back of his skull. His knees folded underneath him.

Mahnah came forward with one heavy bark that made the horses strain against the reins again.

“Stay,” Daynah told him.

The dog stayed, shoulders forward, watching the road.

The mounted escort fired from the gate. His shot cracked stone beside Arthur's head.

Arthur had already changed positions. His second shot took the rider out of the saddle. The horse bolted without him, disappearing through the gate into the yard.

The driver of the enclosed wagon immediately began backing up.

Chris ran down the slope, drew his revolver, and covered the barrel wagon's driver.

“Hands where I can see them.”

The man raised both hands.

Kaze climbed onto the front wagon's step and tore the driver's crossbow out of his grip. He threw it into the ditch.

“Off.”

The driver jumped down on the far side and ran.

Kaze started after him, then looked back at the boxes underneath the canvas.

He chose the boxes.

Three shots, two broken bodies, one dead rider, and the road had acquired new management. None of the goods had agreed to this. Neither had the people.

Kaze looked toward the gatehouse.

Nobody at that gate needed to wait for a second demonstration.

The submarine had spent the previous night making a surface passage along the coast. Its crew had checked the charts, avoided the shoals, and tied the damaged recreation-room panel securely enough that nobody had received Brian's old ceiling wound as a new gift.

At the wheel, the helmsman eased the engine back and waited for the pilot boat to clear his bow. A crewman ran forward with the first mooring line.

Dutch stood at the rail beside the doctor. Clifford remained near the hatch, helmet tucked under one arm, watching the loading yard.

Brian had gone below to find string.

That was what he called it when he noticed somebody else putting useful things in the trash.

Beside the shipboard research desk sat a bin containing crumpled notes, broken pencil stubs, a stale heel of bread, and a thick sheaf of pages whose top sheet read RED DEAD REDEMPTION: CHARACTER REFERENCES.

Brian stopped.

He looked toward the open doorway.

No one was watching.

He took the entire sheaf, shook a bread crumb from it, and carried it to the low bench beneath the desk.

The first page concerned Dutch.

Charisma. Loyalty. Public disagreement. Framing practical objections as protection of the group's shared purpose.

Brian turned the page.

Another sheet described Jack's reading, family history, and sensitivity to being patronized. A note in a different hand warned that source-game portrayals did not account for the man's present tattoos, reconstructed body, or specific experiences with Chris and Arthur.

Brian skimmed that warning and kept reading.

The useful bits looked like instructions.

He opened his journal to a clean page and wrote: PERSONALITY ACCESS.

Then he underlined it twice.

A crewman came through the doorway carrying a coil of cable.

“Those were thrown out,” he said.

Brian put one hand over his new heading.

“I'm aware.”

“Doctor said he remembered the material.”

“All of it?”

“What we gave him, yes. He was quoting pages back at us. Wrong occasions, mostly.”

Brian glanced at the doorway again.

“Does he want them destroyed?”

“He wanted the desk clear.”

The crewman set down the cable and left.

Brian stacked the pages neatly.

He wrote a second heading: INDEPENDENT RESEARCH.

The bread crumb remained stuck in the fold.

On deck, Dr. Evil noticed him come out with the papers beneath his journal.

He looked at the familiar top sheet. Then he looked at Brian's carefully neutral expression.

“Find your string?”

“Something useful.”

“Good.”

The doctor turned back toward the harbor without asking for it.

Brian stood a little straighter beside him.

At the export yard, Chris was buying food and filling their water containers. Twelve silver paid for bread, dried meat, vegetables, and water sufficient for their immediate stay. Arthur counted it from the common purse, leaving two thousand four hundred and thirteen.

Daynah took Mahnah to the shade beside a bollard while Kaze watched two dockworkers struggle with a badly balanced crate.

The dog settled, then slowly extended one forepaw toward a dangling end of rope. Attached to the rope was a basket of dried fish on the stall beside them.

Daynah watched the paw move.

Mahnah watched the fish.

“Don't.”

The paw stopped.

Daynah turned toward the water containers.

The paw moved another inch.

The fish seller saw it and lifted the basket onto a higher hook. Mahnah followed the entire movement with his head, then looked at Daynah as though the man might have an opinion on this deterioration in service.

Daynah scratched behind his ear.

“I saw you.”

The seller cut a strip from one fish and held it out with considerable caution.

“He can have this.”

Daynah took the strip first, checked it for sharp bones, and offered it from his open palm. Mahnah accepted delicately enough that the seller's shoulders dropped.

“He's polite,” the man said.

Daynah looked at the raised basket.

“When he's getting what he wants.”

Mahnah licked the last salt from his fingers and lay down again. The rope remained where it was. Daynah stayed beside him this time.

“Lift the other end,” he called.

One worker glared at him.

“I am.”

Kaze watched the crate tilt farther.

“Not very fucking convincing.”

Then Chris saw the submarine.

He stopped with a loaf under one arm.

“No way.”

Arthur followed his look.

The hull eased toward its berth. Ropes went across. Crewmen secured them, and the gangplank came down only after the ship had stopped moving.

Kaze saw Dr. Evil at the rail and lifted one hand.

The doctor returned the gesture with surprising enthusiasm.

Dutch saw Arthur.

His hand tightened on the rail.

Clifford saw Chris.

He put his helmet on.

Different reunions. One dock. Enough unresolved resentment to keep the cranes working all winter.

Shake demanded exterior access as soon as Chris carried the news inside during the next switch.

Billy let him out onto the quay. He stepped down the last safe fold of the exit, straightened, and surveyed the submarine.

“Finally.”

Meatwad followed only far enough to look from the permitted exit. He chose to stay inside.

“Bring something back,” he said.

Shake didn't promise.

Charles remained in the living space. Chris's camera stayed with Meatwad. Nobody had decided to film the reunion, and no new footage appeared by being interesting.

Shake walked along the quay, around a stack of mooring rope, and up the gangplank under the eyes of a crewman.

The crewman held out a hand.

“Wait.”

Shake stopped.

“I've been here.”

“I know. Wait.”

Dr. Evil looked down from the deck.

“What's the problem?”

“The cup wants aboard.”

“I am a guest,” Shake said.

“Ah,” Dr. Evil replied. “The demanding one. Let him through.”

Shake continued onto the deck as though the delay had been a personal scheduling failure by the ocean.

Billy remained on the quay while Kaze and Arthur approached the ship. Daynah stayed with Mahnah and the purchased supplies.

“You following us?” Kaze asked the doctor.

“Yes. I moved several thousand tons of vessel because I missed your conversation.”

Kaze laughed.

“I knew it.”

“Business,” Dutch said.

Arthur looked at him.

“You finally find some?”

Dutch came down the gangplank. The mango and little trees on his forehead looked particularly aggressive in daylight. The painted portrait tattoo on his left cheek had not improved his patience.

“You always did mistake mockery for independence.”

“And you mistook me being nearby for me working for you.”

Chris's voice came through Billy's banjo.

“Arthur, come on. He gives a speech, you say sure, Dutch. That's the arrangement.”

Arthur turned toward the banjo.

“Is that how it works in your little machine?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then go ask that Arthur.”

Kaze slapped Billy's forearm, delighted.

Dutch looked from one to the other.

“My brother is still dead.”

The line stopped the laughter around him.

Arthur didn't look away.

“I know.”

Dutch's eyes went to Billy's banjo.

“And you still find a way to make this a game.”

Chris did not supply an apology he didn't mean.

“I wasn't talking about Bob.”

“You never are.”

Dr. Evil stepped onto the gangplank before Dutch came closer.

“We have an appointment. And several enemies who have not yet had the courtesy to become personal.”

Dutch held Arthur's gaze for another moment, then stepped aside.

The doctor looked at Kaze.

“Do try not to destroy this harbor until we've finished using it.”

Kaze glanced at the cranes.

“No promises.”

“I was afraid you'd say something encouraging.”

While the others spoke, Clifford came down carrying the headset from his berth.

His saber stayed aboard, laid on the shelf beside his bunk before he left. He had made that choice himself. He was coming to ask a question, not to make a threat that the people in front of him would enjoy answering.

He stopped in front of Billy.

“Is Chris listening?”

“Unfortunately,” Billy said.

Chris answered through the banjo.

“What's up, Clifford boy?”

Arthur's mouth twitched.

Clifford looked at him, then back at Billy.

“I'm not here for that.”

“For what?” Chris asked.

“The performance where you say the name and everybody acts like you've invented humor.”

Arthur looked down at his hands.

Kaze laughed once and let it go.

Clifford held up the headset.

“The fishing part. Before what you did. Can I play that again?”

Chris didn't answer immediately.

“You want to go back?”

“I want the river.”

He looked at Arthur, and for a second the hurt was plain enough that even the old gunslinger stopped arranging his next remark.

“That one talks to me,” Clifford said. “Like he wants me there.”

Arthur's expression shifted.

“Jack—”

“I'm talking about the one in the game.”

Billy lowered himself slightly so Clifford didn't have to keep staring upward.

“That's Chris's equipment. His decision. Entry to my house is mine.”

Clifford nodded.

“I'm asking both.”

Chris moved close enough to the interior monitor to look carefully at the headset in Clifford's hands. Nothing about the man's face suggested he had forgotten the cliff.

“The console stays inside,” Chris said.

“I know.”

“And my campaign save stays alone.”

“Fine.”

“I need to take the other trigger out. The prank part. Show you it isn't running.”

Clifford gripped the headset tighter.

“You can do that?”

“Yeah.”

“Then show me before I put it on.”

Chris looked at the monitor a moment longer.

“All right.”

Billy nodded.

“You can visit. No weapon swinging in my living room. No wandering into the storage because somebody told you there might be a door.”

“I just want to fish.”

“Then we'll get along splendidly.”

Clifford passed the headset through Billy's controlled intake route into the living space. Chris received it, checked its cable and casing, and set it beside the original console.

Clifford watched until the headset was safely through. Only then did he release the strap he had been holding so tightly that its edge had marked his palm.

“Tomorrow,” Chris said. “I gotta sort this stuff first.”

Clifford nodded, accepting a real answer instead of begging for a better one.

He returned to the ship under his own power.

Dr. Evil met him near the hatch and glanced at his now-empty hands.

“Successful reunion?”

“I returned the headset.”

“Ah. Equipment continuity. Very important in a franchise.”

Clifford stopped on the top step.

“I'm going to play again.”

The doctor nodded with the solemnity of a man receiving an announcement he intended to treat as something he had already anticipated.

“Naturally. Back in the saddle.”

“Fishing.”

“Yes. The wet saddle.”

Clifford stared at him.

Dutch, standing close enough to hear, examined a perfectly ordinary knot in the mooring line. He did not smile. He did not explain. His face remained under control through an effort that Dr. Evil interpreted as confidence.

“A classic,” the doctor continued. “One of my favorites.”

Clifford adjusted his grip on the handrail.

“What is?”

Dr. Evil paused.

“The general experience.”

“You've never done it.”

“I appreciate many things from a position of leadership.”

Dutch cleared his throat.

“We should check the launch.”

“Exactly,” Dr. Evil said, grateful enough to sound commanding. “Jack, take whatever time you need after tomorrow's work.”

Clifford's expression softened just a little.

“Thank you.”

He went below before the doctor could prove his familiarity any further.

Brian met him at the foot of the ladder.

“What was that?”

“None of your business.”

Brian glanced at the journal under his arm. He had just read a page about Jack responding badly to pity.

“Of course,” he said carefully. “Personal autonomy. I respect that.”

Clifford looked at him.

“Did you hit your head?”

“No.”

“Then stop talking like you did.”

Below deck, Shake found a chair near Brian's berth and sat down without invitation. Brian entered a minute later and stopped in the doorway.

“That's my chair.”

“I'm using it.”

Brian looked toward the corridor, found no audience for a territorial dispute, and climbed onto his bunk.

Shake noticed the journal.

“You write in that a lot.”

“It's material.”

“Songs?”

Brian's answer came too quickly.

“Among other things.”

Shake held out a hand.

“Lemme see.”

Brian hesitated. Then he covered the research notes with his blanket and passed over the page he'd been revising before the move.

Shake read it in silence.

For once, Brian didn't interrupt to explain where to pause.

“I like that bit,” Shake said, tapping a line.

Brian leaned forward.

“Which bit?”

Shake showed him.

It was an actual line. He had actually read it.

“Yeah,” Brian said, trying to make the word smaller than the pleasure behind it. “That one's pretty layered.”

Shake kept reading.

Outside, the Sins settled on a patch of dry ground beyond the depot's rope sheds, close enough to trade and far enough from the loading lane to sleep without a wagon passing over somebody's foot. Billy admitted Chris's purchased supplies and stayed exterior while the others made camp.

Arthur watched Clifford's dark figure disappear down the submarine hatch.

Kaze sat beside him.

“You gonna say something?”

Arthur shook his head.

“Not tonight.”

Inside Billy, Chris set the returned headset on the table and opened the controls for the custom prank sequence.

He selected the cliff trigger.

Then he switched it off.

Meatwad watched from beside the table.

“You fixing it?”

“Changing it.”

“Was it broke?”

Chris looked at the disabled trigger.

“No. It did what I made it do.”

He kept the separate campaign save untouched and checked the fishing session's return to the ordinary menu. The headset stayed on the table. He was testing the controls with his hands, not putting another person inside to see whether they screamed.

Charles stood near the kitchen, eating from his own portion of the newly bought bread. He looked at the screen, then at Chris, and kept chewing.

Meatwad tilted toward the headset.

“Can I watch him catch one?”

“If he says yes.”

“What if he don't catch one?”

Chris picked up the controller.

“Then you can watch him not catch one.”

Meatwad considered this arrangement and seemed satisfied.

Chris tested the return command once more. The menu appeared immediately.

He left the headset beside it, ready to show Jack exactly what had changed.
