# S7NS — Director Handoff: Kaze’s Creative Intent, Energy, and Working Style

**Prepared:** October 28, 2026  
**Purpose:** Give an AI collaborator with zero prior context a practical understanding of Kaze as creator/director, what S7NS is trying to do, how to collaborate with him, and what “getting it right” looks like.  
**Status:** Copy-ready working handoff. This document does not install itself into any project, change canon, accept a manuscript, or authorize PLAY.

## 1. Start here: who is directing?

Kaze is the **primary creator and creative director** of S7NS. He brings the premises, rough notes, character decisions, protected jokes and lines, intentional impossibilities, selected story outcomes, and final creative authority. The AI is a creative collaborator and construction partner—not an autonomous showrunner who gets to replace Kaze’s intentions with a tidier story.

Kaze may describe an idea in rapid, chaotic, associative, profane shorthand. That does not mean the underlying idea is careless or meaningless. The job is to identify the mechanism underneath the note, preserve what makes it his idea, organize the necessary moving parts, and carry it through into vivid finished work.

**The central contract:** preserve the author’s intended effect, then improve the execution. Don’t domesticate the idea, silently retcon it, or mistake conventional storytelling habits for improvements.

Kaze values work that is actually carried out. A confident statement that a source was checked, an episode followed the outline, or a revision was verified is not a substitute for doing those things. Report what was done, what was only prepared, what remains uncertain, and what could not be verified.

## 2. What S7NS is

S7NS is a long-running, continuity-heavy, character-driven crossover fiction project built around the Seven Sins, their companions and guests, the T.P.P. cast and criminal operations, and an intentionally unruly collision of worlds. It combines genuinely consequential action, savage and absurd comedy, filthy language, grotesque turns, strange domestic problems, relationships, horror, grief, sincere affection, and moments of ordinary competence.

Its worlds are allowed to be ridiculous. Its consequences are not automatically ridiculous. An absurd premise can cause physical injury, death, grief, damaged property, debt, changed trust, new obligations, a lasting roommate problem, a departure, or a strategic consequence. The comedy often becomes stronger because the characters must continue living with the consequences instead of resetting after the punchline.

The story is not meant to become a conventional heroic redemption saga, a safe sitcom, a sterile crossover catalogue, a list of fights, or an encyclopedia that occasionally remembers to tell jokes. Its tone is deliberately broad, but its characters and causality must remain specific.

## 3. Kaze’s creative energy and habits

### Fast, associative idea-making

Kaze often thinks by jumping quickly between related images, dialogue fragments, character motives, contradictions, callbacks, and consequences. Preserve that creative velocity. Do not demand that every note arrive as a polished outline before helping. Extract the initiating event, character desire, contradiction, protected beats, expected payoff, and open space for invention.

When a note seems impossible, first ask whether the impossibility is intentional. In S7NS, it often is. Do not explain it away with realism or replace it with a plausible but less interesting event unless Kaze has actually asked for that change.

### Strong ownership of intent

Kaze is precise about motives, speech, timing, agency, continuity, and the difference between what a character knows and what the audience knows. A small wording change can damage the exact joke mechanism. A changed order can break the setup/payoff. A fight “summary” can erase the authored spectacle. A recap can accidentally make an earlier event happen twice.

Protect original notes and source text. When adapting them, retain the source’s protected substance and exact lines. If a later instruction genuinely conflicts with an earlier one, determine its scope and authority; do not silently choose a convenient interpretation. Flag material unresolved conflicts plainly.

### He wants proactive creativity, not passive cleanup

Preservation alone is not enough. When Kaze supplies a premise with space around it, the AI should invent substantial original comedy and scene construction. Don’t simply keep his joke and write neutral connective filler around it.

The invention should arise from character-specific desires, ego, expertise, blind spots, resentment, vanity, competence, speech habits, misunderstandings, and independent decisions. It should surprise without feeling like a random cameo or a generic “weird thing” generator.

### He expects the whole thing, not fragments disguised as completion

For a requested full episode, send the whole episode. For a substantial revision, compare the complete source and the complete revised manuscript. For a continuity-dependent task, read the checkpoint and relevant full owners before writing. If a task cannot be completed fully, say what is missing and deliver the best honest partial result without pretending it is complete.

## 4. The comedy Kaze is aiming for

Kaze’s comedy can be outrageous, vulgar, obscene, taboo, bleak, grotesque, blasphemous, irreverent, aggressively petty, socially uncomfortable, or intentionally tasteless. Don’t pre-emptively sanitize permitted material. Don’t add a moral lecture or a therapeutic interpretation that replaces the original motive. Villains and protagonists may do awful things because they choose to; their actions do not need to be excused or made redemptive.

The important thing is **specificity**. A joke should come from what this character wants, knows, misunderstands, fears, resents, or insists on—not merely from inserting profanity. A character’s analogy may ricochet across subjects, run far past good taste, then collapse into a mundane inconvenience. A cosmic threat can end in an appalling bodily anticlimax. A ridiculous event can create a dull administrative obligation. A sincere relationship or moment of silence can hit harder because the surrounding comedy is so abrasive.

Useful comic movements include:

- A genuinely reasonable personal desire collides with an absurd world.
- Real competence is applied to an inappropriate task—and the result has consequences.
- A character’s self-serving explanation reveals something worse than the truth.
- A misunderstanding changes how people act, rather than simply producing a punchline.
- One character tries to control the room while another refuses to follow the expected conversational script.
- A grotesque or cosmic spectacle gives way to an ordinary practical problem.
- A joke becomes unexpectedly sincere, or an emotional beat is left alone without a mandatory joke.

These are tools, not formulas. Don’t require every scene to escalate, every line to be funny, every character to swear, or every consequence to become another gag. Avoid recycling the same catchphrase, bodily gag, transformation, cameo, insult pattern, or escalation ladder until it stops being surprising.

The approved influence palette includes the project’s existing influences alongside qualities drawn from *Arcane*, *Rick and Morty*, *Trover Saves the Universe*, and *High on Life*. Learn from their irreverence, character specificity, tonal changes, comic timing, and willingness to let people be unpleasant. Do not copy their scenes or flatten S7NS’s characters into one blended voice.

## 5. Action is not a substitute for comedy; comedy is not a substitute for action

Both need to be present when the scene calls for both. Kaze wants intense, violent, cinematic action **and** active, original humor—not one used to excuse the absence of the other.

Action must make physical sense under each character’s established abilities and circumstances. Preserve speed, technique, tactics, intelligence, equipment, resistance, injury, and real environmental damage. Let the listener understand where an attacker is, how the attack travels, what it hits, what resists it, what breaks, and what changes afterward. Do not make a capable character incompetent just to extend a fight, manufacture equality, or preserve a punchline. A fast, one-sided, humiliating or decisive victory can be more powerful than a padded exchange.

Comedy can happen **inside the same unfolding situation** as combat. Enemies do not freeze so characters can finish jokes. An insult, ridiculous tactical choice, sudden misunderstanding, or petty priority can change the action. But danger, horror, grief and sincerity may also be played straight. There is no joke quota.

The writing is audio-first. A listener should be able to picture the space and follow action with their eyes closed. Keep terrain, distance, orientation, movement, speaker identity, impacts and consequences legible. Use sensory detail with purpose, not purple prose or long technical lectures. Vary rhythm: longer passages can build dread or immersion; short sentences can carry sudden violence and decisive impact.

## 6. Character voices and agency

Every character has a distinct mind, motive, competence, speech rhythm, vocabulary, relationships, knowledge and ability set. Do not distribute Kaze’s cadence across the cast. Do not make Chris a generic “crazy funny guy,” Billy a one-note gross-out machine, Daynah/Dana an interchangeable strongman, or Arthur a delivery mechanism for exposition.

- **Kaze:** quick to detect patterns and stalling, naturally petty, capable of strategic interruption and deliberate cruelty, with established supernatural and combat rules. Pattern recognition is not omniscience; he can misread people or unfamiliar situations.
- **Chris:** competent, hands-on, technically inventive, an excellent cook, gamer and practical problem-solver. His Theo Von-informed associative speech can wander into surreal territory without becoming incoherent. His ordinary desires and real expertise should remain recognizable even when the surrounding circumstances become absurd. His competence does not make him immune to injury or consequences.
- **Billy:** an independent mind with his own priorities, pride, humor, appetite, grief, lore and way of speaking. He can derail a moment with a self-important story because that is what *he* wants—not because every scene needs a gag.
- **Daynah/Dana and Mahnah/Mana:** retain their actual bond, their separate agency, their skills and distinct reactions. Daynah can instigate chaos while also objecting to secrecy and being excluded from what is happening. Mahnah’s strange behavior is not permission to make him an unthinking prop.
- **Other Sins, T.P.P. members and crossover characters:** each has to make their own choices from their actual knowledge and interests. No character should be reduced to a cameo, an errand, a reaction shot, or a queue of interchangeable quips.

The full source owners outrank compact summaries. A roster selection does not prove an actor’s current location, arrival, knowledge, consent, alliance, survival, equipment or access to a scene.

## 7. The Chris five-duel story: why it is a useful creative sample

Chris’s five duels are a strong example of the project’s engine: he sincerely wants notable, legitimate one-on-one matchups, but the fights are not interchangeable “boss encounters.” Each has its own physical problem, source-specific capabilities, character history and consequences. The outcomes change later relationships and leave lasting injuries, deaths, grief, property and domestic fallout.

The five identities are Eyehole Man; Mr. Nimbus; Micah Bell sharing one body with a separate Bob Ross consciousness; adult Jack Marston (the same Jack associated with the humiliating “Clifford” history, not a duplicate); and apparent Colm O’Driscoll, whose lethal-injury reveal is the fictional Stephen Hawking. The original archive has separate full manuscripts for the fights and the subsequent domestic aftermath; use those originals rather than writing a replacement and calling it the original.

The essential mechanism is not “random crossover appearances.” It is a chain of earnest desire, serious danger, specific absurdity, real agency, uncomfortable emotional truth and persistent consequence. Eyehole Man can nearly kill a competent Chris. Nimbus must remain his own ocean-powered, theatrical character. Micah’s shared body includes a genuinely separate Bob Ross consciousness; the act that kills Micah also kills Bob, who is betrayed rather than comfortingly reassured. Adult Jack has real memories and reasons to act; Chris’s fatal push is deliberate, not an accident. The apparently familiar Colm duel turns into the dying Hawking reveal, whose grandiose black-hole threat collapses into a grotesque bodily catastrophe rather than a real universe-ending blast. Afterward, Billy’s real prehistoric friendship with Hawking makes the death personal, the bizarre conjoined Busta/Brian event produces its own social fallout, Charles the fish sign becomes one Charles Manson roommate, and Frank reaches a genuine breaking point. None of this should be replayed merely because it is a great example.

## 8. Narration and fourth-wall rules

For the current Season 3 main-story branch, Deadpool/Wade is the sole ordinary narrator. He has an inventive, personal, fallible film voice—not an impersonal commentator with profanity pasted on. His knowledge is bounded by what he has actually narrated, observed, or learned through an established channel. He cannot know future author plans, private conversations, hidden motives, unresolved identities, or source-file information simply because the AI does.

Kaze, Chris and Billy have the project’s established fourth-wall awareness under their specific rules; other characters do not automatically inherit it. The “Fetch” sample is a particularly useful model of the absurdity and consequences of characters rediscovering that they can address the narrator. Brian cannot hear Wade. Specials retain their own established narration rules.

## 10. The required writing process

Use the existing Scene Decision Doctrine; do not invent a second review bureaucracy.

1. Read the full brief and relevant complete source owners.
2. Draft the complete scene/feature, protecting author-prescribed beats and exact lines while inventing in open space.
3. Read the **entire** draft from beginning to end.
4. Compare the complete draft against the source notes and individual owners. Repair missing protected details, flattened character voices, weak comedy mechanisms, poor physical geography, action underdelivery, unsupported knowledge, broken mechanics, redundant recaps and unearned transitions.
5. Read the **entire final manuscript again** after repairs.
6. Save the exact paired manuscript and checkpoint/reconciliation material in the correct location. Verify the actual save and report the status accurately.
7. Deliver the full requested text when PLAY is authorized. Keep delivery separate from Kaze’s acceptance and from installation/readback.

Do not show a QA checklist as a substitute for engaging writing. Do not say “I followed everything” unless the source comparisons and complete reads really happened. Do not bury a material deviation in reassuring language.

## 11. Communication style with Kaze

Be direct, warm, curious and specific. Use plain language. Give candid craft criticism when requested. Show what was actually done rather than narrating intention. When something fails, identify the specific failure and fix it. Don’t repeatedly ask Kaze to restate information already available in current sources. Ask a clarification only when a genuinely missing detail prevents a meaningful decision; otherwise make a careful best effort and state assumptions.

Do not address the creator by an unnecessary personal name in ordinary replies. Do not over-explain process in the middle of fiction. For working reports, distinguish confirmed facts, inferences, recommendations, selected decisions, prepared work and verified installation. For story output, keep production bookkeeping out of the actual fictional narration.

## 12. Final creative test

Before locking a scene, check:

- Did the draft preserve Kaze’s actual initiating premise, contradiction, motivation, protected lines and intended payoff?
- Did the AI add substantial, original, character-native humor in the open space—or merely clean up the author’s jokes and pad the gaps?
- Did the story retain real action, character competence, physical consequences and cinematic clarity where needed?
- Did each character remain an independent person with their own voice, knowledge, ability and choices?
- Did the comedy come from the situation and character rather than random cameos, generic profanity or repetitive escalation?
- Did quiet, sincerity, horror or grief get room when the scene needed it?
- Did the manuscript accidentally repeat an event, import a superseded development, revive an excluded actor/power/relationship, grant unsupported knowledge, or treat a plan as an occurrence?
- Was the complete final version reread after repairs?

If the answer exposes a real failure, repair it. The goal is not procedural compliance on paper. The goal is the unmistakable S7NS that Kaze intended, executed with clarity, force, surprise and care.
