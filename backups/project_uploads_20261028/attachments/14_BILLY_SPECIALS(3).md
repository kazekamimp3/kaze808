# Eulalie Devadander Abercrombie

Mama found me outside our shelter with red mud drying around my ankles. She set down her bundle of roots and fruit.

"Billy."

"Good morning, Mama."

"Where you been?"

"Nowhere."

She looked at my feet. "Nowhere got an awful lot of mud this morning."

Now, gentlemen, my birth name was Aloysius Devadander Abercrombie, but Mama had called me Billy from infancy. Her own name was Eulalie Devadander Abercrombie. Yoo-LAY-lee. And whatever name she used for me, I could generally tell from the sound of it whether I ought to run.

She recognized the clay from the lower river, where she'd specifically forbidden me to go. I suggested the wind might have brought it uphill.

"And dropped it directly on your lying little feet?"

"Remarkable precision."

She folded her arms. "Billy, where's the little river animal you were bothering yesterday?"

I'd found a young herbivore with an injured leg and discovered that chasing it made me feel considerably larger than I was. Mama had caught me, made me promise to stop, and gone about her work. The next morning I'd returned to the river.

She made me look her in the eye.

"Lord have mercy. You ain't even good at it yet."

"Good at what?"

"Lying."

"I beg your pardon."

"You're using too many words."

Christopher laughed through the banjo beside our fire. I let him enjoy himself. The boy had no idea how much worse that afternoon became.

At the river, Mama found tracks leading toward a fallen tree. The youngster was wedged beneath it, pulling against its trapped leg. When it saw me, it struggled harder.

"Look what you done."

"He's the one who ran under the tree."

"Because you chased him."

"Well, technically..."

"Don't you technically me."

She crouched beside the trunk, then stopped with one hand extended. A dark shape was moving beneath the deeper water. She pushed me behind her.

The river broke over an enormous flattened head. Jaws snapped toward the trapped animal. Mama dragged the youngster clear, and the teeth closed on the timber instead. Wood burst across the bank.

She shoved the animal toward me. "Billy! Get him up that slope!"

I caught it beneath its front legs. It kicked me in the stomach. I complained. Mama was occupied with a mouth large enough to take us both, but she still found time to tell me the creature was frightened.

"So am I!"

She struck the predator's head aside. Its teeth tore her arm. I stopped pulling the youngster and stared at the blood.

"Billy. Take that animal uphill. Right now."

I tried again. The youngster kicked my face. I fell backward and wished aloud that the enormous bastard would eat it.

Mama heard that too.

"You don't get to wish death on somebody because they don't like you!"

The beast dragged her into the shallows before I could offer a defense. Water closed over them. Then her hand caught the exposed roots, and the animal rose behind her with its jaws opening over her shoulder.

Mama grew.

Her shoulders widened. Heavy fur rose along her back, and the roots strained under hands suddenly large enough to tear them out. The eyes looking at that creature were still the eyes that had examined my muddy feet.

She caught its upper jaw and drove its head against the stone. It dragged itself from the channel, swept her legs with its tail, and clawed her shoulder. She lost her grip. Its jaws closed around her forearm.

Mama forced its head upward with her free hand. The throat stretched. She bit into it, held through the thrashing, and twisted until something broke. Its tail scraped the mud a few more times. She waited until it stopped.

When she returned to her smaller form, she was breathing hard. Blood ran down her arm. I was beside myself with admiration.

She looked past me.

"Where's that little animal?"

It was still lying at the foot of the slope.

"Why ain't it uphill?"

"Because it kicked me."

She shut her eyes.

"Twice."

Mama pointed uphill. "Pick. Him. Up."

I picked him up.

On the higher ground, Mama checked his leg and kept a clear route for him to leave. He eventually limped into the vegetation. Her own arm still needed attention. Then she made me replace the roots and fruit she had dropped coming after me. I spent the rest of that afternoon gathering under her supervision; the rotten piece of wood I presented as food made her laugh hard enough to sit down.

Arthur smiled across our campfire. I rested my hand on the wooden rim across my lap.

"This here was my mother's."

He looked at the banjo. Christopher's voice came more quietly through it.

"Wait. That's the actual one?"

"Yes, Christopher."

She could play considerably better than I could. She had work, hunting grounds, opinions, and an entire valley's worth of history before I entered the arrangement. Her brother Nar was my maternal uncle, and a genuine friend of mine. Mama's strength was her own. Her patience, unfortunately, was something I tested myself.

When she was gone, I kept finding things I wanted to tell her. That was the part I hadn't expected.

I remembered waking in our cavern and finding no answer when I called. Outside were broken trees, damaged stone, red blood and green. I hadn't watched the fighting. I'd slept through it. For a long time, all I had were the things it left behind.

I was still a young creature when hunger and danger drove me into my first proper devouring in a ravine. I'd swallowed something five times my size. The wounds were tender afterward; three ugly marks crossed my shoulder, and stretching too far reminded me how close the affair had been. Discovering what my mouth could accommodate didn't immediately teach me when to use it.

My old stomach could keep things apart from food. I began saving pieces of what I'd survived. That little collection was useful long before there were rooms inside me or a young man demanding space for his tools.

There was a centipede called Rattle whose venom left me unable to move while I remained perfectly conscious. Recovering from that experience did rather spoil my opinion of being difficult to kill. And there were hunts I won't detain y'all with: a dead river hunter's armor, a tooth from a predator I'd swallowed, a fragment of a flying hunter's fractured crest. They went into preservation. I learned to hunt something that had been hunting me. None of those meals explained where Mama had gone.

The clue that did was red cloth.

A pterosaur I knew as Skiff had it among the objects gathered at a nesting cliff. I recognized the dark edging and those uneven little stitches Mama used when something needed to hold rather than look pretty. I traded fish for the material. Skiff led me farther along the cliff, where a shelf lay beneath an overhang.

There were bones, deep gouges in the rock, and another scrap of the same cloth. Its torn edge nearly fitted the first. In a sheltered crack I found a dark green stain.

I touched it. A little flaked away beneath my finger.

"Mama?"

Nothing answered.

I knew my own blood was green. I didn't know whose blood had dried there, or whether the cloth had been torn on that shelf at all. Before I could examine much more, the cliff gave way. Falling rock injured Skiff's wing. I found him on a lower ledge, lifted him with the damaged wing supported, and carried him down. The wing later healed. I kept both fragments.

Farther along the search, I walked into a feeding site beside a stream and fought four feathered predators. I killed two outside me and swallowed a third. The fourth retreated. Following it, I found youngsters sheltered behind it and another strip of red cloth caught above their hollow.

I stopped at the entrance. The adult held its ground. I backed away and let Skiff pull the cloth from the roots.

He carried it downstream and dropped it into the water. I caught it before it went beneath a branch, already preparing a magnificent complaint, when I noticed resin-coated bark caught along the same bank. The water had carried that too.

I placed the new scrap against the others. Part of the damaged edge fitted. The cloth could have washed down from the northern hills. The animal guarding its young hadn't supplied an answer about Mama; it had supplied a warning I ought to have respected sooner.

I preserved the cloth with a hooked claw from the earlier fight and followed the stream north.

Smoke rose above the trees near a little hut. The old gray-furred fellow who lived there had a white mustache, a walking stick, and absolutely no interest in making my investigation convenient.

I showed him the fragments.

His fingers stopped over three uneven stitches. He looked at my face.

"What's your mama's name?"

"Yoo-LAY-lee."

He closed his eyes. "That stubborn girl."

Inside the hut, tools and cloth covered his workbench. He laid my pieces beside fabric with the same edging and repairs, then asked for my full name.

"Aloysius Devadander Abercrombie."

He stared at me, and slowly began to smile.

"Your mama's my daughter."

I looked from him to the cloth. He told me his name was William Devadander Abercrombie. Most folks called him Billy.

"My mama called me Billy."

"I know."

"But that's your name."

"Sure is."

She'd named me Aloysius and called me after her father. I sat there trying to fit this irritating little man into something Mama had given me before I could remember receiving it.

"Are you always this much of an asshole?"

"Lord, yes."

I smiled. "I believe we're going to get along magnificently."

William recognized the cloth because he'd seen Mama wearing it. The resin came from several groves in the highlands. That narrowed the search without telling us where the garment had torn.

We had scarcely begun getting acquainted when fire spread through the resin grove. William knew a route to water. A fallen tree blocked it, with its roots wedged against rock and smoke coming down behind us.

He told me to crawl through a gap and leave him to find another way. I caught the accessible trunk between my jaws, pulled until the wood split, and swallowed the section obstructing the path. The rest loosened enough to clear.

Grandfather stared into the opening.

"Your daddy's boy, all right."

I stopped smiling. Then we went on. At the stream, a burning branch came toward us on the current. I lifted him and jumped to the opposite bank. By the time he had finished ordering me to put him down, smoke was rising where his roof had been.

"I'm sorry."

"Built one before."

He watched the smoke a moment longer. "Can build another."

We traveled through the burned country afterward. Grandfather could put me on my backside with that walking stick without being anything like as strong as I was. Once, after I'd finally caught it, he simply let go. I was still pulling. My heel found a stone, and my face found a puddle.

"Want another lesson?"

"Fuck you, Grandpa."

"That's my boy."

Rain followed the fire. At a ravine, I had stepped onto a fallen tree despite Grandfather's warning about its burned roots. The bank gave way under my weight, and the trunk fell into the stream. Downstream it caught more debris and dammed the rising water. When the blockage shifted, the burned bank beneath us began coming apart.

Grandfather slipped. His cane broke against the stone, and he hung above the brown water by one hand.

I caught his wrist. A branch slammed across my back and drove us both toward the edge. I bit into the wood, tore away the section pinning me, and swallowed it. Grandfather pointed out a crack in solid rock. I caught it with my free hand and hauled us onto the upper shelf just before the bank dropped away.

His forearm was cut. My shoulder was bleeding. Under an overhang, he showed me how to hold the cloth while we bound his wound.

"How'd you know the crossing was going to collapse?"

"Didn't know for certain."

He'd known the ground was burned, rain was coming, and the bank was moving. I looked out at the flood. He looked at what remained of his cane.

"But dignity don't float worth a damn."

When the rain eased, we took a longer route to a ridge Mama had loved. An old tree overlooked the valley. She'd cut marks into the stone nearby. Grandfather had watched her come up there to enjoy the view and be somewhere nobody could tell her what to do.

Before we left, I found a branch beneath the tree. He showed me how to shape it into a new walking stick. I worked carefully, which took considerably longer than biting it.

He tried the finished grip.

"That'll do."

The next place he showed me was an old hunting basin. Mama had marked her routes on a dark stone formation beneath an overhang. Between us and that stone stood an enormous ridge-backed predator guarding a dead herbivore.

Grandfather pointed toward the upper trail. We could go around.

I looked at the predator and smiled.

"No," he said.

I stepped into the basin anyway.

The animal advanced when I approached its food and stopped when I backed away. I understood exactly where the boundary lay. Then I crossed it again.

Its jaws drove me into the gravel. I caught a leg, lost my hold to a sweep of its tail, and got up with blood running down my side. When it charged, I took its upper jaw in both hands. My heels plowed the ground until it twisted free and slammed me into a standing stone.

Behind me, Grandfather shouted about the rock. I thought he meant the one at my back. I moved aside, caught the next lunge, and turned the animal's head into the dark formation we'd come to see.

The impact cracked it. I bit the predator's neck and began swallowing. It dragged me toward the river, its feet tearing through gravel. I held on. Its tail struck the weakened stone, and a broad slab broke away and slid into the flooded channel.

I finished the intake before I looked up.

Grandfather was staring at the ruin.

Three uneven cuts remained on the broken face. Most of Mama's marks had gone into the water with the slab. I asked whether we could recover them.

"Maybe some."

I started toward the channel.

"But not all."

The creature was still struggling inside me. I stood between the broken stone and my grandfather and explained that it had been dangerous, that it had attacked, that I'd handled it.

"You did," he said. "And the stone's still broken."

"Well, it's only a rock."

He looked at the remaining marks.

"To you."

He walked up the trail. I stayed. I could carry broken stone in my stomach, but I couldn't put back grooves I'd never seen. When I found him later, I apologized. He was still angry. I admitted I'd chosen the fight because I wanted to find out whether I could take the animal.

"You could," he said.

I kept a tooth knocked loose during the fight, separated from the meal. The stone stayed broken.

# A Gentleman's Final Fitting

The black hide was what caught my attention. Then the blood.

I'd been traveling near Mama's old valley after Grandfather William returned toward his own country. Something enormous was feeding in a clearing below me: a tyrannosaur-shaped brute with heavy folds of leathery skin and a face cut by three old scars.

He tore at the carcass. A fresh wound beneath his jaw opened, and green blood ran down his neck.

I crouched behind the vegetation. Those scars began above his eye, crossed his cheek, and vanished beneath the corner of his mouth. I remembered three grooves outside our cavern, black tissue caught against a broken rock, and green in the cracks.

The wind shifted. He lifted his head and looked straight at me. I hadn't identified Mama's killer simply by finding something ugly. But I had enough reason to stop looking elsewhere.

I stepped out.

"Good afternoon, sir."

His jaws opened. I pointed toward the scars.

"I remember finding something that looked remarkably similar outside my mother's home."

He charged. I jumped clear, and his teeth went through the fallen tree behind me. One foot crushed the place where I'd landed a moment earlier. I scrambled toward exposed rock, trying to get room without giving him a straight run.

When his head came down, I struck beneath his jaw and bit into the folds of his throat. The taste was bitter. He swung me into a tree, broke my grip, and caught my shoulder. Teeth went deep. He lifted me and threw me through rotten timber onto the slope.

For a moment, I could only watch him approach. My arm hung wrong. Every breath moved something broken inside my chest.

"Well, sir. You're considerably less charming up close."

I went beneath the next bite and attacked the softer tissue behind his leg joint. He turned, and I tore sideways. Green blood hit the rock. The leg buckled before he caught his weight on it again.

I moved toward that side. He had to load the damaged joint to follow me. When it faltered, I struck across the old scars. He caught my forearm. This time I pulled toward him, closing the space his neck needed to swing, and bit into his face.

He backed away with one cheek torn open. I let go and dropped, watching the injured leg. The beast put his shoulder against a weakened tree and drove it over toward me.

I jumped from the falling trunk. He came through the branches behind it.

His jaws closed around my torso. My ribs folded inward. He drove me into stone, shook me, and tightened when I tried to pull his lower jaw away. I couldn't wrench myself out. I could reach the flesh hanging beneath his throat.

I bit there until his grip loosened, dropped against his injured leg, and tore through the joint I'd already opened.

The whole black body came down. I caught his head before he could raise it, pulled his throat across my mouth, and drove through hide and muscle. His tail hammered the ground. I held until the kicks weakened and his chest stopped moving. Then I held longer.

I stepped back. No breath. No attempt to rise.

I was hungry, broken in several places, and furious that he couldn't die twice.

"Well. That was disappointing."

I took black hide, a tooth, and green blood to William, preserving them apart from anything I intended to digest. Grandfather listened while I described the scars and the clearing. Then I laid out the old tissue from our cavern with the fresh sample.

His fingers stopped moving.

"He'd been trouble long before your mama had you."

They'd fought over territory and hunting ground. Mama had stood against him. William knew the face, though he wouldn't pretend to know when every scar had been made.

"I can't tell you what happened every minute of that night, Billy. But I knew your mama's enemy. And the creature you killed was him."

"He killed her?"

Grandfather nodded. "Everything you've brought me points to him."

"Good."

He looked up.

"Means I killed the right one."

I returned to the body. Scavengers had begun on the softer wounds. They objected when I approached, then reconsidered after I opened my mouth.

Removing usable hide took work. I tore too carefully for it to be quick, separating a substantial black section without ruining it. I cleaned and preserved it. The rest of that enormous dead bastard went through my jaws into digestion.

Years later, when I'd learned enough about preparing leather, the hide became my hat. I maintained it. Repaired it. Carried it through changes in the world its former owner would have found deeply inconvenient.

I touched the brim beside our campfire. Arthur followed the movement with his eyes.

"Not every old possession has an agreeable history, Mr. Morgan."

"Brian, I'm telling you, that's not my fucking name!"

Kaze looked up and grinned. I recognized the other voice before its owner reached the firelight.

"Tell them what you did."

"Brusta Rhymes."

"You really just got rid of me."

"Brian. My mother's name was Yoo-LAY-lee. And I was telling my friends something rather important about her."

He started to answer. I lowered my head.

"Do not interrupt me."

"One body, gentlemen. Two minds. One mouthful. I wouldn't even have to separate you."

"Billy. Respectfully, we didn't know you were talking about your moms. And I ain't here looking for a fight. But I ain't letting somebody eat me over Brian running his mouth, either."

"A reasonable position."

"Don't point that fucking thing at anybody."

"Charles wants to say something."

"Hey, Brian."

"Who the fuck is that?"

"Just a fella listening to you make a whole lotta noise."

"Every time somebody asks you a little question, you give 'em a big answer nobody asked for," Charles said.

"Life advice? Boy, who said I was giving you advice? I'm just sitting here listening to you make a damn fool outta yourself."

"But you don't get to tell me I'm finished."

"Now, gentlemen."

"Eventually I encountered something I couldn't settle by biting it."

Kaze followed my gaze. "The asteroid?"

I adjusted my grip on the banjo.

"Mr. Morgan, you have absolutely no idea how badly that afternoon went."

# The King of Monsters

I didn't know what an asteroid was. I knew something was coming out of the sky, getting bigger, and showing no respect whatsoever for the afternoon.

Animals were running beneath it. A predator struck a tree, recovered, and kept going without investigating anything it might have eaten. That ought to have settled the question for me.

Instead, I took Mama's banjo up the coastal ridge.

The approaching object became a wall of light. Heat rolled over the exposed stone. I planted my feet, raised the instrument, and challenged it.

Arthur took the cigar from his mouth.

"You challenged a rock?"

"Yes, sir."

Kaze grinned. "Bro really squared up with an extinction event."

"Young man, I didn't know it was an extinction event."

The great catastrophe was roughly sixty-six million years ago. Knowing its date now has done remarkably little to improve my performance in it.

I ran along the ridge and leaped toward the descending brilliance. Mama's banjo was between my body and the falling mass when we met. My arms folded back. The instrument struck against me. Heat, pressure, and broken earth took away everything I could distinguish.

The asteroid continued down.

It struck the coast. The ground I had jumped from came apart. I was carried through debris too violently to count the blows. I kept hold of the banjo until I couldn't remember holding anything.

Days later, I began digging myself out.

Stone pressed against my back. My mouth was full of grit. One arm moved, painfully, then the other. I dragged myself through ash into a daylight so dim that I first looked up to find out whether it was evening.

The sun was a dull patch behind smoke and dust. The forest had been flattened. The shoreline was unrecognizable. I climbed over broken timber and called into a ravine. Nothing answered.

There were bodies along a ruined watercourse. I'd hunted their kind. They lay together under the ash, and there wasn't a thing my mouth could do about it.

I sat down because standing hurt.

"Well, fuck."

Then I noticed my hands were empty.

I searched the rubble. Turned over branches, moved loose stone, and got angrier every time I found something that wasn't Mama's instrument. At last I saw a little wooden rim beneath a slab. I got my fingers under the rock and lifted it away.

The banjo was intact. Straight neck. Whole strings. No burn, crack, or splinter anywhere I could find.

I turned it over again.

"Well, Mama. You certainly knew how to pick a sturdy piece of wood."

I sat with it against my chest. I didn't play it. As my body repaired enough for travel, I carried it away from the ruined coast with the strings untouched.

The catastrophe hadn't killed every living thing. In one burrow I caught a frightened animal and saw smaller ones behind it. I put it down. An hour later I found a larger meal, which restored some balance to a deeply disagreeable period of my life.

I traveled. In time there were green shoots in the ash, insects, forests again. I won't pretend to date every stretch of that wandering. What I remember precisely is that I hadn't played one note since waking when something enormous began moving beneath the sea.

Armored spines broke the surface. Water poured from a dark head and thick shoulders as the animal rose in the shallows. When he stood, he was nearly four hundred feet tall. I was ten feet and six inches, a perfectly respectable size until that moment.

Godzilla looked down at me and gave a warning rumble.

I introduced myself. He advanced.

His foot broke the shelf where I'd been standing. I jumped along the beach. His tail demolished a line of rocks behind him, sending water against the cliffs. I secured Mama's banjo against my back and ran at his leg.

My teeth penetrated the armor near his ankle just enough to get his attention. A hand came down. I moved before the claws reached the rock, then saw the tail coming too late.

It drove me through coastal stone, trees, and a hillside.

I pushed the rubble off myself. One arm had acquired a deeply offensive bend. I spat out a tooth and felt its replacement begin coming through.

"That was unnecessarily enthusiastic."

Christopher laughed through my banjo. "Of course you fucking went back."

I did. The animal knew how to use his weight. I approached low from the side, struck the same leg, and climbed where the skin gave me purchase. He slammed against the cliff. I jumped clear before a great section of it fell across the beach.

Then the plates along his back began glowing blue.

The light traveled forward. His mouth opened. The beam struck the ground between us and swept toward me through exploding stone.

I pulled Mama's banjo from my back and raised it.

The atomic breath drove me backward. My feet cut trenches through rock. Heat poured around the instrument and tore up the ground behind me. I leaned into it with both arms while a ridge of broken stone gathered at my heels.

The banjo held.

When the pressure stopped, I nearly fell forward. I caught myself and examined the wood. Still whole. The strings were waiting exactly where Mama had put them.

Across the ruined shore, Godzilla's chest expanded. The light in his spines faded.

I decided to sing him something insulting.

That was the entire plan, gentlemen. I hadn't worked out a weapon or discovered a charge. I settled my fingers and plucked the first note I'd played since the asteroid.

I never reached a word of the song.

The note expanded into a discharge that struck Godzilla square in the chest. The force left over from the asteroid and the beam I'd just intercepted went together in one catastrophic TWANG.

His body left the ground. His tail tore through the coastal forest. He hit the earth, dug a trench across it, and crashed through a ridge in a rising wall of stone. The land continued collapsing after he'd disappeared behind the dust.

I stood holding a banjo.

"What the fuck?"

Kaze laughed. I looked down at the same instrument across my knees.

"I was trying to play a song."

It was one extraordinary release. I've never reproduced that particular combination. I couldn't explain its workings then, and I wasn't about to learn them before the dust settled.

Godzilla got up.

He was badly hurt. His chest was torn, one side moved unevenly, and his body trembled under the effort of standing. His eyes found me. He lowered his head and charged.

I secured the banjo and ran toward him. His jaws swept over me as I changed direction. Then one vast foot rose directly above my head.

I braced and caught it.

The ground collapsed beneath my legs. My arms shook. I was resisting one descending foot while his other leg supported him; the distinction mattered considerably to the stone giving way around me.

I pushed at an angle. His injured body couldn't correct cleanly. The planted leg slid on broken rock, his weight shifted, and the pressure above me changed enough to let me jump free.

I climbed the damaged side of his leg and reached his back. One dorsal spine had fractured near its base in the earlier impact. I braced my feet against him and pulled at the broken edge.

He twisted. I held. A jagged piece came away in my hands.

I swallowed the fragment into preservation, separate from digestion, before his next movement could throw it from me.

Then I dropped near his shoulder and went for his head.

He turned with his jaws open. I caught the side of his face and opened mine. His head entered. His claws drove into the ground. The tail struck the hillside while I held the intake and pulled.

Head, neck, shoulders. He fought every part of it. He was weakened, not willing. I stayed with him until the torso followed, then the legs and the immense tail.

I closed my mouth on the ruined coast with the whole animal alive inside my original stomach.

Christopher spoke after a moment.

"Did he fit?"

"Christopher. I just told you he did."

Inside me, he pushed, twisted, and struck against his confinement. I increased the acidity to its maximum and began traveling away from the wreckage.

His resistance was tremendous on the first day. By the second, it came less often. On the third I felt another series of heavy movements and a concentrated pressure I couldn't identify. Whatever he was attempting, it didn't get him out.

By the fourth day his movements had weakened considerably. There was little resistance left on the sixth. On the seventh, the last activity stopped. I waited and felt the remaining identifiable tissue break down.

By the end of that seventh day, Godzilla was dead and completely digested. The dorsal fragment I'd preserved separately was all I had kept.

"Did you get his powers?" Christopher asked.

"No."

"So you just ate him?" Daniel said.

"Yes."

Kaze began laughing. Christopher had moved on to the more important matter.

"You said you broke off one of his back spikes."

"Indeed."

"I wanna see it."

I told him to clear the space beside his workshop. Footsteps came through the banjo, followed by a scraping sound and something metallic being moved.

"All right."

I released the actual fragment from my old preserving hoard and directed it along the controlled internal route into the cleared space. It had traveled inside me all those years. Now Christopher had it before him, away from the acid and the penitentiary.

"Billy. Is this the actual fucking thing?"

"The very same."

For a little while, I heard him examining it and reporting what he'd found: the jagged break, its size, the surface beneath his hand. I could follow his reaction through the channel without guessing what was in his head.

"And I've decided you may have it."

He stopped talking.

"For real?"

"Yes, Christopher."

"Thanks, Billy."

"You're welcome."

I told him I didn't know what properties the fragment retained beyond being part of the animal. He agreed to examine it before embarking on anything foolish. The agreement was real; my confidence in its longevity was limited.

Kaze looked at me. "So that's why you hate Godzilla?"

I considered the matter. "Have you seen how much attention that reptile receives?"

Movies. Posters. Merchandise. I'd been roaring, damaging property, and behaving offensively long before anyone offered him a film career. Catfish Billy, directed by Michael Bay, would have been a magnificent production.

"You never auditioned for shit, did you?" Christopher asked.

"I refuse to entertain these allegations."

Arthur laughed. I rested Mama's instrument across my knees, careful with the strings out of old habit.

"A fine opponent, gentlemen. Terrible manners."

# Old Friends, Bad Appetites

By the time I met Manny, Diego, Sid, and that squirrel, the world had acquired an astonishing quantity of ice.

I won't take y'all through the business of getting acquainted. Manny was a mammoth who answered a threat of being eaten with "Good for you." Diego was a saber-toothed hunter whose patience I admired. Sid could ask whether I remembered dirt being invented and expect an answer.

Scrat had an acorn.

I took it from him on a frozen crossing. He bit my ankle. Between my weight, the disturbance, and water already weakening the shelf, the ice broke. Manny caught Sid with his trunk and went through it himself.

I returned the acorn to Scrat on the bank.

Diego had arrived looking at a stranded mammoth as prospective dinner. I intercepted him, then decided I wanted Manny alive. We got a fallen tree against the remaining ice; Diego braced it while Manny used his tusk and trunk for leverage. I pulled him onto the bank.

While we were occupied with Manny, Scrat had resumed trying to bury the acorn. Afterward I saw him retrieve it from the river and risk drowning for it again. We traveled south, and I came to enjoy the company. That's enough about how it began. What I did afterward is the part I owe y'all.

We were overlooking a frozen lake when Sid stopped in the middle of a sentence.

A vibration moved through the ground. He stiffened, dropped to his knees, and stared at the ice. Then he looked around as though he'd never seen us.

"Wait. This isn't where I was."

Manny called his name. Sid asked who we were, blinked, and suddenly identified us all. When Manny questioned him, Sid couldn't explain asking.

I watched him over the following days. He'd abandon an intention halfway through it, then recover it. Sometimes he sounded like himself. Sometimes his attention seemed to belong to somebody else.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Sid."

"Are you certain?"

"Billy, are you feeling okay?"

I couldn't tell him what was happening. Sitting there at the fire, I finally put the two names together.

"Zion and Sid."

"You're telling it wrong."

The voice came from beside the fireplace.

My hand stopped on the banjo. Kaze turned so sharply his foot dragged through the dirt.

There hadn't been anybody in that seat.

Now a yordle sat there with Sid's long face and loose mouth, looking at us with Zion's suspicion. His shoulders sagged as though keeping himself upright had already become somebody else's responsibility.

Zion and Sid. One person.

Zid.

Kaze looked from him to me, then back at him.

"Where the fuck did you come from?"

"i dont know. i forgot"

Nobody answered him.

Zid glanced behind his seat.

"Somebody moved this."

"You just got here," I said.

"Doesn't mean they didn't move it."

"Hold the fuck on," Christopher said through my banjo. "Zion?"

"Zid."

He said it easily. The rest of us were still looking at him.

Then his eyes traveled around the fire.

"Am I back, or am I sitting here getting interrogated?"

"You want back in the Seven Sins?" I asked.

"Yeah. That's what I said."

"You absolutely did not," Christopher said. "But I'm good with it."

Kaze let out a short laugh. "Fuck it. Welcome back."

The agreement went around the fire. Each of us gave him an answer I gave him mine last.

"Then you're back."

Zid immediately made room for the dog. Whatever question he'd expected next seemed to leave his face.

"Good. Keep going."

I looked down at the banjo. I'd been explaining an absence. Now I had another Sin beside my fire telling me to hurry up.

I went back to the ice.

The cold grew worse. Herds moved south. Manny began protecting a group of mammoths traveling out of their usual grazing country. One evening I found an adult female separated from the others near a rocky pass. There was enough meat on her to reward the trouble of a hunt.

I circled downwind. Manny appeared on the ridge.

"Leave her alone."

"Why?"

"She's with us."

He came down between us. The female backed toward the trees. There were other meals I could have pursued. Manny knew that as well as I did.

"Then you know how to find another meal."

"An excellent point. Unfortunately, I don't wish to."

He planted his feet.

"Move," I said.

"No."

I went low under a sweep of his tusk. He turned faster than I expected and knocked me across the frozen ground with his shoulder. When I got up, he was still between me and the animal.

"Get out of here, Billy."

I charged.

His tusks caught my side and drove me into the trees. I seized one, but he twisted his head and dragged me. His foot came down beside me as I rolled. I jumped at his shoulder, caught the fur, and bit beneath his neck.

Manny crushed me against the rock face. I fell beneath his chest. He stepped back, and I followed with my mouth opening.

He saw it.

"Billy. Don't."

I could have stopped. I lunged instead.

His tusks struck me. His legs tore up the snow as he tried to pull away. I held the intake until the great body passed through my jaws. Then I closed my mouth.

The female escaped. I didn't follow her. I stood looking at the place where Manny had been, still angry, with precisely what I'd chosen inside me.

Arthur watched the fire.

"That's a rotten thing to do to somebody you called a friend."

"Yes."

He looked up. I had no better answer for him.

Several days later, Diego found me beside a frozen stream in a narrow pass. I smelled him coming and recognized the movement over the stone. He stopped several yards away.

"Where's Manny?"

I sat up. "I swallowed him."

Diego looked away, then back.

"Why?"

"He stood between me and something I wanted."

"That's it?"

"That was sufficient."

He told me Manny had trusted us when he had every reason not to. I observed that trust was a dangerous arrangement.

"No. You made it dangerous."

I stood. "Have you come here to fight me?"

"I came to find out whether Sid's next."

"Sid is not my enemy."

"Neither was Manny."

His claws opened in the snow. He intended to keep me away from Sid. This time, he attacked first.

Diego went for my flank and released before I could catch him. He slipped behind me, struck again, and used the rocks to make me turn. When I reached, he was already out of range. His fangs caught the back of my leg.

"Diego, you're fighting beautifully!"

"Stop enjoying this!"

I caught his shoulder on the next attack. A saber tooth went through my forearm; his claws opened my face. He kicked free and backed into the narrower pass, watching my jaws.

I followed until I saw the loose stone overhead. He knocked a supporting slab aside. Boulders came down. One caught my shoulder, another drove me against the cliff, and he charged through the dust toward my throat.

I caught his head. His teeth sank into my palm. I pulled him close enough that his speed no longer gave him room.

"Sid better never see you again."

"An excellent warning."

I swallowed him. He fought until the intake was complete, claws striking my chest, body twisting against my hold. When the dust settled, I stood alone in the pass with my injuries repairing.

I can tell y'all what I took into my stomach. I haven't given you a further account of Manny's or Diego's fate, and I won't manufacture one to make this easier to finish.

Sid found me at dusk. His fur was wet with snow. He asked about Diego, then Manny. I nodded.

"Why?"

"Because I wanted something more than I wanted to remain his friend."

He stepped back.

"That's terrible."

"Yes."

"No, Billy. That's really terrible."

He told me we could have talked. Then the strange distraction crossed his expression again. He pressed a hand against his head and fought to hold on to what he'd come to say.

"I don't know what's happening to me. Half the time I can't remember what I was about to say. Sometimes things feel like they're not even my thoughts. But I remember Manny. And I remember Diego. And I remember what you did."

He didn't want to travel with me anymore.

"Very well."

He watched my hands and my mouth. I let him stand there until he believed me.

"You're free to leave."

Sid turned and walked into the trees. I didn't follow. I never swallowed him, then or later. Neither he nor Zion ever entered my stomach.

I encountered Scrat months afterward, farther south where the weather had eased. A stone hit the back of my head. I turned. Another struck between my eyes.

He was on a branch with his acorn, grinning at me.

I caught the next stone and threw it against his perch. The branch broke. He caught another, swung, lost the acorn, caught that with his tail, and somehow regained his footing. Then he threw the nut at my face.

I caught it.

He screamed, jumped for it, and climbed me when I held it out of reach. He bit my nose and pulled my barbels. I took him by the tail and held the acorn in my other hand.

We'd done versions of this before. He'd even tried stealing Mama's banjo while I slept, only to hit himself in the face with it. This time I became bored with the game.

I put the acorn in my mouth and swallowed it.

Scrat watched the lump move down my throat. His eyes widened. I opened my empty mouth, and the little bastard launched himself after it.

I closed my jaws and swallowed him too.

He resisted once the movement began. It carried him into my original stomach, where the acorn had already gone.

Christopher asked what happened to the nut.

"Christopher, that is not the most important question."

"It is to Scrat!"

"An excellent point."

I had no further ending for the squirrel or his property. Kaze shook his head.

"Certainly. Didn't feel like it."

The fire cracked between us. I turned the banjo a little on my lap. Those creatures had possessed wills of their own. Manny had refused me, Diego had acted on his refusal, and Sid had walked away. I remembered each of them quite clearly without making my part in it any prettier.

After them came more years than any of us needed described individually. Human beings built cities and kingdoms. They developed entire professions around persuading one another of things I found questionable.

One of those disagreements involved Jesus.

Christopher stopped whatever he was doing inside me.

"What the fuck did you do to Jesus?"

I looked around the fire.

"Well, Christopher."

# The Sky Don't Give a Fuck

"Christopher, put the tools down."

A clatter came through my banjo.

"Why?"

"Because I'd prefer you hear this without converting your workshop into a crime scene."

Kaze glanced at the instrument. "He's definitely still working."

"Of course he is."

Before the religious disagreement, there was somebody I wanted them to know properly. I'd mentioned Stephen Hawking, but I hadn't told them how a man in a wheelchair came bouncing over a root in front of me in an ancient forest.

The flash vanished behind him. His overturned chair lay beside a fallen log. A device announced a destination error.

"This is not Oxford."

"No, sir."

He'd entered the wrong sequence into his time equipment. I helped him right the chair, then walked around it while he examined the controls. Two primary wheels, a frame, a mechanical voice. A magnificent contraption.

"You ever teach that thing for sweet jumps?"

He asked me to explain. I pointed toward a hill and described a ramp. He considered the proposal.

"How high?"

Stephen calculated. I handled the timber. When we had a clear approach and landing, he secured himself and told me to push.

The wheels rattled over the ground. I gave him a final shove up the ramp, and Stephen Hawking took three actual feet of air over a prehistoric clearing.

He landed on the timber hard enough to bend a support. I hurried forward and asked whether he was hurt.

His machine answered, "Again."

I had to sit down to finish laughing.

We spent a few extraordinary days together. He took observations; I supplied transportation. Once he insisted on studying a predator closely enough that I ended the experiment by swallowing its subject when it charged. He objected to my scientific method.

Then the battery began failing. Stephen said his temporal equipment needed eighty-eight miles per hour to activate the return under those circumstances. I examined the wheels and chose an open stretch of ground. He prepared the machine.

I pushed until the frame shook and his voice counted eighty-five. Three more, and the air opened. The chair vanished in a flash.

I stopped on empty ground. I hadn't seen his landing and couldn't claim to have watched him arrive at the laboratory. I knew the friend who'd asked for another jump. When I saw him again in Noxus, I recognized him.

Papa Franku and Pink Guy introduced me to something less useful than a wheelchair: Kancho. I encountered them arguing beside a muddy path, and their demonstration gave me an unfortunate new interest. I practiced the timing and ambush for sixty-seven years, among other pursuits. Christopher requested that I refrain from demonstrating it beside the fire.

History also supplied Diogenes, who enjoyed making important men uncomfortable; Cleopatra, who knew how to make people surrender authority; and Leonardo, who wanted more access to my anatomy than I cared to grant. Newton and I disagreed over the educational value of an asteroid. Napoleon's people tried explaining their ownership of Louisiana. Jean Lafitte appreciated concealed goods, Twain appreciated fools, and Houdini took an alarming interest in my stomach.

Louis Armstrong played beautifully enough to make me stop talking.

"Now that's impressive," Kaze said.

I smiled and laid a hand on Mama's instrument. Then I gave Christopher the account he'd demanded.

"Gentlemen, there was a time, near two thousand years ago, when I encountered a religious impersonator of such commitment that the poor son of a bitch had himself nailed to his own display. And don't y'all interrupt me before I've established the particulars."

I found the man on a hillside, nailed to a wooden crucifix with thorns pressed around his head. I waited for the crowd to leave. When he heard my footsteps and looked up, he appeared to have expected somebody considerably smaller.

"Jesus Christ!"

I stopped beneath him.

"Hold yourself right there, my dear friend. What was that you just said?"

"Jesus Christ!"

"I heard you fine the first time. It's the implications that concern me."

"I am Jesus."

"You're what?"

"I am Jesus, the son of Mary."

"Well, congratulations. Most creatures I know have somebody for a mother."

"The Virgin Mary."

"The what?"

"The Virgin Mary."

"You're telling me your mama's a virgin? And that she gave birth to you? Now how in the sam hell does that work?"

"It was a miracle. Through God, all things are possible."

"Well, ain't that a mighty convenient way to settle a disagreement."

He hadn't asked for money, which left me wondering how far we were from the invoice. I examined the nails, the wood, the crown, and the extraordinary commitment to his story. Then I began laughing.

"Now, I ain't the smartest bastard in the swamp just yet, mind you, but I sure as hell wasn't mulch-bagged yesterday, my dear friend. And I'd thank you kindly not to confuse one with the other."

The man stared.

"Your attempts were juvenile at best. Juvenile. And that's me trying to be nice about it."

He tried to speak. I raised a hand.

"See, I've been alive long enough in this game we play that I can confidently distinguish the ones who witness, imitate, and practice—that's you—from the ones who wrote and pioneered the unwritten books before books were ever written."

I leaned closer.

"And it's that very same game you're attempting to play on me, son. I've smelled that bait coming downriver long before you ever thought to cast it."

Now, I express this recollection with names and history I learned afterward. I wasn't acquiring knowledge of other people's lives by looking at him. The opinion was mine, and I was delighted with it.

"In other words, you can't bullshit the bullshitter."

I raised a finger.

"You can't judge Judas without justice—unless it's just a paradoxical position, Jesus. Catfish Billy's always been one to justify juxtaposition, merely to adapt and adjust."

Then I spread my arms.

"And you absolutely cannot catfish the catfish whom they call Billy."

"It's the truth," he said. "My mother was a virgin."

"Now, the birds and the bees need their honey and their seeds to breed what's outer and beneath. And I reckon you're quite the joke, my dear friend, if you expect me to believe."

He insisted it was a miracle. Every hole I found in the account, he filled with God. I commended the costume and opened my jaws.

"Wait—please!"

"Now don't take it personal, my dear friend. There's no shame in having your deception uncovered by a more experienced practitioner. Besides, I'm getting a little hungry."

I swallowed him, crucifix, nails, crown, and all. Then I walked away convinced I'd exposed a fraud. What ultimately became of Jesus is another question. I knew what I'd taken into myself; I had no authority to settle everything else about his existence.

Kaze stared at me across the fire.

"Nigga. You saying you ATE Jesus Christ?"

"An alleged Jesus, son. Do try to keep up."

Arthur removed his cigar. I observed that he was making it sound unreasonable.

"Because it was unreasonable."

Christopher's voice came through the banjo.

"Wait—crucifix? Crown of thorns? BILLY. Did you digest them?"

I gave a filthy smoker's cough. Then I opened a section of my old preserving hoard and selected two objects. The crucifix and the crown traveled through my controlled internal route into Christopher's accessible living space. The nails stayed in my custody.

He went quiet. I heard him move closer, and then his report came through the channel.

"Oh my god, sweet baby Judas. THIS IS THE REAL THING. Jesus was a BIG DEAL, Billy!"

"Well, he certainly believed so."

He wanted to know how long I'd had them and how many compartments I hadn't shown him. I observed that I was entitled to some privacy.

"Privacy? I live inside your fucking stomach!"

Kaze looked from the banjo to me.

"You STILL think Jesus was fake?"

"Kaze, the gentleman announced his own divinity and immediately proceeded to explain away his mother's virginity by invoking supernatural authority. I submitted an extremely thorough criticism."

Arthur shook his head. Christopher asked what I intended to do with the relics.

"You may have them. The crucifix and the crown. Consider them a gift."

He asked whether the nails went with them.

"Not part of the gift."

"Why not?"

"Because, Christopher, I have already been quite generous."

I could hear him examining his two new possessions. When he invoked Jesus's name again, I sighed.

"Now don't you start taking his name in vain too, Christopher. That's how the whole goddamn problem began."

"Billy, you're the motherfucker who ate him!"

I let the ensuing disagreement expire without assistance. There was somebody more important to get to.

The first time I met Christopher was in New Orleans during Mardi Gras in the early two-thousands. Music poured into streets crowded with people making questionable decisions. I was enjoying myself, having made an exceptionally bad one involving ten strips or more of LSD. I later learned he'd taken ten on his first trip. Neither of us was in a condition to improve the other's judgment.

He stood staring at me for about five minutes.

Finally he pointed.

"Your hat! That's some sick fucking shit!"

He asked whether the leather was real and what it came from.

"An old enemy."

He laughed, then showed me a fish tattoo. I could recognize the image but had little use for the human writing accompanying it. Christopher explained Phish, the band. Music gave us somewhere to begin.

Then he showed me another tattoo. I saw a handle and rounded shape and thought of an instrument.

"Is that a banjo?"

He looked at his own arm.

"A spoon!"

I asked what the hell a spoon was. He stared, reached into his back pocket, and produced his own peanut-butter-eating utensil. There was still some on it.

He demonstrated the purpose. I suggested the arrangement involved unnecessary equipment. He laughed and held it out.

"You can keep that one, buddy."

I accepted it and put it in my marsupial pouch.

"Thank you, Christopher."

I still have that same spoon. He eventually made me wash it.

Near a food establishment, we argued about cooking. Christopher knew preparation, temperature, texture, and how to organize ingredients before beginning. Mise en place. He took it seriously. I wanted to know why he needed so many steps when he could eat the ingredients together, and he explained, at some length, that I had no idea what I was talking about.

We argued for hours. I liked his work before I had any reason to depend on it. We laughed about the hat, the tattoos, music, and the spoon.

Then our bodies changed.

The intoxication didn't account for what happened. It was an exceptionally rare physical anomaly in our world. One moment we were separate people standing in the festivities. The next I couldn't see where Christopher had gone, and his voice came from inside me.

"Billy? What the fuck?"

I looked down.

"Christopher?"

"Why the fuck am I inside you?"

I was occupied with the same question.

He demanded to come out. We had to learn the arrangement: one exterior at a time, the other of us fully sized inside, both of us still ourselves. Exterior control could change. Our thoughts stayed private. Intake and release remained mine to manage.

The workshop, monitors, residential spaces, and prison weren't waiting for him on that street. They came later, through changes and considerable work, much of it Christopher's. He could cook, build, shoot, and find uses for equipment other people might have preferred to keep.

"That last part sounds like Chris," Kaze said.

"Because I'm fucking right!"

"She left me alive."
